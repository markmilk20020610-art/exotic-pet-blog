White's Tree Frog Oedema: Reading Fluid Retention Before the Lymph Hearts Fail

A frog that looks pleasingly plump is often a frog that is not moving fluid. The difference between a good body condition score and oedema is where the swelling sits.

An adult Ranoidea caerulea has a resting blood pressure low enough that venous return depends on the lymph hearts — four pairs of small muscular pumps under the skin of the back and flanks, each beating ten to thirty times a minute independent of the cardiac rhythm. When those pumps lose their rhythm, fluid pools in the subcutaneous spaces fastest where gravity has the least to say, and the animal you find on the cage floor at 78 °F with a belly like a partly inflated balloon is usually several weeks into a problem that started in the drainage layer.

The night I got the water right and the frog still filled up

I found a subadult male in his water bowl at ten past eleven on a Sunday, floating nose-down with his forelimbs held off the bowl floor. I had been misting him at 70 % relative humidity against a glass lid and emptying the drainage layer whenever I thought of it. What I had not done was test the LECA reservoir for ammonia, and when I eventually dipped it the reading was off my chart — the cheap dip strip went straight to its darkest colour. He was hydrating into a sour sponge. He survived; the bowl went onto a daily dump-and-refill schedule and the LECA layer to a fortnightly full rinse. The point is not that I was cruel. The point is that oedema in this species is usually a plumbing problem wearing a disease costume.

What the lymph hearts actually do, and what happens when they stall

An amphibian has no closed pressure-driven venous system like a mammal's. Blood and lymph move in large part because muscles squeeze it, and the lymph hearts are a set of dedicated pumps that return lymph from the tissue spaces to the circulation. In White's tree frogs the anterior pairs sit behind the jaw and the posterior pairs near the hips, which is why early swelling so often shows as a puffy throat and fat-looking flanks before the whole animal boings up.

Three things interfere with those pumps. The first is electrolyte imbalance — a frog sitting on a substrate bathed in ammonia, nitrite or nitrate loses osmotic control at the skin, and the tissue fluid shifts before any organ fails. The second is infection: bacterial septicaemia, and in this species in particular the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, can produce the generalised fluid retention keepers call dropsy. The third is renal and hepatic failure, which is often the endpoint rather than the start.

What the keeper can measure is upstream of all three. Ammonia should read zero ppm, nitrite zero ppm, and nitrate under 20 ppm in any water the frog sits in or drinks through its skin. This species does not need a swimming pool, but a water bowl deep enough to cover the vent should be changed daily, and drainage layers should be flushed on a fixed schedule, not a remembered one.

The enclosure as a small water-treatment plant

White's tree frogs are arboreal: in the wild they spend their nights metres up in eucalypts and their days wedged into bark crevices where the air runs cooler and wetter than the canopy. What that means for captivity is an enclosure that is warm and dry at the top and humid near the floor, with no standing water that has been there long enough to foul. I run a 45 × 45 × 60 cm tall enclosure for a single adult, with a 30 °C basking spot under a low-output heat source and a cool end that settles to 24 °C overnight. Humidity sits at 55-70 %, measured with two probes rather than a decorative dial — one at the top, one just above the drainage layer, because the difference between those two readings is where most oedema cases are hiding.

The drainage is the part people skip. LECA clay pebbles under a false floor, or a drilled bottom with a reservoir, gives you a place for the water to go that is not the frog's skin. Without it, waterlogs substrate rot, then ammonia climbs, then the frog absorbs the wrong chemistry through its belly.

Substrate is personal. I use no loose coir for this species now — it holds water too long and I could not keep nitrate readings honest — and run instead a simple drainage layer, a sheet of fine mesh, and a thin layer of leaf litter over a couple of fake plants. Bioactive substrates work if the clean-up crew is thick enough to process waste faster than it accumulates, but that is a different article. For the frog with a swollen body, the first move is always to strip the enclosure back to something you can drain and sterilise.

Reading the swelling: what to measure, what to change

Before any vet visit, do the three measurements. Weigh the frog on a kitchen scale to the nearest gram, record the humidity at both probe heights, and test the water bowl and drainage layer for ammonia, nitrite and nitrate. Write the numbers down with the date. A one-gram gain overnight in a 60 g subadult after a heavy misting is normal hydration. A three-gram gain over ten days with no change in food intake is not.

The clinical picture splits roughly three ways, and where the swelling lives tells you which:

What you see Most likely cause What to check first What to change
Puffy throat behind the jaw, animal still feeding Early lymph-heart insufficiency from electrolyte stress Ammonia and nitrite in the water bowl; drainage layer rinse date Daily bowl changes, flush drainage, hold humidity at 55-65 % for a fortnight
Swollen flanks and thighs, sitting on the floor, not feeding Bacterial septicaemia or chytrid Body weight trend; skin for grey patches; any recent new animal Vet now — swab for chytrid, culture if possible; quarantine anything new for 60 days
Whole body bloated, eyes sunken, no urine Renal failure or hepatic failure 24-hour weight change; skin colour; whether the frog has urinated Vet now; survival is poor and honest discussion of euthanasia is fair

The one thing no keeper can fix at home is a fluid-loaded frog with sunken eyes. That animal needs a vet who knows amphibians, and the treatment — usually a diuretic alongside antibiotics and supportive fluids — is not something you improvise in a tupperware.

Stages, and why the juvenile is the one to watch

A froglet of 4-5 cm snout-to-vent loses water faster than an adult of 8-10 cm because its surface-to-volume ratio is larger, and its tolerance for a rise in ammonia is lower. That is why juvenile oedema is usually water quality, and adult oedema is more often infection or organ failure. Keep juveniles at 60-70 % humidity with a nightly mist and a bowl they can walk out of, and do not — I will say this plainly — put them in a sealed plastic tub with wet paper towel and call it an enclosure. Subadults between about 5 and 7 cm eat heavily and foul their bowl quickly; that is the age at which the daily bowl change matters most. Adults are drought-tolerant for a day or two and will not die of a missed mist, but they will die of a fortnight of stale water.

Morphs matter less than people think. Blue and snowflake lines show oedema against pale skin faster than the standard green, so an experienced keeper will see swelling in a blue frog a week before it would show on a green one. That is the only husbandry difference and it is a small one.

The quiet recommendation

The cheapest fix when you first suspect you are losing the water argument is a second hygrometer, so you can see the gap between the top of the enclosure and the wet end under the drainage floor.

Frequently asked questions

Can I treat a bloated White's tree frog by soaking it in clean water?

No. A freshwater soak does not draw fluid out of an amphibian, because their skin is already permeable and they will absorb more. What you can do at home is correct the environment — daily water changes, drainage flush, humidity held at 55-65 % — and get the animal to a vet within 48 hours. Soaking is not a treatment.

How much weight gain overnight is normal after misting?

In a healthy 60 g frog, one to two grams gained and lost over a wet-dry cycle is ordinary hydration. A persistent gain of three grams or more across ten days, with no matching food intake, points to fluid retention rather than fat. Weigh at the same time each evening, after the frog has emptied its bladder.

Does oedema always mean the frog is going to die?

No, but the odds depend on which cause you are looking at. Water-quality oedema caught early and corrected usually resolves within ten days. Septicaemia and renal failure carry a much poorer outlook, and a vet will often want to test for chytrid first. Survival past three weeks without relapse is a reasonable marker of success.
