Madagascar Hissing Cockroach Moult Failure: The Humidity Window That Decides It

A nymph that cannot get out of its own exoskeleton is not unlucky. It dried out at the wrong moment, and the window is narrower than most care sheets admit.

A Madagascar hissing cockroach that fails its moult is usually not a sick animal. It is a dry one. The old cuticle splits along the dorsal midline, and the new one underneath has to slide free at a moment when the air around it is doing a specific job — the new shell is soft, and it hardens by cross-linking proteins and chitin once it is exposed to air, which means the animal needs a stretch of elevated humidity before it commits to the split, not after. I keep the moulting band at 75-85 % relative humidity, with the everyday range running 60-70 % for the colony at large, and I have never once regretted the extra few percent in the week before a subadult looks swollen and waxy.

The night I cost a roach its right middle leg

It was a November, around half ten at night, and I was doing the fortnightly substrate change on the display colony. Six adults, eleven nymphs, the usual. I had pulled the coco fibre and put in a fresh block that I had rehydrated that morning — and I had rehydrated it thin, because I was in a hurry and the block had been sitting open since August. I remember the weight of the bin: lighter than it should have been. I put the animals back and went to bed. Five days later a subadult male was hanging half out of his exuvia like a poorly fitted coat, right middle leg still trapped in the old limb case, abdomen wrinkled. He was alive. I got him out with a damp cotton bud over about forty minutes, and the leg came away. He lived another eighteen months with five and a half legs, and I still think about that bin.

What ecdysis actually asks of the enclosure

In Gromphadorhina portentosa, the process runs in two phases you can set an instrument to. First there is apolysis — the old cuticle separates from the epidermis underneath, and the animal lays down a new, folded, soft cuticle in the space. During this phase water is drawn back out of the old cuticle and into the body, which is why a roach in late premoult looks tight, slightly dull, and often stops eating for two or three days. Then comes ecdysis itself: the old shell splits, the animal pulls out, and swallows air and haemolymph into the abdomen to expand the new body to its post-moult size.

Here is the mechanism that catches keepers. The new cuticle hardens by sclerotisation — a tanning process that cross-links the proteins in the cuticle — and water loss during that period is what deformities come from. If ambient humidity is low, the new shell partially dries before the animal has fully inflated and hardened in its final shape, and the wing cases or the leg joints set wrong. If the old cuticle itself has dried to a crisp before the split, the split doesn't run cleanly and the animal gets stuck inside it. Both failure modes trace back to the same thing: hours of RH under 55-60 % at the wrong moment. The old cuticle drying before the split is the one I see most often, and it is entirely a keeper problem.

Two practical consequences fall out of that. First, a moulting roach needs access to standing water and damp substrate at the moment it chooses — not when you next open the lid. Second, the whole colony needs a stable background humidity, because you cannot predict which animal will moult on which night. You can see a swollen abdomen coming, but you cannot see a nymph's apolysis. I treat the whole bin as the moult chamber.

Setting the humidity, and how to actually measure it

I run substrate depth at 4-6 cm of coco fibre, moistened so that a fistful clumps when squeezed but no water runs out — roughly 60-70 % moisture by weight if you want the number. The lid is vented at about 30-40 % of surface area, no more. The colony sits at 24-28 °C, which is the band where they feed well and grow at a rate that doesn't leave them stalled in premoult for weeks.

The problem with all of this is measurement. A hygrometer pinned to the lid of a plastic bin tells you about the air at the lid. The roaches sit on the substrate, and the substrate surface in a vented tub can read 40-45 % while the lid reads 70 %. I run two probes — one at lid height, one lying on the substrate surface — and I trust the lower one for moult decisions. This is the single upgrade that changed the way I keep them:

Those numbers will vary by bloodline and by feeding — a colony raised warm and fed on apple and dog biscuit grows faster than one on kitchen scraps alone — and there is no published growth curve for the species I would stake anything on. Treat the interval column as a schedule to expect, not a rule to enforce. The adult stage is where people get it wrong in the opposite direction: adult G. portentosa do not moult, so a sudden "moult" in a fully-winged adult is not a moult at all. It is usually a female producing an ootheca or a male whose wing cases have been chewed by tankmates. Check both before you treat for anything.

When you find a roach stuck mid-ecdysis

You will find them in one of three states. The old cuticle split but the animal hasn't pulled free. The animal has pulled free of the body but one or more legs are caught in the exuvia. The animal has emerged but the wing cases or abdomen are visibly deformed. Your response differs by state, and the clock matters — a roach that has been stuck for more than about six to eight hours has usually lost the race on sclerotisation.

  1. Do not pull. The new cuticle is soft and will tear. I have seen a keeper pull a nymph clean in half this way, and the rear half kept moving for twenty minutes.
  2. Move it to a small, high-humidity tub. A 1-2 litre container with 3 cm of damp (not soaked) coco fibre, vented, held at 26-28 °C, is enough. Dampen the exuvia directly with a fine spray of room-temperature water; the old case softens and the animal's own movements often free it within an hour.
  3. If the limb is still caught after an hour, dampen again and leave it overnight. Do not attempt to help. If the leg is genuinely necrotic and constricting the body, a cotton bud and steady gentle pressure at the joint can free it — but if it comes away, it comes away.
  4. Isolate for two to three days after a hard moult. The new cuticle is not sclerotised for roughly 48-72 hours, and other roaches will chew a soft sibling. This is not aggression; it is opportunism.
  5. Feed nothing but moisture for 48 hours. A freshly moulted roach cannot process hard food well, and the priority is hydration, not calories. A slice of apple or a damp cotton pad is enough.

If a roach misses a moult, do not immediately blame humidity and stop there. Run the differential. Substrate that has dried out but still reads high at the lid. Temperature too low, so the animal never reaches the ecdysis trigger. A colony crowded enough that a moulting nymph is being walked over by six adults. And dehydration in a roach that simply has no water dish — they do drink, and a shallow lid with a cotton pad is the cheapest fix I know.

The bioactive half, and where it breaks

Springtails do a lot of work in a hissing cockroach bin: they clean the frass and keep the substrate from souring, which matters because wet coco fibre without a clean-up crew goes anaerobic and smells like a pond. But springtail populations crash when the substrate dries, and in a moult-focused enclosure you are deliberately keeping it damp. That balance is tight. In my own bins I run springtails and a thin layer of leaf litter, and I accept that I will re-seed the springtails every six to nine months. I no longer use a drainage layer in the roach bins — the species doesn't need it and it just makes the bin heavier — but I drain any standing water from the bottom every fortnight. If a bin starts to smell sour, the substrate is wrong, and no clean-up crew fixes that.

I also keep the sexes apart deliberately. Adult males will jaw each other over territory, and while the bouts are mostly hissing contests with body shoves, a male in premoult can be chewed. Sex them at subadult — the male's pronotum horns are visible at around 35-40 mm — and keep the ratios in the reference notes, not in your head.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know a hissing cockroach is about to moult?

Two to three days before ecdysis the abdomen looks tight and slightly waxy, appetite drops, and the animal often sits still in one corner rather than ranging. The wing buds on a late-instar nymph visibly swell. None of that is reliable at hatchling size — under 10 mm you simply cannot see it coming, which is why the humidity has to run right all the time.

Is a stuck moult always a humidity problem?

No, but humidity is the first thing to check and the easiest to correct. Low temperature will stall the process, dehydration will stall it, and a roach moulting in a crowded bin can get mechanical damage from tankmates during the split. Rule out moisture at substrate level, then temperature, then crowding, in that order.

Can a roach survive losing a leg in a bad moult?

Yes, and they usually do. A nymph will regenerate a partial limb at the next moult if the stump is not infected, and an adult has no further moults and will live out its life with fewer legs without apparent distress. The risk is infection at the wound, not the loss itself. Keep it clean and dry after the moult hardens.
