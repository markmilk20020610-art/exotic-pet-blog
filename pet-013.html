Axolotl Water Chemistry: The Nitrogen Cycle That Kills Them Quietly

Ammonia, then nitrite, then nitrate — the three numbers that decide whether an axolotl thrives or quietly burns.

Ammonia at 0 ppm, nitrite at 0 ppm, nitrate under 20 ppm — those three readings are the whole welfare budget of an axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum), and the gap between them and what most new tanks actually measure is where the animals die. The species is neotenic: it keeps its larval gills, and those gills are a gas-exchange membrane a couple of cells thick, directly exposed to whatever is dissolved in the water. Ammonia at 1 ppm burns that membrane before a cheap strip test tells you anything happened.

The evening I read the wrong bottle

April, a two-year-old leucistic male, 23 cm nose to tail, in a 90 × 45 × 40 cm tank. I cycled it five weeks on a bottle of one-shot bacteria and tested on the sixth. Zero ammonia, zero nitrite, nitrate 5 ppm. He went in on a Sunday. By Wednesday night he was floating at the surface with his gills curled forward like a fist, one filament pale and frayed at the tip. Ammonia 0.25, nitrite 0. I told myself that was a rounding error, did a 30 % change and went to bed. Thursday morning nitrite was 0.5 ppm and I understood: the bottle had cycled the tank to ammonia oxidation and no further, and my own water change had stripped what little biofilm there was. That cost me £140 in vet time, methylene blue, and eight days of 50 % changes. He is still here. The gill tip never fully grew back.

What the nitrogen cycle is doing, molecule by molecule

An axolotl is ammonotelic — it dumps nitrogen as ammonia straight through gills and skin, because it does not need to conserve water the way a lizard does. A colony of Nitrosomonas-type bacteria in the filter oxidises ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺) to nitrite (NO₂⁻). A second group, Nitrobacter and Nitrospira, oxidises nitrite to nitrate (NO₃⁻). Nitrate is the relatively benign end product, removed by water changes.

Two populations, two growth rates, and the second is slower. That lag is why a new tank shows a nitrite spike two to three weeks after ammonia first reads zero. The tank is not cycled on the day it hits zero-zero — it is cycled when nitrate is rising and both read zero for a week of normal feeding.

Temperature sits behind all of it. Nitrifiers in a 26 °C tropical tank are far more active than the ones in an axolotl tank held at 16-18 °C. Cold water protects the animal and slows its filter. You cannot warm the tank to speed the cycle — 20 °C is the ceiling before the animal is stressed — so you run a long cold cycle, or you cycle the filter in a warm bucket and move the media across.

Setting the parameters and holding them

  • Ammonia: 0 ppm. Above 0.25 ppm you are on daily changes. Unionised ammonia is the toxic fraction and it rises with pH, so in a cold tank at pH 7.4-7.6, settle for nothing above zero.
  • Nitrite: 0 ppm. Nitrite binds haemoglobin into methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen. A brown tinge to the gills is the late sign, not the early one.
  • Nitrate: under 20 ppm, ideally under 10. Not acutely toxic, but a signal your water-change schedule is behind.
  • pH: 7.4-7.6. Stable beats perfect. A pH falling across the week means the filter is losing the fight with ammonia.
  • Temperature: 16-18 °C, ceiling 20 °C. Do not let a summer room push it higher.
  • KH around 3-5 dKH to buffer against the acid nitrification produces.

Then the decision that matters: what to do when either reading is off zero. A large, temperature-matched water change, and a daily one until both read zero. Chemical binders convert ammonia into a form the kit may still read and do not remove the nitrogen from the system. Water removes it. Under time pressure that is the only tool that works.

Life stages, and one table to run the whole thing

Juveniles are the hardest on a filter and the least forgiving. A 10 cm animal in a small tank produces enough ammonia to crash a filter within days, because it grows fast, eats daily, and small volumes have no dilution buffer. Most breeders I know skip the small tank and raise juveniles in a large well-filtered volume with daily siphoning. Subadults, roughly 15-20 cm, settle into a rhythm. Adults over 20 cm are the most robust and the heaviest waste producers, and need a genuinely mature filter.

Gills are your free continuous test. Curled forward, flattened against the head, floating at the surface, gulping air, or refusing food all mean check the water before you check anything else. An axolotl in stress posture is not subtle once you have seen it.

Running the tank as a small system

Filtration is where beginners lose the plot. These are messy animals with a high waste output and a low tolerance for current. A large sponge filter on a decent air pump gives you huge bacterial surface area, gentle flow, and no impeller for a curious salamander to shred a gill on. I run one plus an air stone in every tank, and I keep a piece of bio-media in a mesh bag I can move to a hospital tank in a hurry. A cycled piece of media is worth more than any bottle.

Substrate affects the cycle through surface area. Bare-bottom is easier to siphon and completely legitimate for quarantine; sand gives more bacterial surface and looks better. Not gravel — axolotls ingest it and impaction is a surgical problem. Plants take up some nitrate and give cover, but don't expect them to remove meaningful nitrogen from a tank with an adult in it. They are a nice-to-have, not a filter.

Use a liquid reagent kit for ammonia, nitrite and nitrate. Strips are a rough second opinion, and everyone I know who crashed a tank on them has the same story: "it always read fine." If your kit says zero and the gills say otherwise, do a change and test again with a second kit. Quarantine any new animal in a separate cycled bare-bottom tank for at least four weeks, watching for fungus — in cold water it spreads fast between animals, and cottony white tufts mean isolate immediately.

Cycling from scratch, properly

  1. Fix temperature first. 16-18 °C before you start, by room or chiller, not a heater. You need to learn the cold-tank timeline.
  2. Dose ammonia deliberately to 2-4 ppm. A dead prawn or a pinch of food works but is imprecise; I no longer use it, because a liquid source lets you read the number.
  3. Wait for ammonia to fall to zero. That is the first colony up. This is the moment most people add the animal. Don't.
  4. Watch for the nitrite spike. It may climb to 1-5 ppm. Normal. Keep a small ammonia dose going to feed the first colony.
  5. Wait for nitrite to fall to zero and nitrate to rise. Nitrate rising is the handshake — both colonies are working. Do a large change to drop nitrate, then the filter is ready.
  6. Stock slowly. One animal, normal feeding, daily tests for a week. Then trust it, and test weekly for the life of the tank.

In a cold axolotl tank expect four to six weeks, sometimes longer in winter. Do not treat bottle bacteria as a substitute for reading a test, and don't assume a cycling product formulated for tropical water will work at 16 °C.

Frequently asked questions

My tank has run a week and reads zero ammonia — is it cycled?

Only if ammonia was dosed to a readable level, fell to zero, and nitrate is rising. A tank that has never had a deliberate ammonia source and reads zero-zero-zero is not cycled — it is empty. Dose ammonia to 2-4 ppm and see whether it clears within 24 hours. If it doesn't, and nitrite appears, you are mid-cycle.

Can I use a product that "detoxifies" ammonia instead of a water change?

No. Binders change ammonia to a form that may still register on some kits, and they do not remove the nitrogen from the tank. A temperature-matched water change takes out ammonia, nitrite and nitrate in one operation. Under a spike, do 30-50 % changes daily until both ammonia and nitrite read zero.

Do I need to test every day once the tank is cycled?

Not for an adult — weekly is enough once you have a stable zero-zero and rising nitrate. Test more often after a filter clean, a power cut, a new animal or a change in feeding, because those are the events that knock a filter back. For juveniles, test every couple of days for the first months.
