Axolotl Water Chemistry: The Nitrogen Cycle That Kills Them Quietly

Ammonia, then nitrite, then nitrate — the three numbers that decide whether an axolotl thrives or quietly burns.

Ammonia in an axolotl tank should read 0 mg/L and nitrite 0 mg/L, and the moment either climbs off zero you have a countdown running, not a warning light. Axolotls are Ambystoma mexicanum, a fully aquatic salamander with a permeable skin that does gas exchange and ion regulation directly with the water — there is no scaled barrier between the chemistry and the bloodstream. At 0.5 mg/L free ammonia I have watched gill filaments go from feathery to clumped and pale within a day, and that's before the animal looks sick. So the water is the husbandry.

Nineteen litres, three fish, and the night I learned about nitrite

November 2019, a Friday. I had a juvenile leucistic axolotl in a 19-litre plastic storage tub on the bench — a quarantine tub, I told myself, temporary, while the main tank cycled. Temporary lasted eleven days. I fed two small earthworms on the Thursday, didn't siphon, topped the tub up from the tap on Friday morning without dechlorinating first because I was late for work. By 9 p.m. the animal was floating tail-up at the surface, gills curled forward into a tight C, skin slightly red at the throat. The test strip read somewhere north of 3 mg/L ammonia. I did a 50 % water change with conditioned water, then another four hours later, and sat up with a torch until 2 a.m. watching it right itself. It lived. The tub cost £4. The lesson — that "temporary" is what kills more axolotls than anything except heat — cost me a night's sleep and very nearly the animal.

The nitrogen cycle, translated for a tank with no filter bacteria yet

Fish and salamander waste, uneaten food and decaying plant matter all break down into ammonia (NH₃ plus its ionised form NH₄⁺, together measured as "total ammonia"). In water at axolotl temperatures — which you want between 15 °C and 20 °C (59–68 °F) — a meaningful fraction of that is the un-ionised NH₃ form, which is the one that crosses gill membranes. Hydrogen ions in the water start to displace ammonium, but the point is: it is the free ammonia that burns tissue.

Two groups of bacteria fix this. Nitrosomonas-type organisms oxidise ammonia into nitrite (NO₂⁻); Nitrobacter-type organisms oxidise that nitrite into nitrate (NO₃⁻), which is much less toxic and which you remove with water changes. The trap is the middle step. Nitrite spikes after ammonia has started to fall, and nitrite binds haemoglobin in the blood to form methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen — the animal suffocates chemically while sitting in clear water that smells of nothing. That's why a tank reading 0 ammonia can still kill a salamander, and why "the cycle has started" is the most dangerous thing a new keeper can tell themselves.

Nitrifying bacteria double slowly at low temperatures. At 18 °C you are looking at a cycle that takes four to eight weeks in my logbooks, sometimes longer in winter. Do not try to rush it with a heater — the same warmth that speeds bacteria also speeds ammonia toxicity and physiologically stresses the axolotl. Cycle in a separate bucket if you must cycle fast; keep the animal's water cool.

What to test, how often, and when to do a water change

Test liquid reagent kits, not strips. Strips are fine for a rough sanity check on pH but their ammonia and nitrite readings drift badly at the low end, which is exactly the end you care about. Here is the schedule I run and the numbers I act on:

  1. Daily, for the first six weeks of a new tank: ammonia and nitrite. Any reading above 0.25 mg/L triggers an immediate 50 % water change with dechlorinated water at the same temperature.
  2. Weekly, once cycled: ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH. Ammonia and nitrite must read 0 mg/L; nitrate should sit under 20 mg/L, and I change water at 30.
  3. Water change volume: 25 % weekly as routine, 50 % if nitrate is climbing faster than that. Two smaller changes beat one big one — sudden shifts in hardness and pH are their own stressor.
  4. pH: hold 6.8–7.6. Axolotls tolerate the range, but the bacterial filter is happier above 6.5; a pH crash below 6 stalls nitrification and ammonia accumulates silently.
  5. Hardness: 7–15 dGH and 7–16 dKH in my notes. Soft, low-alkalinity water is prone to pH crashes, so if your tap water is very soft, add a carbonate buffer or accept more frequent testing.

Here is the trap most people hit around week three: you test, ammonia reads zero, nitrite reads somewhere near 2 mg/L, and you conclude the tank is fine because the first number is clean. It isn't. Nitrite is the number to watch during cycling. If it is red on the chart, the animal comes out.

Temperature, oxygenation, and the failure modes nobody warns you about

The single most common husbandry mistake with this species has nothing to do with nitrite: it is keeping the tank at tropical-fish temperature. Axolotls are highland animals — the lakes they evolved in sit in the mid-teens Celsius year-round. The band I aim for is 15–20 °C (59–68 °F), never sustained above 22 °C (72 °F), and above 24 °C (75 °F) I treat it as an emergency. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen and exposes the animal to more free ammonia at the same total reading. Chillers exist; fans over the surface and frozen bottles do not, in a heatwave.

Oxygenation is tied to the filter outlet more than to the air pump. An airstone in a 45 cm cube provides surface agitation, which matters, but a small internal filter with the outlet near the surface giving a gentle ripple is better. Axolotls dislike current — they are lake-bottom animals — so baffle the flow, and if you see the gills being pushed sideways by the current, it is too strong.

Behaviour worth reading in the water: floating is normal once every few days when the animal gulps air, but persistent floating tail-up is almost always gas or an ammonia insult. Gills curling forward into a C shape, surface gulping, and loss of the feathery filaments are the three early signs I check for. A healthy animal has long, dark, fringed gills and a slightly plump abdomen; a sick one has short, pale, stubby gills and a hollow belly almost before any other sign.

On morphs, one husbandry change matters: the albino and leucistic lines can show less obvious colour-based stress signals, and the melanistic wild-types are easier to read for ammonia reddening at the throat. Otherwise colour doesn't change how you keep them.

Life stages and the tank they need

One of the real errors I see is putting a juvenile in a full-size adult tank and never being able to test and correct the water quickly enough. Small animals in large volumes make a cycle slow to catch a spike and slow to correct. I start juveniles in a 40-litre bare-bottom setup with daily testing, then move them out as they grow.

Two adult axolotls in a 110-litre tank is the tight end of workable if you are rigorous about water changes; three is not. Goldfish-rule politics aside, the floor area matters more than litres for a bottom-dweller, and axolotls have been known to bite each other's legs and gill stalks when crowded — a slow-healing injury in cold water.

Substrate is where I changed my mind. I kept adults on fine sand for years, but on a 6 mm grain I had an impaction scare in 2021, and I now run bare-bottom for juveniles, fine sand for adults only if it is the small, round, inert river type and only if I'm siphoning thoroughly. Gravel is out, full stop. A bare-bottom tank looks clinical but it is the one setup where I can actually test and see what's going on.

Frequently asked questions

What ammonia reading is dangerous for an axolotl?

Any free ammonia above roughly 0.25 mg/L on a liquid reagent test is a red flag, and I would do an immediate partial water change. Between 0.25 and 1 mg/L the animal will often show reddened skin or clamped gills within 24–48 hours. Above that, get the animal into clean, cool, dechlorinated water and re-test the source.

Can I use a fish-in cycle with an axolotl?

You can, but it is the riskiest way to cycle. If you must, test twice daily and change 25–50 % whenever ammonia reads above 0.25 mg/L. The safer route is a fishless cycle using a bottled ammonia source in a separate bucket, then moving the cycled filter media into the axolotl's tank before introducing the animal.

Why does my nitrate keep rising after ammonia and nitrite are zero?

Nitrate is the end product of nitrification and only leaves the tank by water change or through plant uptake. A rise of 20–40 mg/L per week in a well-stocked tank is normal. Change 25 % weekly to hold it under 20; if it climbs faster than that, your stocking or feeding is heavier than your water-change routine can carry.
