Humidity and Salt Water Pools for Land Hermit Crabs

Hermit crabs do not drown in water — they drown in air that is too dry. The two-pool system, the substrate depth, and the gauge that decides whether they survive a moult.

An adult Coenobita clypeatus carries a shell no more than a few centimetres across, and the air in the tank around it has to sit at 70-80 % relative humidity — the percentage of water vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature — or the crab stops being able to breathe properly. That's not a comfort issue. Land hermit crabs carry water in their shell and pass it over gill tissue inside the abdomen, so dry air drains the reservoir and the animal slowly suffocates while perfectly still looking healthy. The salt pool matters for the same reason: it is the refill station.

The keeper's diary

Six years ago I came home at 11:40 pm to a noise I couldn't place — a dry, clicking scrabble, like someone tapping a fingernail on glass. One of my crabs, the biggest, was out of its shell and walking. I'd left the lid propped for a feed and the hygrometer behind the log was reading 51 %. I panicked, dunked the crab in the freshwater dish and put it back — and that was the mistake. What I should have done is what I do now: a shallow, soft-bottomed container of the salt mix, the crab's own shell beside it, and dark. Naked crabs often die from the shock of handling, not from the dryness. I lost the animal three days later. The shell was empty and the abdomen was softer than it should have been. I still hear that noise.

Dialling in the humidity without guessing

The target is a stable 70-80 % relative humidity, dropping no lower than 70 %. One hygrometer is not enough because you don't know what the air is doing at the cool end, in the moss pit, or in the corner nearest the heat. Two probes is the honest minimum — one at substrate level on the warm side, one on the cool side — and both should be digital, since the dial types drift. Cross-check with your own hand-on-glass test: condensation on the walls after a misting should still be there in three hours.

Your levers, in order of how much they change the reading:

  1. Substrate depth and moisture. Mix coco fibre to a "sandcastle" dampness — squeezes together, doesn't drip. Too dry, the lower layer stops buffering. Too wet, you get standing water and a sour smell.
  2. Lid coverage. A solid lid over three-quarters of the mesh top will hold you near 75 % in most rooms. Vent the rest.
  3. Water surface area. Bigger pools mean more evaporation. Two pools, one fresh, one salt.

If you're fighting 55 % on day one, don't mist the whole tank — add a damp moss corner and re-measure after four hours. Misting the surface raises the reading for twenty minutes and then it falls, and you learn nothing.

The salt water problem

Salt pools are where I see the most improvisation and the most errors. A land hermit crab needs seawater mixed to roughly the specific gravity of natural seawater — around 1.023-1.025 — measured with a hydrometer, not by eye. Plain table salt will kill a crab; it is iodine, anti-caking agents and no trace minerals. Marine mix only.

  • Mix and test. Follow the tub, then check with the hydrometer. If it reads 1.020, add a little mix and re-test.
  • Depth. Shallow enough for the crab to climb out of — a body length of water, no more, with a rough stone or ramp in.
  • Change. Replace the salt pool fully every three to four days; it collects debris and evaporates, both of which shift the salinity upward.
  • Never top up a salt pool with fresh water — top up the freshwater, change the salt water.

Freshwater needs to be dechlorinated and changed daily. Both pools need a way out; I've pulled crabs out of bare-sided dishes that couldn't get a grip.

Temperature and the pool together

Hold the tank at 24-28 °C (75-82 °F) on the warm side; below about 22 °C the crab slows, eats less, and stress-moults. Humidity and temperature are read together because warm air holds more vapour — a 70 % reading at 20 °C and a 70 % reading at 27 °C are not the same amount of water in the air, so check both gauges in the same spot. If the tank is heating but drying, the pools are too small or the lid too open, not the moss.

One thing I can't give you a number for: whether your tap water's minerals are fine long-term. I use dechlorinated tap for fresh and mix for salt; others use RO. The evidence is thin. Watch for shell wear and lethargy, and act on what you see.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use sea salt from the kitchen instead of marine mix?

No. Edible sea salt is mostly sodium chloride with anti-caking agents and no magnesium, calcium or trace elements. A crab soaks to balance its internal salinity, and table or cooking salt forces the wrong direction. Use a marine aquarium mix, test it with a hydrometer, and aim for 1.023-1.025 specific gravity.

My hygrometer says 75 % but the tank feels dry. Which do I trust?

Trust the instrument after you've checked it. Cheap analogue dials commonly read 10 % high or low. Compare two digital probes side by side; if they disagree, replace them. Then look at your substrate and pools — the probes measure air, not the moisture your crab actually walks on.

How often should I change the freshwater?

Daily, or sooner if it looks cloudy or smells. Dechlorinate it and keep it shallow with a rough route out. Change the salt pool fully every three to four days, and top up only the freshwater between changes so your salinity stays where you set it.
