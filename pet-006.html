Humidity and Salt Water Pools for Land Hermit Crabs

Hermit crabs do not drown in water — they drown in air that is too dry. The two-pool system, the substrate depth, and the gauge that decides whether they survive a moult.

A land hermit crab (Coenobita clypeatus) needs air at 70-85 % relative humidity, and that number is the whole story. Below it, the gills dry and the crab survives on borrowed time that runs out in the moult — the one process it cannot abort halfway. The salt pool matters for the same reason: these crabs drink through their gills, and a pool of the wrong strength is poison delivered with every sip.

The keeper's diary

The crab I still think about came in on a Tuesday, into a tank I had set up four days before. My hygrometer read 55 % and I told myself it would settle. By Friday it said 48 %, and the crab had spent the day tucked under the water bowl. I lifted the shell: the abdomen was still plump, so I stopped worrying. Wrong. On Saturday I filled that bowl from the tap with a pinch of table salt, and the crab drank from it that evening and again Sunday. Monday morning it was in a death curl, legs limp, no smell yet. I have never ruled out the salt. I do know the tank was too dry and the pool was too weak, and a hygrometer and a hydrometer would have told me both, in numbers, two days earlier.

Getting the air right first

Seventy to eighty-five per cent is jungle air, not room air. A hygrometer with a probe is the only way to know: an analog gauge on the glass can read 15 points high because it measures the glass, not the air. Put the probe at substrate level and read it twice a day — morning is worst, because air dries overnight.

  1. Lay 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) of coco fibre or sand, dampened like a wrung-out sponge, never dripping. Depth is what stops humidity collapsing between mistings.
  2. Cover the lid. Mesh is a humidity drain; glass or foil with a gap for airflow holds the air you paid for.
  3. Mist the substrate and walls, not the crab. Two or three light mists a day is a working start; the gauge tells you when to stop.
  4. Refill the bowls daily to a marked line — evaporation is the cheapest humidity you can get.

When humidity sags, the crab stops eating, digs in, and tries to moult in dry substrate, where the moult stalls. That is the cost of a 55 % reading left alone.

Mixing and running the salt pool

Use a marine mix, never table salt and never aquarium tonic. Mix to a specific gravity of 1.022-1.025 — standard seawater — and check every batch with a hydrometer, because mix strength drifts with the jug. A 1-2 inch pool with a ramp or decor inside is the working shape: the crab must be able to climb out, because drowning in a bowl is more common than people expect.

Keep fresh water and salt water in separate bowls, both available at all times. Crabs regulate salt in their gills by moving between the two. Change the salt pool every two or three days, or sooner if it smells rank, and top up with mixed salt water, never fresh — evaporation takes water and leaves the salt behind.

What goes wrong, and what it looks like

A crab sitting under the water bowl for hours has a humidity problem before it has a sickness. One that soaks in the salt water all day is telling you the tank is too dry. One that will not eat, digs in and stays under is likely moulting: raise humidity, stop disturbing it, leave food out anyway. Cloudy, soupy pool water means a dead animal or rotting food inside — empty it, rinse it, reset.

Know when it is a vet problem. An animal that is limp, smells, and has dropped limbs is past a humidity tweak. I have not seen good data on how long a hermit crab survives a stalled moult in a dry tank, and I will not guess. A tank run at 75 % and 1.024 doesn't produce many of them.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use table salt for the pool?

No. Table salt is sodium chloride with additives and lacks the magnesium, calcium and potassium a marine mix carries. Mix a proper sea salt product to 1.022-1.025 on a hydrometer. In a pinch, offer no salt water at all and fix it the next day — a bad pool is worse than none.

My gauge says 80 % but the substrate is dry. What is happening?

The gauge reads the air, and air can be humid while the deep substrate is bone dry. Push a finger down 3 inches (about 8 cm) and feel it. If it is not damp like a wrung-out sponge, mist and mix the substrate itself rather than the walls. Wet surface over dry depth is where moults fail.

Do I need a heater as well as the humidity?

Usually, yes. Land hermit crabs are warm-climate animals, and 72-80 °F is hard to hold at room temperature. Fit a heat mat to the side or back, never under the tank — under-tank heat cooks the substrate and the crab in it. Check the thermometer at the warm end after a day, because mats overshoot.
