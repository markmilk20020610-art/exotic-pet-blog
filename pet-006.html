Humidity and Salt Water Pools for Land Hermit Crabs

Hermit crabs do not drown in water — they drown in air that is too dry. The two-pool system, the substrate depth, and the gauge that decides whether they survive a moult.

Salt water pools in a hermit crab enclosure run at 1.020-1.025 specific gravity, mixed from marine salt and read with a hydrometer, and that number is doing two jobs. It sets the drinking water close to what Coenobita clypeatus finds at the shoreline, and it stops the pools becoming a bacterial soup the crabs walk through and then carry into the shell. Bracket that with 70-80 % relative humidity and you have the two parameters that fail first when a crab is found standing still in the corner. The rest of this piece is about holding those two numbers steady.

The evening the hydrometer saved me a vet bill

Half past eleven on a Tuesday in February, and I was mixing salt water on the kitchen counter because the pool in the tank had gone cloudy the night before and I had topped it up with tap water like an idiot. The crab in question — a mature male, shell aperture about 5 cm — had been sitting half-submerged in the fresh water dish all day, which any keeper with a year of experience knows is a bad sign. I tested the old pool: 1.010. Too dilute. The hydrometer had been telling me this for a week and I had not been reading it because the meniscus sits at the wrong angle under the kitchen spotlights. Fresh batch at 1.023, pool drained and refilled, thermometer probe back into the substrate rather than left on the lid. He moved to the salt pool within twenty minutes. That evening cost me fifteen minutes. The fortnight after the one where I let it slide cost me more.

Getting humidity right without soaking the tank

Relative humidity is how much water vapour the air holds as a percentage of what it could hold at that temperature, so 80 % at 26 °C is not the same amount of water as 80 % at 20 °C. Land hermit crabs want 70-80 % most of the time, and the substrate carries them there. A coco fibre block rehydrated until it squeezes out a couple of drops but not a stream sits at roughly 60-70 % moisture by weight. Keep it there, cover three-quarters of the lid with glass or foil, and let the pool and substrate do the work. Spraying the lid alone does nothing when the room is 20 °C and the heating is off.

Read both probes at the same hour daily. More than about 15 points between them means the substrate is drying from the top down and needs rehydrating from the base. A substrate probe above 85 % for a week, with a damp-basement smell, is past the point where mould on shell interiors is a real risk — open more of the lid rather than spraying less.

The salt water pool, measured rather than guessed

The pool must be deep enough for the largest crab to submerge with the shell upright, and wide enough for two crabs to sit in it at once. Shallow bowls do not work; the crabs need the whole body under, because it is the exchange of water through the shell opening that matters for osmoregulation — the process by which a crab holds its internal salt balance against the surrounding water.

  1. Mix marine salt (not aquarium tonic salt, not table salt) to 1.020-1.025 specific gravity. A 5-litre batch takes roughly 165-190 g; use the hydrometer, not the spoon.
  2. Let the mix stand at least two hours before it goes in, so the reading is stable.
  3. Change the pool every 48-72 hours, or sooner if it clouds or smells.
  4. Refresh the fresh water daily. Both pools need a rough ramp — cork or rock — that does not let the crab drag wet substrate into the shell.

The failure mode is quiet. A crab that cannot get out of a smooth-sided pool will drown. A crab kept in 1.010 water for weeks cannot hold its haemolymph salts and stops moulting properly. Neither shows up dramatically. You see it in a crab that will not move from the corner, and you measure it with a hydrometer.

Juveniles lose water faster than adults because their surface-area-to-volume ratio is higher, so they need the top of the band. A 40-gallon tank with 12 cm of coco fibre holds humidity far more steadily than a 10-gallon with 6 cm, and the small tank is where new keepers get caught out. Starting with juveniles, oversize the enclosure and run two hydrometers, not one.

What the evidence does not settle

The humidity window is contested in hobby circles — some keepers argue 60-70 % is fine for adults, others that anything under 70 % risks gill damage. The working consensus among exotic-animal vets who see hermit crabs is 70-80 %, and I have no data to improve on that. What I will say is that the crabs tell you. A crab that sits in the fresh water pool for hours every day is telling you the air is too dry, whatever the hygrometer reads. Trust the animal, then check the instrument.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use aquarium salt instead of marine salt mix?

No. Marine mix carries the full mineral profile — magnesium, calcium, trace elements — that a land hermit crab needs to hold its internal salt balance. Aquarium tonic salt is mostly sodium chloride and will not keep a crab in condition over months. Use a marine mix, check it with a hydrometer, and mix fresh rather than topping up.

Why is my crab sitting in the fresh water dish all day?

Usually because the air is too dry, not because the crab is thirsty in the ordinary sense. Check the substrate probe first — below 65 % means rehydrate from the base and cover more of the lid. If humidity is in band, test the salt pool; a crab in dilute water often sits in fresh water trying to correct the imbalance.

How often should I change the salt water pool?

Every 48-72 hours, and immediately if it clouds or smells. A crab walking through soiled water carries bacteria into the shell aperture, and shell rot is slow to spot and hard to treat. Two small pools changed on alternate days beat one large pool changed weekly. Mix fresh each time.
