Humidity and Salt Water Pools for Land Hermit Crabs

Hermit crabs do not drown in water — they drown in air that is too dry. The two-pool system, the substrate depth, and the gauge that decides whether they survive a moult.

Land hermit crabs are sold in wire cages with a sponge and a shallow dish, which is roughly like keeping a cat in a shoebox with a saucer of milk. They are not desert animals. They are tropical coastal animals that live in humid air, drink fresh water, and require marine water to regulate their salt balance.

Get the air and the water wrong and the animal does not die immediately. It slowly fails to moult, loses limbs, and dies weeks later in a way that looks like bad luck.

Why humidity is the whole ballgame

Hermit crabs breathe through modified gills that must stay moist. In air below roughly 70% relative humidity, those gills dry and the animal suffocates slowly. This is why "misting occasionally" is not a strategy — you need a sealed, humid microclimate that holds its number overnight.

Target 70–80% RH, measured at substrate level with a digital gauge, not a dial stuck to the glass. Cover most of the lid with glass or foil to hold humidity, and leave a gap for airflow. A tank that fogs completely and smells stagnant needs more ventilation, not less misting.

Two pools, not one

This is the part that gets skipped. Land hermit crabs need both fresh water and marine salt water, in separate pools deep enough to fully submerge the shell.

  • Fresh pool: dechlorinated water. Tap water must be treated; chlorine and chloramine both harm them.
  • Salt pool: marine salt mixed to 1.023 specific gravity, measured with a hydrometer. Aquarium "tonic" salt is not the same thing and does not work.
  • Depth: deep enough that the crab can submerge completely, with an easy exit — a ramp of mesh, driftwood, or stacked stones.
  • Change: fresh daily, salt every two to three days.

A crab that cannot submerge cannot replenish the water held in its shell, and a crab that cannot climb out will drown. Both are design failures, not accidents.

A hermit crab carries its own water supply in its shell. Your job is to make sure it can refill it, in both salinities.

Substrate: deep enough to bury a crab

Fifteen centimetres minimum, and more for large specimens. A 5:1 mix of play sand and coco fibre, damp enough to hold a tunnel when you push a finger into it — that is the consistency that lets a crab dig a moulting chamber that does not collapse.

Moulting is when crabs die in captivity. They bury for weeks, shed their exoskeleton, and harden. Disturbing them mid-moult, or keeping them in shallow dry substrate where they cannot bury at all, is the most common cause of "it just died for no reason".

Temperature and the gradient

24–28 °C, with a warm end and a cooler end. An under-tank heater on a thermostat at one end, never a heat lamp blasting the whole tank. Above 30 °C and below 22 °C both suppress activity and feeding.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use table salt for the salt pool?

No. Table salt is sodium chloride with anti-caking agents; marine salt contains the full mineral profile the crab needs. Use a proper marine mix and measure it with a hydrometer.

My crab is buried and has not moved in two weeks. Is it dead?

Probably moulting. Do not dig it up. Keep humidity stable, keep the water fresh, and wait. Disturbing a moulting crab can kill it.

Do I need a lid?

Yes, with partial coverage. An open tank cannot hold 70–80% humidity; a sealed one goes stagnant. Cover most of the top and leave a vent gap.
