Mexican Redknee Tarantula: Reading Pre-Moult Before She Seals Herself In

A tarantula that stops eating for six weeks is not starving. She is rebuilding an exoskeleton, and the humidity in those weeks decides whether she comes out of it intact.

At 72-76 °F (22-24 °C) a Brachypelma hamorii abdomen turns from a dull, hair-worn brown to a lacquered near-black before she moults, and in that window a 25 % drop in body mass is normal, not a diagnosis. The problem is that keepers weigh the empty dish, not the spider. A healthy adult female can stop eating for six to twelve weeks as the old cuticle separates from the new one underneath, and if you panic-feed her through it you are pushing crickets at an animal whose mouthparts have already retired.

The one that refused for forty-one days

I logged a subadult female in a 30 × 30 × 30 cm terrarium from the 4th of February last year. She had eaten a 12 mm cricket every eleven days like clockwork, and then stopped. The abdomen was still plump, so I kept offering — a small cricket every Sunday, removed after an hour, and every Sunday she walked over it. By day forty-one the abdomen had dulled, the bald patch on the dorsal surface was almost a perfect oval, and she had begun excavating a corner. I had been holding the enclosure at a flat 80 °F (27 °C) because warmer supposedly means faster metabolism, which was wrong for that phase. I dropped it to 73 °F (23 °C), stopped feeding entirely, and she sealed in on day forty-nine. Twelve days later she emerged with a new abdomen, both fangs intact, and a shed skin I could still read the hairs on.

What the fasting is actually for

Ecdysis is a two-part event: apolysis, when the new exocuticle forms and the old one detaches, and ecdysis proper, when the spider extracts itself through a split in the carapace. The whole affair is driven by ecdysone, a moult-inducing hormone, and takes weeks. During apolysis the gut lining is replaced too, and the mouthparts effectively decouple from the old cuticle. A spider that eats in this window can rupture mid-moult or wedge itself with a partly digested meal still inside. The fasting is not pathology — it is a deliberate shutdown of the feeding post.

The other half is water. The new cuticle is soft and folded, and the spider pumps haemolymph — her blood — into it to expand each segment after the split. Low internal hydration stalls that expansion, and you get crimped segments or a small, wrinkled abdomen. Hydration in pre-moult matters more than any meal.

Reading the four signals before you commit

  1. Abdomen colour and texture. When the bald patch darkens to a shiny near-black, apolysis has begun. From that point, no food.
  2. Burrow or web-mat behaviour. B. hamorii often excavates and lines a corner with silk, then seals it. Once sealed, do not open the enclosure to check.
  3. Refusal pattern. One refusal means nothing; three consecutive refusals across four to six weeks with a plump abdomen means pre-moult.
  4. Posture. A pre-moult spider often rests with legs splayed flat on the substrate, not tucked. That is preparation for the flip onto her back, not illness.

Temperature is where I changed my own practice. I run a gradient from a small heat mat under one-third of the floor, on a thermostat, giving a warm corner near 78-82 °F (26-28 °C) and a cool corner at 72-74 °F (22-23 °C). In pre-moult I let the warm side drift down a few degrees, not to trick the spider but to slow desiccation of the old cuticle before the split. I hold 65-75 % relative humidity by damping one corner of the substrate, never the whole floor.

The humidity number that does the actual work

Ecdysis fails when the old cuticle dries and sticks to the new one, and the hardest places to separate are the leg joints, the chelicerae and the pedipalps. The old cuticle takes up water because the spider drinks and draws moisture into the gut, which then diffuses outward. That matters: a damp floor alone does not solve a moult. She also needs a water dish she can lower her body into.

I set humidity with two probes rather than one, because a single hygrometer in a 30 cm cube reads whatever microclimate it happens to sit in. Substrate is a coco fibre block, rehydrated to damp-but-not-dripping at one end and left dry at the other, so the spider chooses. I aim for 65-75 % in pre-moult and never let the humid corner reach condensation on the glass, which in a small box is a mould-promoting world.

They live months, not years, after maturity, and no husbandry changes that. That fasting is a different animal from a female's pre-moult, and I treat it as such.

The moult itself, and what to do — which is nothing

When she flips onto her back, the carapace pops at the front, the abdomen skin splits behind, and she works her legs out one pair at a time. This takes between twenty minutes and several hours, and the only correct keeper action is to stay out of the enclosure. I do not mist, I do not lift the lid to look, I do not move her for photographs.

Afterwards the new cuticle hardens over roughly five to seven days in adults. The fangs are soft — cherry-red at first, darkening to near-black — and prey offered too early can bite back through the new cuticle. I feed nothing for at least seven days post-moult, and no hard-shelled prey until the fangs have darkened. Water is available throughout.

If she has not completed ecdysis within twelve hours of flipping, or still has legs trapped in the old cuticle after twenty-four, that is a real emergency. An experienced keeper can place a damp paper towel against the stuck leg and support the spider as it works loose, but the success rate is mixed and veterinary support for a spider is realistically thin in most regions. I will not pretend otherwise: the evidence base here is weak, and what good keepers do is patience and a very damp towel.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a Mexican redknee go without eating before it is a problem?

An adult female in pre-moult can refuse food for 60-90 days with no welfare concern, provided she has water and her abdomen stays plump. A juvenile typically fasts 14-42 days. If she has lost more than 30 % of body mass, or refuses with a visibly shrivelled abdomen, that is no longer a normal pattern — check temperature and hydration first.

Should I keep offering food to see if she has changed her mind?

No. Once the abdomen has turned shiny and dark, food stops entirely. Repeated offerings stress the spider and risk a live cricket burrowing into a moulting animal. If you genuinely cannot tell whether apolysis has started, wait a full week between offerings and remove uneaten prey within a few hours.

What temperature and humidity do I need during pre-moult?

I run a gradient at 72-82 °F (22-28 °C) across the enclosure and let the warm side drift down slightly during pre-moult. Humidity sits around 65-75 % in the damp corner, with a shallow water dish she can lower her body into. The damp corner should never be soaked — condensation on the glass means you have overshot.
