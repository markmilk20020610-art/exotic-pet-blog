Mexican Redknee Tarantula: Reading Pre-Moult Before She Seals Herself In

A tarantula that stops eating for six weeks is not starving. She is rebuilding an exoskeleton, and the humidity in those weeks decides whether she comes out of it intact.

A fasting Brachypelma hamorii at 24-26 °C (75-79 °F) burns so little that a two-month refusal costs her almost nothing; the temperature is what buys her that time. Below about 18 °C (64 °F) digestion stalls and the abdomen shrinks faster, because she is spending reserves faster than she is replacing them. The refusal is usually the cheap part. What kills tarantulas in this window is what a keeper does to break it.

The spider that taught me to stop offering

She came off the heat mat on a Tuesday and I did not notice. A three-inch female, plump, glossy abdomen, last fed ten days before — she took a cricket from the tongs and then ignored the next four. I had a fresh tub of large crickets and a habit of killing them, so I kept dropping them in, one a night, scooping out the uneaten ones each morning. That went on for eleven days. Then she stopped leaving her half-log and her abdomen went from bronze to a duller shade of itself. I moved her to a quiet shelf at 24 °C, put a water dish under her, and left her alone. She webbed a thin curtain between the log and the glass, which I took at the time for aggression. It was a moult hammock. She flipped two weeks later and came out a size bigger. The eight dead crickets cost me about three pounds. The lesson cost more.

Reading the abdomen and the calendar together

The abdomen tells you how much reserve she carries. The calendar tells you nothing much, because this species is slow and irregular and a captive female at stable temperature may skip a moult entirely for a year. What you are looking for is the combination: a plump abdomen plus a refusal plus a change in behaviour. Three things to check, in order.

  • The abdomen. Before a moult the old exoskeleton darkens from the inside, and on a red knee that reads as the pale bronze patches going darker and less pink. A freshly moulted spider is brighter than you remember; a pre-moult spider is duller.
  • The bald spot. If she has kicked hairs from the top of the abdomen — common in this species, one of the more urticating-hair-happy Brachypelmas — the patch under the bald spot turns from pale tan to a dark, near-black shade in the days before ecdysis. That darkening is the new cuticle forming underneath.
  • The retreat. She stops sitting out. She webs the hide entrance shut, or builds a web mat on the substrate and sits on it. In my notes this shutdown runs five days to three weeks. Longer is possible in a slow female.

What she does not do is look thin. A red knee refusing food while the abdomen stays full is a pre-moult spider or a recently fed one. One that is refusing food and visibly shrinking is a different problem — too cold, too dry, or a male reaching the end of his road, which is not a moult at all.

The temperature, the humidity and the mistakes that matter

Set the enclosure so the warm end reads 26-28 °C (79-82 °F) and the cool end 21-23 °C (70-73 °F), a gradient across a 30 × 30 cm footprint or larger. Do not drop the whole box to room temperature to "calm her down"; a spider at 20 °C (68 °F) in pre-moult is digesting nothing, and if she moults cold the mechanics get harder. If you need to slow things, do it before the bald spot darkens, not after.

Humidity is where the hobby argues. You will see 60 %, 65 %, 70 % and "just mist the substrate" given with equal confidence, and the honest position is that the number matters less than the substrate holding moisture at depth. Run 60-70 % relative humidity measured with a probe at substrate level, not the stick-on dial at the top of the glass. Over-wet is its own hazard: standing water and saturated coco fibre grow mould, and mould on the underside of a freshly moulted exoskeleton is a real and ugly problem. I stop adding water the moment the substrate goes from damp to wet.

The mistakes I have made or watched other keepers make, all during a moult or the week before:

  1. Continuing to feed. A cricket left in with a moulting spider chews on her while she is soft. Remove anything you dropped in and did not see eaten within twenty-four hours.
  2. Molesting her during ecdysis. Once she is on her back, do not prod, move, mist or open the lid for anything short of a fire. It takes a few hours in a small spider, longer in a big female.
  3. Panicking at the wet, crumpled animal. A freshly moulted red knee looks like a drowned tarantula: legs folded, pale, soft, dark at the joints. She darkens over the next few days. Leave her.
  4. Feeding too soon. Her fangs are soft and the exoskeleton is hardening for several days. I wait until the fangs have darkened — usually five to ten days at 25 °C, longer in a large female — then offer one small live cricket.

Where keeping her becomes a system, not a spider in a box

A red knee does not need a bioactive setup the way a gecko does, but substrate depth is a system parameter. Give her 5-10 cm of coco fibre you can keep slightly damp at the bottom and dry on top. She will dig, the damp layer holds humidity down where she sits, and the dry surface stops the mould you would get in a permanently saturated box. Springtails work as a clean-up crew; isopods are a much bigger animal than a spiderling and will compete for food. I keep it simple.

Enclosure size follows life stage, and the numbers change more than the hobby admits:

  • Spiderling (under 2 cm leg span): a small deli cup, 2-3 cm of substrate, one hide, one shallow water dish. Ventilation matters more than volume — a spiderling that cannot find its cricket in an oversized box starves politely.
  • Juvenile (3-6 cm): a 15 × 15 cm clear tub or small glass cube. Two to three small crickets a week at 26 °C; a moult every month or two is normal and the appetite swing is sharper than in adults.
  • Adult female (14-15 cm leg span): 30 × 30 × 30 cm floor, substrate 5-10 cm, two hides — one dry, one slightly damp. One or two large crickets a fortnight, adjusted by abdomen size.

Drill ventilation low and high so air moves through; stagnant, damp, warm air is a mould factory. The cheapest fix is a second hygrometer, because one reading at the top of the glass tells you nothing about the damp layer at the bottom:

Mature males are a different animal. A male who matures, refuses food and wanders the glass in long loops is not in pre-moult — he is looking for a female, and he will not moult again. Do not treat his fast the way you treat hers. Offer water and keep him comfortable. For her, the fasting is a rehearsal. For him it is the last chapter.

The decision tree: to wait or to worry

I have watched too many keepers break a perfectly good tarantula fast by trying to fix it. Here is the sequence I actually run.

  1. Check the temperature. A digital probe, warm end 26-28 °C, cool end 21-23 °C. If the whole box is under 20 °C, fix that first. A spider too cold to eat is not in pre-moult.
  2. Look at the substrate under the hide. If it is bone-dry and she has not touched the water dish in days, add water to the back corner, not over the animal. Aim for 60-70 % humidity and moisture at depth.
  3. Look at the abdomen. Plump = wait. Visibly shrinking over weeks in a female that should have moulted = check husbandry before anything else, and no moult is coming for a shrinking male.
  4. Leave the hide in place. If she has webbed the entrance, that is the signal. Stop feeding. Leave her alone for two weeks, then check whether there is an exuvia in the enclosure — the moulted skin. When you find it, leave it until she has hardened.
  5. Wait for dark fangs, then feed one small cricket. One. If she refuses and the abdomen is still plump, she is not ready. Wait another week.

If she has refused food for three months, the abdomen is sunken and curled, and the temperature and humidity are correct, that is outside my experience and I have no good answer. That is a vet conversation or a loss. It is not a moult. Say so, rather than forcing one more cricket into a box in hope of a miracle.

Frequently asked questions

How long do Mexican red knees stop eating before a moult?

It varies enormously. A juvenile at 26 °C might fast for two to three weeks; a large adult female may stop for four to eight weeks or longer, and I have had one go nearly ten weeks before she flipped. What matters more than the days is the abdomen staying plump and the retreat being webbed shut. If it is plump and she is in her hide, she is on schedule.

Should I mist the enclosure during ecdysis?

No. Once she is on her back, the enclosure is closed. Misting adds humidity but also risk, because you are opening a lid above a soft, wet animal. Keep the substrate moist before she starts — 60-70 % relative humidity at substrate level — and leave the box sealed while she moults. I have seen a freshly moulted spider injured by a keeper checking on her. The check can wait.

Is a curled spider in pre-moult or dying?

Pre-moult, she sits with legs out or tucked close, usually in or on her hide, and the abdomen stays full. A dying spider, especially one that has fasted for months or been kept too dry, tends to curl the legs underneath in a tight hunch and the abdomen looks sunken. If she is curled and thin rather than folded and plump, that is not pre-moult and a different problem needs treating.
