Budgerigar Goitre: Why a Seed-Only Diet Swells the Throat

A budgie that squeaks instead of chirps and puffs at the throat is not fat and is not egg-bound. Its thyroid is enlarging against a trachea the width of a pencil lead.

A budgerigar's thyroid sits in a 3–4 mm band of tissue wrapped around the trachea in the lower neck, and on a dietary iodine intake below roughly 0.3–0.5 mg/kg of dry food that tissue starts to thicken. Melopsittacus undulatus evolved on a seed diet supplemented by soil, grit and green matter, and the trace mineral it needs most for this gland is the one a cleaned, hulled seed mix carries least. The first symptom is not a lump you can see but a change in voice — a cock that stops whistling and starts squeaking. By the time the swelling is visible at the base of the throat, the gland is pressing on the syrinx and the oesophagus, and the bird is regurgitating seed it never swallowed.

The evening I found the hen behind the nest box

She was a two-year-old normal grey hen, unpaired, kept in the flight aviary with four others. I found her at about 20:40 on a Tuesday, wedged behind a nest box in the corner, beak open, breathing with the whole body. I picked her up — she weighed 38 g on the kitchen scale, down from her usual 44 g — and felt a soft, warm swelling at the base of the throat that moved when she swallowed. I had been feeding a plain canary-and-panicum mix for eighteen months because the birds picked out the millet and left the rest, and I had convinced myself that was cheaper than waste. It was not. The swelling was the size of a pea, and my vet's note the next morning said "thyroid, unilateral, likely iodine-responsive" and gave me a bill for £146 before I had even driven home.

What the thyroid is actually doing, and why iodine is the lever

The thyroid produces two hormones, T3 and T4, from iodine trapped out of the blood. In budgerigars the gland is a paired structure lying ventral to the trachea in the lower neck, close to the thoracic inlet, and in a normal bird it is small enough that you cannot feel it through the skin. When dietary iodine falls short, the pituitary gland releases more thyroid-stimulating hormone to squeeze more out of the gland, and the gland responds the only way it can — by growing. This is goitre: a functional hyperplasia, not a tumour. The tissue thickens, the neck widens, and the gland begins to press on the structures it is wrapped around.

The consequences stack. Compression of the syrinx changes the voice. Compression of the oesophagus causes regurgitation and a slow crop. Compression of the jugular veins raises pressure in the head and can produce a puffy face. Chronically, the gland cannot make enough hormone, so the bird becomes hypothyroid — lethargic, obese on the same ration, slow to moult, and intolerant of cold. Some of those budgerigars die of the goitre itself. Most die of the hypothyroidism behind it, which is far harder to see.

The signs you can check at the perch

Goitre in budgerigars is not a single symptom; it is a cluster, and the cluster is different in a juvenile than in an older hen. The neck swelling is the last sign, not the first. If you are reading the bird at the perch, look for these:

  • Voice change. A cock that was a reliable talker drops to a breathy squeak. A hen that chirped all day goes quiet. This is the syrinx under pressure, and it usually precedes visible swelling by weeks.
  • Regurgitation of seed hulls. Not vomit — the bird flicks out a small spray of half-cracked seed, often onto the cage bars, five or ten minutes after eating.
  • Reduced exercise tolerance. A bird that used to fly a lap of the room now lands after three or four wingbeats.
  • Weight gain on an unchanged ration. Hypothyroidism reduces metabolic rate; a hen can gain 4–6 g without eating more.
  • Delayed moult. The bird hangs onto old feathers, and the head stays pin-feathery through what should be a clean moult season.
  • Cold-seeking. Perched close to the heat source, fluffed, at 24 °C when the rest of the flight is at 20 °C.

The single most useful measurement is a digital kitchen scale used at the same time each morning, before the first feed, on every bird in the group. A 0.1 g resolution is enough to catch a 2 g drift over two weeks, which is the window where an iodine correction still reverses the swelling.

Running the correction, and what to set

The correction has two halves: iodine in, and the rest of the diet rebuilt so the bird does not go back to the same deficiency.

  1. Get the bird to a vet first. Any bird with a visible neck swelling, regurgitation, or a voice change needs a physical exam before you change the diet — a goitre can be a mimic for a seed impaction in the oesophagus, a crop infection, or a lymphosarcoma, and none of those want iodine.
  2. Change the base mix. Move to a fortified pelleted ration as 40–60 % of the diet, with a small amount of seed kept as foraging and reward. Iodine-fortified pellets typically supply the necessary trace mineral; a seed-only mix does not, because the iodine lives in the soil the seed is grown in and the seed coat carries almost none.
  3. Offer a cuttlebone and a mineral block. Not for the calcium alone — some blocks carry iodised salt, and a block left in the flight gives the bird a route to top up at will.
  4. Weigh weekly. A bird on the correction should reach a stable weight within 4–6 weeks. If it is still gaining at the eight-week mark, the diet change is incomplete, or the bird is genuinely hypothyroid and needs the vet.
  5. Keep the room at 22–26 °C during the recovery window. A hypothyroid bird is cold-intolerant, and a drop to 18 °C at night can set it back.

The figures I use: pellets at 40–60 % of the ration, fresh greens (kale, dandelion, broccoli) offered four days out of seven, and seed limited to about a teaspoon per bird per day for a 40–45 g adult. A juvenile in its first six months needs closer to 60–70 % pellets, because the thyroid is being asked to support growth and feather production at the same time.

What changes with the bird's age

A juvenile budgerigar under six months is growing bone, feather and muscle, and its thyroid demand is at its highest. A borderline iodine supply in a young bird does not show as neck swelling — it shows as a slow moult, poor feather quality and a bird that seems fine but never quite fills out. Breeders who keep their pullets on a plain seed mix through the first moult see more of this than the hobby generally admits.

The adult hen in breeding condition is the second risk group. Egg production pulls calcium, protein and iodine together, and a hen that lays a clutch on a seed-only diet is being asked to run a thyroid deficit and a calcium deficit at once. Provide iodised mineral access and pellets at 55–60 % through the breeding window, and rest the hen after two clutches.

The older bird over five years is the third. Its thyroid is already less responsive, and the goitre in a six-year-old is more likely to be a functional hyperplasia on top of an ageing gland than a simple deficiency. That bird needs the vet before you change the diet, because the correction is slower and the compression damage may be permanent.

Ecological half: the flight and the floor

Goitre is a diet problem, but the husbandry around it matters. Budgerigars are ground-feeding birds in the wild, and they spend a significant part of the day foraging on the floor — a bird that never has to work for its seed will pick out the millet and ignore the pellets. Scatter the fortified pellets in a shallow tray with a little seed mixed through, and the bird has to sort for its reward. That sorting behaviour does more for a diet change than any thirty-minute lecture from me.

The floor matters for hygiene too. A goitrous bird that is regurgitating seed is dropping food where the others forage, and in a flock that is a route for yeast and bacterial overgrowth. Clean the floor trays daily through the recovery window. I keep a separate hospital cage for that — not because the bird is infectious, but so I can weigh it twice a day without catching it out of the flight.

One honest gap: nobody has published a controlled iodine requirement trial for budgerigars at hobby-keeper scale, so the mg/kg range above comes from poultry and caged-bird nutrition tables extrapolated to a 40 g bird. That is the state of the evidence. What I trust is the response: the birds that soften within six weeks on a fortified pellet ration and a mineral block are the birds that were deficient, and the ones that do not need a different diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just add iodine drops to the drinking water?

I do not, and I would steer you away from it. Iodine in water is hard to dose, degrades in light, and tastes unpleasant enough that a bird may drink less than it should. The reliable route is a fortified pellet ration plus a mineral block the bird can visit at will. If your vet has prescribed a specific iodine supplement, follow that dose and do not add a second source on top.

How long before the swelling goes down?

With a diet correction alone, a functional goitre in a young adult often softens within four to six weeks, and the voice starts to return around the same time. A compressive goitre with regurgitation takes longer, sometimes eight to twelve, and the swelling may never fully resolve. If the neck is unchanged at eight weeks, the diagnosis needs revisiting, not a stronger dose.

Does a bird with a goitre need to be separated from the flock?

Not for infectious reasons — goitre is not contagious. Separate if the bird is being bullied away from food, if it is regurgitating onto shared surfaces, or if you need twice-daily weights. A hospital cage at 24–26 °C with a shallow food dish and a low perch is easier to monitor than a flight, and the bird is less likely to lose condition in a small, quiet space.
