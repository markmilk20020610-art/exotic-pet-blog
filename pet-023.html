Budgerigar Goitre: Why a Seed-Only Diet Swells the Throat

A budgie that squeaks instead of chirps and puffs at the throat is not fat and is not egg-bound. Its thyroid is enlarging against a trachea the width of a pencil lead.

Melopsittacus undulatus carries a pair of thyroid glands in the thoracic inlet, and in a healthy bird each one is no bigger than a grain of canary seed — a few millimetres long. When iodine runs short, the tissue doesn't fail quietly; it enlarges, sometimes to the size of a pea or a small grape, and compresses whatever is travelling through that narrow space — the oesophagus, the trachea, the syrinx, the nerves to the crop. A swelling of 8-10 mm across is enough to change the sound of the bird's voice and the way it swallows. That is the whole story of budgerigar goitre (from the Latin guttur, throat), and it turns on a trace element we can't see and rarely think about.

The diary: a Thursday night and the bird that squeaked

She was a four-year-old hen, skyblue recessive pied, owned by a couple who had fed her on a plain millet-and-canary mix since they bought her at a show. On the Thursday she came in, she was perched at the bottom of the cage, wings slightly out, and every few breaths she made a thin squeaking noise — the kind of sound a budgie makes when air is being forced through a narrowed pipe. I picked her up and felt a soft fullness low in the neck, just above the wishbone. I could not tell from touch whether it was thyroid or fat. I weighed her: 32 g, down from the 38 g she had weighed two years earlier on my own scales. The vet aspirated nothing useful from the swelling and put her on iodine supplementation and a pellet conversion, and told me the honest truth: had she been seen three weeks earlier the prognosis would have been better. She lived another fourteen months. The bill was £140 for the consult, the X-ray and the bloods. I have not fed a seed-only mix since.

What the thyroid actually does, and why iodine is the bottleneck

The thyroid makes two hormones — thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) — and both require iodine atoms to be assembled. Iodine is a dietary element, meaning the bird cannot make it and cannot store much of it; every molecule has to come from the gut. When iodine falls below what the gland needs, the pituitary gland in the brain senses the shortfall and pumps out thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which tells the thyroid to grow and work harder. The cells multiply, the gland hypertrophies, and because the bird's thyroid sits where it does, the enlargement becomes a mechanical problem before it becomes an endocrine one. The bird is not primarily sick from low thyroid hormone — it is sick because a growing lump is pressing on its airway.

Here is the part people miss. A seed mix is not just low in iodine — it is low in iodine and high in goitrogens, plant compounds that interfere with iodine uptake by the thyroid. Millet, brassicas, and some of the greens keepers offer as "healthy" are all implicated. So the bird gets a double hit: less iodine coming in, and more of what it does get being blocked at the gland. The result is the same swelling, only faster.

How much iodine a budgerigar needs is actually contested. Commercial pellet manufacturers typically build in around 0.3-0.5 mg/kg of diet; older aviculture texts quote figures anywhere from 0.1 to 1 mg/kg, and no single number has been pinned down for the species by controlled study that I can point you to. The clinical consensus among avian veterinarians is that a complete pelleted diet, formulated for psittacines, provides adequate iodine without supplementation. That is the position exotic-animal teaching hospitals take, and it is the one I follow. What is genuinely unknown is the exact requirement of a breeding hen or a moulting bird — the two life stages where demand is highest.

Reading the bird: the symptoms in the order they appear

Early goitre is easy to miss because the bird compensates. It eats, it flies, it chatters — but the voice has changed. A hen that used to say a clear "pretty boy" now produces a croak or a whisper. That voice change is often the first thing a keeper notices, weeks before anything visible. Then the bird starts to sit with its beak slightly open, especially after flying, because the airway is narrowed and it cannot get enough air through the nares alone.

Later signs are mechanical. Regurgitation of food, crop stasis, a change in the shape of the throat when the bird turns its head, weight loss despite a full crop, and eventually respiratory distress at rest. A bird at that stage needs a vet today, not a supplement ordered online. If the bird has stopped eating or is regurgitating, supportive feeding is the bridge to the vet appointment:

For the birds still eating but losing condition, a 0.1 g resolution scale is the only way to know whether your interventions are working. A budgerigar's healthy weight sits between roughly 30 and 40 g depending on frame; weighing at the same time each morning, before feeding, on a digital kitchen scale is the single most useful measurement you can take. Weigh every three days during treatment, not every day — daily weights on a small bird are dominated by crop fill and droppings.

Feeding for iodine: what actually works

The cleanest fix is a complete extruded pellet formulated for small psittacines, fed as the base of the diet — around 60-70 % of intake — with fresh vegetables making up the rest. The pellets are manufactured to a fixed iodine level, which is the whole point. Seed mix, however "fortified" the label claims, does not deliver reliable iodine because the bird selects the seeds it likes and leaves the rest.

Converting a seed junkie is the hard part. Budgerigars are flock feeders and will refuse unfamiliar food for days. The method that has worked for me, and for most keepers I know:

  1. Weigh the bird and record the number. This is your baseline and your safety net.
  2. Offer pellets in a shallow dish alongside the usual seed for two weeks, with no reduction in seed. Let the bird investigate on its own schedule.
  3. Once the bird is seen eating pellets, begin reducing seed by about 10 % of the seed portion every three days, provided the weight holds within 1 g of baseline.
  4. If weight drops more than 2 g over a week, slow down or stop the reduction and go back a step.
  5. Keep offering a small seed ration (no more than 10-15 % of the diet) as enrichment, not as the base.

Do not supplement iodine blindly on top of a complete pellet. Iodine has a narrow therapeutic window, and oversupplementation can produce the opposite problem — hyperthyroidism, with weight loss, increased appetite and hyperactivity. Supplement only under veterinary direction, and only for a bird with confirmed or strongly suspected deficiency.

Life stages: what changes and what doesn't

Juveniles are the easiest to convert, because they have no established preferences. A young budgerigar weaned onto pellets at 8-10 weeks, at 30-35 g, will usually accept them as food with no fuss. This is the single biggest reason to buy from a breeder who weans onto pellets: the bird arrives already iodine-secure, and you never have to fight the conversion battle.

Adults — anything over about six months — are where the conversion fights happen, and where goitre is most often diagnosed, because that is where the cumulative damage from a seed diet shows. A bird that has been on seed for three years may have subclinical enlargement already. The conversion is harder, the monitoring more critical, and the vet checks more frequent.

Breeding hens are the elephant in the room. Egg-laying puts enormous demands on the thyroid and on calcium, and a hen on a seed diet with a developing goitre may produce weak-shelled eggs, become egg-bound, or simply stop laying. The iodine demand in a laying hen is higher than in a resting adult, and the safest approach is a complete breeder pellet plus a check with your vet before the breeding season begins. Do not guess at the dose.

Prevention is the whole game

None of this is complicated once you accept that a seed mix is a treat, not a diet. The complete pellet is the base; vegetables are a supplement; seed is the enrichment. Offer a cuttlebone for calcium (not iodine), fresh water daily, and a bird-safe iodine source only if the diet is not complete. The single biggest variable is what is in the bowl at 7 a.m.

Watch the voice. That is the cheapest diagnostic in the toolkit. A hen that has started to squeak instead of sing is a hen whose thyroid has begun to swell. Weigh every bird every three days for a fortnight after any diet change and keep the numbers in a notebook. The numbers are what tell you whether you are winning.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just add iodine drops to the drinking water?

No. Iodine in water is hard to dose accurately, birds drink variable amounts, and the narrow therapeutic window means you can overshoot into hyperthyroidism. The clinical consensus from avian vets is that a complete pelleted diet provides adequate iodine, and supplementation is reserved for confirmed deficiency under direction.

Will the swelling go down once I change the diet?

Sometimes, in early cases — the gland can shrink when iodine is restored, over weeks to months. In established cases the tissue has scarred and the mechanical problem may persist. Your vet will assess whether the airway is compromised. Do not expect a quick reversal if the bird has been on seed for years.

Are millet sprays as bad as my vet says?

Millet spray is not poisoned — it is simply low in iodine and high in goitrogens, and it is very palatable, so a bird on it may eat little else. Used as an occasional training treat, fine. Used as the base of the diet, it is the classic recipe for goitre.
