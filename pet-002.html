The Best Bioactive Substrate Mix for a Crested Gecko Tank

Coir alone is not bioactive — it is a wet sponge. The mix that actually supports plants, springtails and a gecko that sheds cleanly, plus the drainage layer everyone skips.

A crested gecko (Correlophus ciliatus) wants 72-80 °F in the tank and roughly 60-70 % relative humidity with a dry spell between mistings, and almost every husbandry problem I see traces back to the substrate underfoot holding those numbers wrong. Substrate is the tank's engine: get the drainage and the moisture gradient right and the gecko regulates itself; get it wrong and you have standing water, mould, and an animal that stops eating. This guide is about building that base layer properly, from the drainage up.

From the keeper's notebook: the night the bottom went sour

I still have the notes from a February evening with my adult male, a 45 × 45 × 60 cm glass box in the corner of the room. Lights off at 21:40. I misted as usual, then lifted a cork hide to spot-check the soil and the smell hit me — that flat, sour, anaerobic reek of waterlogged coir. Under the surface the mix was a wet brick. The drainage layer had compacted and the gecko had been spending his nights plastered to the upper glass, avoiding the floor. I pulled the whole thing at 23:00, bagged 12 litres of soggy substrate, and rebuilt it in a single sitting. Cost: the better part of a bag of fresh mix and a wasted evening. The lesson was physical, not philosophical — coir alone holds water far too well.

The substrate layer: what to mix and why the ratios matter

A bioactive mix has to do four jobs: hold humidity without staying saturated, drain freely, support a clean-up crew, and anchor plants. Coir and bark give you water retention and structure; sand adds weight and drainage and keeps the mix from turning to paste. My working ratio is roughly 60 % coir, 25 % bark, 15 % sand by volume — contested in the hobby, and some keepers run leaner on coir, but this is the blend I have run without souring. The reptile bioactive substrate mix is built to that spec, so you get coir, bark and sand together rather than three separate bags.

Underneath, a drainage layer of LECA clay pebbles — lightweight expanded clay, the round baked pellets used in hydroponics — about 2-3 cm deep, keeps the water table below the soil so roots and clean-up crew don't drown.

  1. Layer 2-3 cm of LECA pebbles on the floor of the tank as drainage.
  2. Add a thin barrier if you like, then 5-8 cm of the coir-bark-sand mix on top.
  3. Plant, then seed the clean-up crew: a springtail culture scattered over the damp surface.
  4. Mist to settle, then let the surface dry between waterings — crumble-dry at the top, cool and damp below.

Moisture, temperature and the failure mode to watch for

The instrument to trust is a hygrometer placed at soil level, not at the top glass, because the gecko lives at both. Aim for the 60-70 % band at night after misting, and let it fall to 50 % or so by late afternoon. Temperature holds 72-80 °F; below 70 °F the clean-up crew slows and the tank goes stagnant. The classic mistake is over-misting to chase a number: water pools in the drainage layer, the soil goes anaerobic, and you get fungus gnats and a floor the gecko avoids. If the surface stays wet past midday, you are overwatering — cut back and let the pebble layer breathe.

Seeding and maintaining the clean-up crew

Springtails — tiny wingless hexapods that eat mould, fungus and decaying organic matter — are the workhorses of a bioactive floor. A live culture scattered over the damp surface gets them established within a couple of weeks if the soil stays moist but not soaked. They need the same conditions as your gecko: warm, humid, never drowned. I check the crew by turning a corner of bark and looking for movement; if they have vanished, the substrate is either too dry or too wet. Feed the system with leaf litter and the occasional bit of vegetable matter rather than trusting it to be self-sustaining from day one.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use coir alone as substrate?

You can, but I would not. Coir holds water tightly and compacts, so it drains poorly and sours fast. Without bark for structure and sand for drainage, you get a wet brick rather than a living floor. The blend matters more than any single component.

How often do I replace a bioactive substrate?

With a healthy clean-up crew, you rarely replace the whole mix — you top it up. Spot-clean waste, turn the soil occasionally, and refresh the top few centimetres when it breaks down. A full rebuild every year or two is what I budget for, but the smell and drainage tell you when.

How do I know if my substrate is too wet?

Press a finger into the mix. It should feel cool and damp, not sodden, and the surface should be crumble-dry by afternoon. If water pools in the drainage layer, or the soil smells sour and anaerobic, you are overwatering. Let it dry back before misting again.
