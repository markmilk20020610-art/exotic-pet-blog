The Best Bioactive Substrate Mix for a Crested Gecko Tank

Coir alone is not bioactive — it is a wet sponge. The mix that actually supports plants, springtails and a gecko that sheds cleanly, plus the drainage layer everyone skips.

Almost every "bioactive" crested gecko setup online is a layer of coco coir with a fake plant pushed into it. It looks the part, it holds water, and it is not bioactive in any meaningful sense — there is nothing living in it to process waste, and no drainage to stop it going anaerobic.

A working substrate does three jobs at once: it drains, it hosts a cleanup crew, and it grows roots. That requires a layered mix, not a single bag of fibre.

  • 60% organic topsoil — the structural base. Must be free of fertiliser, perlite and wetting agents.
  • 30% coco coir — moisture retention. Coir alone is too dense; as a third of the mix it is ideal.
  • 10% coarse sand or fine orchid bark — drainage and particle size variety, which is what stops the mix turning to mud.
  • A handful of leaf litter on the surface — food and cover for the crew, and it slows evaporation.
  • Crushed cuttlebone — calcium for isopod moults. Without it, a colony quietly fails.

The drainage layer nobody builds

Under the substrate, 4–5 cm of LECA clay pebbles, separated by a fine mesh or weed fabric. Crested gecko enclosures are misted twice a day; without drainage, the bottom two centimetres of substrate become anaerobic within weeks and smell of sulphur when disturbed.

The drainage layer is also where your water reserve lives. You should be able to see the water level through the glass and keep it below the mesh — never touching the soil.

A bioactive floor without drainage is not a ecosystem. It is a terrarium-shaped bog.

Depth, and why 7–10 cm is the number

Shallow substrate cannot buffer moisture or support plant roots. Deep substrate without drainage drowns. Seven to ten centimetres over a drainage layer is the range that gives springtails somewhere to retreat when the surface dries, and gives plants enough root volume to establish.

If your enclosure is front-opening with a bottom vent, keep the substrate line below the vent. A blocked vent turns a well-designed enclosure into a stagnant box.

Seeding the crew — and the order matters

Add springtails first, generously, and give them two weeks before the gecko goes in. They colonise the mould before it becomes visible. Then add dwarf isopods — Trichorhina tomentosa is the safe choice for a humid, planted enclosure because it does not burrow deeply or chew live plants.

Do not add large Porcellio species to a crested gecko tank. They are protein-driven, and a sleeping gecko on the floor is, to them, a very large piece of scenery worth investigating.

What "cycled" actually looks like

A cycled substrate is dark, smells of forest floor, holds a burrow when you press it, and has visible springtails when you lift the leaf litter. It does not smell sour, it does not grow white fuzz across the surface, and it does not stay wet for a week.

Expect two to four weeks. Feed the crew a vegetable scrap once a week during that period, then stop — the gecko's waste takes over.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use garden soil from outside?

Only from ground you know is unsprayed, and only after baking or freezing it. The risk is not dirt — it is pesticides, which will kill your cleanup crew and can harm the gecko.

Do I ever have to replace the substrate?

Not on a schedule. Spot-clean visible waste, keep the crew alive, and top up leaf litter. Replace it if it turns anaerobic or the crew crashes — both are symptoms of a design problem, not a maintenance interval.

Will the gecko eat the isopods?

Occasionally, and it does no harm. A healthy crew out-breeds casual predation.
