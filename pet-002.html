The Best Bioactive Substrate Mix for a Crested Gecko Tank

Coir alone is not bioactive — it is a wet sponge. The mix that actually supports plants, springtails and a gecko that sheds cleanly, plus the drainage layer everyone skips.

A crested gecko (Correlophus ciliatus) enclosure sits between 22-27 °C (72-80 °F) at the warm end and 60-70 % relative humidity, and almost everything the animal does — hunting, shedding, digesting a meal — is timed to those two readings. Hold the humidity above that band for a fortnight and you are not just keeping a damp box; you are giving respiratory bacteria a standing invitation. Drop it below and the shed starts to stick in rings around the toes.

The keeper's diary

I found the female on the base of the drainage layer, not on the cork bark where she usually slept, at about 21:40 on a Tuesday in February. The vivarium had been running wet for nine days because I had overfilled the misting reservoir — the digital hygrometer read 84 % and had done so all week. She was dull-eyed and her abdomen was still plump, which meant she had not simply gone off food. Two toes on the left rear foot carried a retained shed, a pale collar of skin. The fix was a paper-towel quarantine tub, two weeks, and a single £4 hygrometer that I should have bought a year earlier. The chest infection cleared with a vet visit; the lesson cost me a fortnight and a consultation fee, and I have misted to a rechargeable timer ever since.

Building the drainage sandwich

Bioactive means the substrate hosts a living clean-up crew — springtails and, commonly, dwarf isopods — that eat mould, droppings and rotting plant matter. Without a drainage layer the "bioactive" part drowns. The sandwich goes in this order:

  1. Two to three centimetres of LECA clay pebbles (lightweight expanded clay, water-resistant) across the base of the enclosure, rising to four centimetres at the low corner.
  2. A permeable landscape fabric barrier over the pebbles — the mesh keeps the substrate from silting into the drainage reservoir.
  3. Eight to ten centimetres of the reptile bioactive substrate mix (coir · bark · sand) across the top, deeper at the planted end.
  4. Springtails inoculated into the top two centimetres, with a small pinch of leaf litter as a food source.

A 45 × 45 × 60 cm enclosure takes roughly two litres of LECA and ten to twelve litres of substrate. The sand fraction is the part newcomers underweight; coir holds water like a sponge, and without the mineral grit the mix compacts into a wet brick within weeks. Aim for a substrate that holds its shape when squeezed and crumbles when poked — too wet and the roots rot, too dry and the springtail culture starves.

Watering, humidity and the failure modes

I mist once in the evening, heavily enough that the substrate surface darkens, then let the enclosure dry back so the hygrometer reads 50-60 % by mid-afternoon before the next mist. A single evening spray that has dried to 45 % the next morning is the correct rhythm. The mistake I see most is the constant 80 % box above; the second is a drainage layer left full to the brim with stagnant water. Syphon the reservoir every two weeks — the water should never touch the bottom of the substrate.

Springtails need warmth and damp, not a swimming pool. If the culture dies out, add a fresh introduction and lightly dampen the surface for a week. There is genuine disagreement in the hobby about whether a pure coir-based mix can sustain a colony long-term without the sand fraction; my own trials suggest it can for a few months, then thins out.

Light, plants and the cleaning crew

Live plants — pothos and similar epiphytes — anchor the bioactive system by drawing excess moisture and nitrate from the substrate. Crested geckos do not need a UVB lamp to survive, but there is no harm in a low-output 2 %-type tube, and a small dose (around 0.6-1.1 UVI at the basking spot) is what some keepers use to support plant growth and vitamin D synthesis. Whether it improves the gecko's health is genuinely unsettled in the literature; I use it and cannot prove the case either way. Avoid any bulb that pushes the enclosure above 27 °C.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use coco coir on its own?

For a planted bioactive tank, no — pure coir holds too much moisture and compacts. Mix it with bark and a measurable sand fraction, roughly by eye a third of each, so water drains through rather than pooling. Standalone coir works as a simple quarantine substrate but will not support a springtail colony for long.

How often do I have to replace the substrate?

In a well-drained, well-colonised bioactive setup, rarely — six to twelve months, or sooner if the reservoir smells sour. Spot-clean droppings and dead leaf litter weekly. If springtails vanish and mould appears on the surface, that is the substrate telling you it is waterlogged or spent.

My gecko keeps sitting on the glass door at night — is that a humidity problem?

Sometimes. Check the hygrometer first: if the surface near the door reads below 50 %, the animal is probably looking for damp. If humidity is fine, it may simply be hunting at floor level. Weigh it weekly — a 25-gram adult that drops under 22 grams needs a vet look, not a new substrate mix.
