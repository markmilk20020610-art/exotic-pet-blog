The Best Bioactive Substrate Mix for a Crested Gecko Tank

Coir alone is not bioactive — it is a wet sponge. The mix that actually supports plants, springtails and a gecko that sheds cleanly, plus the drainage layer everyone skips.

An adult Correlophus ciliatus is 20-25 cm long including the tail, and its skin loses water faster than a leopard gecko's — no eyelids to blink away the drying, and a welfare budget that lives inside 60-80 % relative humidity, cycling down to 50-60 % by day and back up overnight. Cross those bands for a week and the first thing you lose is appetite, not weight.

The night I found him on the glass, not in the plants

October, 22:40, and my subadult male — 34 g, weighed that morning — was flat against the front pane at the top, all four feet splayed. I lifted the coconut hide and the coir underneath smelled of stagnant pond and crumbled into grey paste between my fingers. The drainage layer had flooded; the overflow I drilled was too small. I lost that springtail culture in four days. He did not eat for nine days, and I spent £40 on a second hygrometer to confirm what my nose already knew: the base of the tank sat at 92 % humidity for hours with no air moving. What fixed it was not more moss. It was a rebuild with a proper reservoir and a mix that drains.

What "bioactive" actually means for a gecko that licks its own face

A bioactive setup is not substrate with bugs in it. It is a small nitrogen cycle in a box: the clean-up crew breaks faeces and shed skin into simpler compounds, the mineral and carbon fraction of the mix holds moisture without going anaerobic, and plants pull nitrate back out. When it works you are managing ammonia and moisture, not scooping. When it fails you have a saturated, oxygen-starved layer producing hydrogen sulphide, with the gecko sitting above it in still air. That failure is invisible from the top and detectable at the bottom, which is why drainage matters more than any ingredient list.

The mix I run, by volume: roughly 60 % coir, 25 % fine orchid bark, 15 % coarse sand, over a 5 cm LECA layer with a mesh barrier between. I will say plainly that no controlled trial compares hobby mixes to each other. What we have is a mechanism and a lot of drowned springtails.

Running it: the numbers I actually set

A juvenile under 15 g starts in 30 × 30 × 45 cm; a 45 × 45 × 60 cm front-opening vivarium is the realistic adult minimum, and taller beats wider because this is an arboreal species that shelters in the canopy. Total substrate depth of 7-10 cm gives roots and crew somewhere to work.

  1. Drainage. LECA to 5 cm, mesh sheet over. Slope the tank so one corner is your water table; if standing water touches the soil line, you have overfilled.
  2. Moisture cycling. Mist the back wall and foliage in the evening to 80-90 % RH, let it fall to 55-65 % by late afternoon. Two hygrometers: one 5 cm above substrate, one mid-canopy. If the base reads above 85 % for six hours, cut misting.
  3. Temperature. 22-26 °C warm end, 19-21 °C overnight. Digestion stalls below about 20 °C, so do not feed below it — the food rots in the animal instead of the tank.
  4. Clean-up crew. Seed with a temperate springtail culture and dwarf isopods, and feed the crew dried beech or oak leaf litter. Restock when you can no longer find a springtail under a leaf within ten seconds.
  5. Feeding. CGD at 2:1 powder to water, refreshed every 48 hours. Insects twice weekly for juveniles, weekly or less for adults. Remove uneaten insects; a cricket will chew a sleeping gecko.

Where keepers get it wrong

The commonest failure is over-wet, not too dry. A bioactive base with no drainage and heavy daily misting becomes a swamp in a fortnight: springtails drown, isopods climb to the surface, and anaerobic pockets form where the coir meets the glass. The tell is a sweet, sour smell or a gecko that suddenly refuses CGD. The fix is mechanical — more overflow, more LECA, misting once nightly, a small fan on a timer for a few hours a day.

The second is under-seeding. Two tablespoons of springtail culture in a 90 cm tank is a snack, not a crew. Seed heavily and re-seed after big feeds, because the population will crash. The third is misting the top and forgetting the base: the gecko is fine in the canopy while everything below the drainage line quietly goes anoxic. Feel the base weekly. Cold and sodden means you have your answer.

Morphs, age and season

Harlequin, pinstripe, dalmatian, cappuccino — colour morphs change nothing about substrate or humidity. Lily white carries a documented risk of a fatal white-cell condition in some lines, and that is a veterinary conversation, not a husbandry one. What does change husbandry is age and photoperiod: a 12-on, 12-off light cycle plus a genuine night drop to 19-21 °C supports feeding better than any appetite supplement. Breeding females need insects dusted with calcium at every feed, and a drop of more than 5 % body weight between weekly weigh-ins warrants a vet.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use plain coir with no drainage layer?

You can, and I did for two years before I lost a springtail culture to it. Coir alone compacts and holds water at the base. Add a 5 cm LECA reservoir under a mesh barrier; it costs little and prevents the anaerobic layer that kills crews and stresses the gecko.

How often should I replace the substrate entirely?

In a healthy bioactive setup, rarely — some keepers go years without a full change. Spot-clean faeces, top up leaf litter monthly, and replace only if the base smells sour, the crew collapses twice, or mould shows at the glass. Quarterly checks are enough.

Do I need a drainage layer in a smaller juvenile tank?

Yes, or you need to mist lightly and weigh weekly. Juveniles dehydrate faster than adults — 6-15 g is not much water reserve — so a shallow LECA layer and two hygrometers are cheap insurance. Dry-down matters as much as peak humidity.
