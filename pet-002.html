The Best Bioactive Substrate Mix for a Crested Gecko Tank

Coir alone is not bioactive — it is a wet sponge. The mix that actually supports plants, springtails and a gecko that sheds cleanly, plus the drainage layer everyone skips.

A crested gecko (Correlophus ciliatus) is kept in a 45 × 45 × 60 cm enclosure held between 72-80 °F and 60-70 % relative humidity, and almost every husbandry decision you make is really a decision about whether those two numbers stay put. Miss high on humidity for a week and you are culturing bacteria, not keeping geckos; miss low and the next shed comes off in patches with toes still wrapped in skin. The substrate is the machine that runs both numbers, which is why I care about it more than most people think is reasonable.

The night the drainage layer failed

I set up a 45 × 45 × 60 cm tank on a Tuesday, LECA in the bottom, a mesh divider, and what I thought was a sensible amount of water. By Thursday evening the glass was fogged at 91 % on my hygrometer and the false bottom had gone from a drainage layer to a swamp. I lifted the mesh, and the smell that came up was the specific sour note of anaerobic soil — organic matter breaking down with no oxygen. The clean-up crew, forty-odd springtails, were clustered on the mesh above the waterline like passengers on a raft. I lost most of them, redid the whole floor at 11 p.m., and learned that a drainage layer is only drainage if the water has somewhere to go and the soil above it is coarse enough to let air in. I had built a bucket with a lid.

What a bioactive mix actually has to do

Bioactive means the enclosure processes its own waste: a living clean-up crew of springtails and isopods eats mould, fungus and droppings, and the substrate hosts the bacteria that finish the job. For that to work the mix has to hold moisture without going sour, stay open enough for air, and support plants. The mix I use and recommend is coir, bark and sand in roughly equal parts by volume — coir holds water, bark keeps it airy, sand gives structure and weight so plants root and the gecko's feet find purchase. The exact ratio matters less than the texture: it should clump when squeezed and break apart when poked, not drip.

Depth is the decision most people get wrong. I run a 5-8 cm soil layer over a 5 cm LECA drainage layer, with a mesh screen between them. Under 5 cm of soil and the springtails have nowhere to retreat from a dry spell; over 10 cm and you are storing water you cannot monitor. This is the point where the gear matters, so:

The drainage layer goes in dry, the mesh goes on top, and the soil goes in damp — not wet. If water pools visibly above the LECA on day one, you have overwatered before the tank has even started.

Running the moisture cycle

Crested geckos are arboreal and nocturnal, and they do not want a permanently damp floor. My routine, which I check with a hygrometer at floor level and a second one mid-tank:

  1. Mist the glass and foliage in the evening, enough that droplets run, aiming at 60-70 % relative humidity overnight.
  2. Let the tank dry toward 50-60 % by late afternoon. That dip is not a failure; it is what keeps mould from taking hold.
  3. Water the soil directly, at the base of plants, no more than once a week, and only if the top 2 cm reads dry on a finger test.
  4. Never let standing water sit in the drainage layer for more than a day. If it does, you have watered too much, not drained too little.

The clean-up crew is your early warning system. Springtails that stay on the surface and refuse to burrow mean the soil is waterlogged; springtails massing on the glass mean it is too dry. A starter culture scattered onto damp soil in the first week establishes far faster than one added later: If you see mites — tiny, fast, pale specks moving independently of the springtails — treat that as a quarantine problem and isolate the tank before it spreads.

Feeding and the substrate connection

Substrate and feeding meet in one place: the floor. Crested geckos are largely frugivorous and take a complete powdered diet mixed with water, plus occasional insects. I feed from a small dish mounted higher in the tank so that spilled food does not land in the soil, and I wipe any spill within the hour — powdered diet is exactly the sugary substrate that mould and flies love. Two dish feeds a week suits most adults; portion to what gets eaten overnight, roughly a teaspoon of mixed diet, and remove the rest in the morning.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use plain coco coir on its own?

You can, and many keepers do, but it packs down, holds water at the surface and offers little structure for plants. Mix in bark for air and a little sand for weight. A pure coir floor turns into a wet sponge within a fortnight in a tank at 70 % humidity, and the clean-up crew suffers first.

How often do I need to replace the substrate?

In a working bioactive tank, not often — I spot-clean and top up, and I have run floors for over a year without a full change. Replace it when the smell turns sour, when the crew collapses, or when the soil has broken down to sludge. Check texture yearly at minimum.

Will springtails survive if I let the tank dry out?

Some will, in the deeper, damper layer, but a hard dry-out knocks the population back badly and you lose your waste processing for weeks. The daily mist-and-dry cycle is the compromise. If the crew vanishes, reseed rather than assume the tank is still bioactive.
