Degu Sugar Sensitivity: Why One Raisin Is a Different Risk Than One for a Rat

Degus are the only common pet rodent that cannot handle dietary sugar at all. The mechanism is a missing gene, and it makes fruit a species-inappropriate food rather than a treat.

A degu's resting blood glucose sits somewhere around 5.5–8.3 mmol/L (roughly 100–150 mg/dL), and that range is not a courtesy — it is the animal's whole metabolic identity. Octodon degus is a caviomorph rodent from central Chile that has been studied for decades precisely because it develops a diabetes-like syndrome that rats and mice mostly resist, and because its natural diet is scrub and grass rather than seed. The insulin axis that keeps those numbers tight is slow, easily overloaded, and it does not recover the way a predator's would.

The evening the bowl was wrong

I have kept degus since 2011, and I will tell you about the November evening that changed how I portion their food. I had a four-year-old female, the dominant one, and I had been giving the group a small handful of raisins for "training" — three or four each, most evenings. Nothing dramatic happened. That is the point. Over about six weeks her abdomen thickened at the ribs rather than the flanks, and one night I found her sitting hunched on the shelf, fur slightly ruffled, refusing the piece of apple the others mobbed for. I weighed her on a 0.1 g scale I use for hatchling weights: 268 g, up from 232 g in the spring. A urine dipstick at the vet read strongly positive for glucose. The bill was £96 and the diagnosis was "stop the fruit and revisit the diet". I still have the scale. I no longer own the raisin tub.

What the insulin axis is actually doing

Degus are unusual among small rodents in having a relatively low insulin secretory capacity and a high reliance on dietary fibre fermentation in the caecum. When you feed a sugar load — and for a degu, "sugar" includes fruit, honey treats, molasses-bound pellets and many commercial "rodent mixes" — blood glucose rises, the pancreas releases insulin, and the peripheral tissues take up glucose. In a rat that loop is fast and forgiving. In a degu it is sluggish, and repeated load on a slow axis pushes the animal toward insulin resistance: the tissues stop responding properly, the pancreas works harder, and eventually the beta cells tire. The clinical picture is persistent hyperglycaemia, glucose in the urine (which draws water with it and produces the polyuria you will notice as a wet cage), and, if it runs long enough, cataract formation and weight loss despite eating. This is not type 1 diabetes in the human sense; it is closer to a diet-induced insulin-resistant state, and the veterinary consensus is that it is largely preventable by what you put in the bowl.

Fibre is the other half of the mechanism. A degu's hindgut ferments structural carbohydrate into volatile fatty acids, which the animal absorbs as a slow-release energy source. That slow release keeps blood glucose flat rather than spiking it. A high-fibre diet is not just "healthier" — it is literally the buffer that protects the insulin response from being asked to do work it cannot do.

Building a bowl that will not break the axis

The target is a diet where the bulk is structural fibre: grass hay, fresh or dried, ad libitum, plus a small measured portion of a low-sugar, high-fibre pellet. Look for a crude fibre figure of at least 18–20 % on the pellet label and a sugar/starch content you can actually read; if the label will not tell you, treat the product as a treat. A reasonable adult portion is 10–15 g of pellet per animal per day alongside unlimited hay, adjusted against body weight weekly.

Which brings us to the measuring. A degu's weight is the single most sensitive instrument you have for early trouble. Weigh weekly, same time of day, on a 0.1 g scale, and record it. A steady climb of 10–15 % over a month, or a drop of the same, is a signal to change something before you change anything else.

  1. Anchor the diet in hay. Offer a grass hay (timothy, meadow) fresh daily; discard what is soiled or damp. This is the base, not the garnish.
  2. Measure the pellet. 10–15 g per adult per day, split across two feeds. Do not top up a bowl that still has food in it.
  3. Treats: one small piece, twice a week maximum. A single raisin, or a fingernail-sized piece of a low-sugar vegetable. Not fruit daily. Not "training treats" from a bag labelled for hamsters.
  4. Water always, and check intake. Increased drinking alongside increased urination is the earliest visible sign of hyperglycaemia. Watch the bottle level across a day.
  5. Weigh weekly and write it down. A paper chart on the cage beats a mental note.

What changes with age and stage

  • Juveniles (weaning to ~6 months): growing animals need more energy density and can take a slightly richer pellet; still no sugar. Hay from day one so the caecum is colonised with the right fibre-fermenting flora.
  • Subadults (6–18 months): the highest-risk window for keeper error, because they are active, hungry and easy to over-treat. Lock the treat ration now.
  • Adults (18 months +): maintenance pellet, hay, weekly weights, and a lower treat ceiling. This is when insulin resistance tends to surface if you have been generous.
  • Seniors: watch for weight loss with a normal appetite — that combination is worth a vet visit and a urine check.

Reading the warning signs — and the table

None of this is subtle if you are looking, and most of it is measurable at home. The table below is what I use when a keeper emails me about a degu that "seems off".

The honest caveat: degu diabetes is studied more in the laboratory than in the pet enclosure, and the exact threshold where a pet degu tips into clinical disease is not settled in the literature I can defend. What is settled is that the axis is slow and the diet is the lever. Err toward fibre and restraint.

Keeping the enclosure working for the diet

A degu that is bored eats. That is not a moral failing; it is a rodent with a scratch-and-forage instinct and an enclosure that may not feed it. Build the cage as a small system: deep substrate to dig in (a mix of soil and coir, 10–15 cm deep, spot-cleaned and refreshed), untreated branches to chew, and hay scattered rather than piled so the animal has to search for it. Foraging raises the time spent eating and lowers the total taken in. A cage that runs 18–24 °C with good ventilation and a dry substrate also means you are reading the animal's urine output against a clean floor rather than a damp one.

The single best upgrade for a degu keeper is not a treat — it is a chart. Weigh weekly, note water intake, note what went in the bowl. That log will tell you about a diet problem two months before the vet does.

Frequently asked questions

Can a degu ever have fruit?

Rarely, and never as routine. A fingernail-sized piece of low-sugar fruit once or twice a week is the ceiling I use, and none at all if the animal is gaining weight or showing any urinary signs. Fruit juice, dried fruit and honey-bound treats are out — they are exactly the sugar load the animal's insulin axis handles worst.

Will a "low-sugar" hamster pellet solve it?

Not automatically. Read the crude fibre figure and the starch content on the label; many small-rodent pellets are grain-heavy and marketed to a different species. Aim for 18–20 % crude fibre or higher and treat anything you cannot read the composition of as a treat rather than a staple.

How do I know if my degu is diabetic rather than just fat?

You cannot from the cage alone. Weight gain at the ribs, wet bedding, heavy drinking and a cloudy eye are the signs that warrant a vet visit and a urine dipstick. A vet can measure blood glucose and confirm; I would not guess from appearance. Early diet change is the best intervention, so act on the signs, not on the diagnosis.
