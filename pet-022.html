Degu Sugar Sensitivity: Why One Raisin Is a Different Risk Than One for a Rat

Degus are the only common pet rodent that cannot handle dietary sugar at all. The mechanism is a missing gene, and it makes fruit a species-inappropriate food rather than a treat.

An adult degu (Octodon degus) maintains a body temperature near 37-38 °C, and the gut behind it is a fermentation chamber tuned to a diet of roughly 60-80 % fibre and almost no free sugar. Push a glucose load through that system and the animal's insulin response is blunted — the physiology of a species that evolved on dry scrub and never met a ripe fruit. That is the whole reason a degu diet conversation is a fibre conversation, not a sugar one.

Why the degu's insulin system is not a rat's

Degus are caviomorph rodents, close to the chinchilla end of the family tree, and their feeding ecology is a textbook of scarcity. In the wild they eat dry grasses, bark, seeds and the occasional root; the fibre fraction is high and the simple sugar fraction is close to zero. The pancreas of a rodent adapted to that diet does not need to handle a rapid post-prandial glucose spike, and the clinical picture in captive degus is of animals that respond to a glucose load with a sluggish, prolonged rise rather than a sharp clear.

The mechanism that matters for us is the one in the hindgut. A degu's large intestine is a fermentation vat, and the bacteria there convert fibre into short-chain fatty acids that the animal absorbs as an energy source. This is why fibre is not "roughage" in the usual sense for degus — it is a food supply. Strip the fibre out and two things happen at once: the fermentation factory runs down, and the animal is forced to rely on a diet that hits the small intestine as absorbable sugar. The caecum shrinks, the stool gets loose, and the animal starts eating its own bedding to find fibre it is not getting.

I should be honest about the limits here. There is no large controlled feeding trial in degus I can point you to that gives a clean fibre target. What there is, and what I lean on, is clinical consensus from exotic-animal practice and the experience of keepers who have been running colony diets for a decade. The consensus is a crude fibre target of 18-22 % of the dry diet for adults, with fermentable fibre from grass and hay doing the heavy lifting. I would not defend a number to one decimal place, and anyone who does is bluffing.

Building the diet: the fibre-to-sugar ratio, not the sugar alone

The practical question is not "can my degu have a raisin?" — the answer to that is already no, and it belongs in a different article. The practical question is what the bowl looks like every morning, because a degu will eat the palatable fraction first and leave the fibre if you let it choose.

Here is the ration I run, per adult of roughly 200-250 g body weight:

  • Grass hay, ad libitum. This is not a supplement, it is the base of the diet. Aim for a hay rack that the animal works at throughout the day. A degu that has to pull hay through a rack gets more fibre and more tooth wear than one eating from a pile on the floor.
  • Pellets formulated for degus, 10-15 g per adult per day, split across two feeds. Check the guaranteed analysis: you are looking for fibre at 18 % or above and sugar low enough that it is not in the first five ingredients. If the label reads like a rabbit muesli, put it back.
  • Fresh leafy greens, a small handful (roughly 10-15 g) two or three times a week: dandelion, plantain, chicory, dark lettuce. These add moisture and micronutrients without a sugar load.
  • Root vegetables and fruit: not a routine food. If you use them at all, treat them as a rare enrichment item and weigh the animal before and after. I no longer feed fruit to any degu in my care, and I stopped in 2021 after the pear.

The one thing to watch is the animal that is "eating well" but losing condition. A degu on a high-fibre diet needs to drink, and a degu that is not drinking will go off fibre first. Check water intake before you change the diet.

Juvenile, subadult and adult: what actually changes

The biggest mistake I see is feeding a growing degu like a sedentary adult, then wondering why it is fat at 18 months. Growth is expensive, and a juvenile of 8-16 weeks needs more calories per gram of body weight than an adult — but the fibre base does not change. What changes is the proportion of pellets to hay, not the type of food.

By roughly 6 months the animal is at adult weight, and the ration should settle into the adult pattern. Senior degus, generally over 4-5 years, are the ones I watch most closely: they lose muscle mass in the hindquarters, and a degu that cannot reach its hay rack will lose fibre intake before it loses condition. Move the rack lower and feed smaller, more frequent meals. That single change has added good months to more than one old animal of mine.

The failure mode: what a sugar slip actually looks like

A degu does not go into a neat diabetic crisis the way the textbook drawing suggests. What you see is vaguer than that, and it is why the condition gets missed.

  1. Days 1-2: the animal is a bit flatter than usual, picks at pellets, sits with its belly on the floor more than it should. The stool gets looser and paler.
  2. Days 3-7: weight starts to drift down — 2-5 g over the week is enough to notice on a 0.1 g scale — while the animal eats. That combination of eating and losing is the flag.
  3. Week 2 and beyond: condition loss in the hindquarters, a dull coat, and if it is allowed to continue, a degu that is genuinely unwell. This is where a vet visit stops being optional.

If you are already in that state, strip the bowl back to hay and degu pellets, stop every treat, and get the animal weighed daily on the same scale at the same time of day. The cheapest diagnostic tool you own is the kitchen scale, and it is faster than any blood test.

I am deliberately not giving a glucose curve target here. There is genuine uncertainty about what constitutes a normal degu glucose response, and the numbers I have seen quoted in hobby circles do not all agree. Where I am unsure, I say so. What I am sure of is the pattern: fibre up, sugar out, and the scale tells you whether the animal is improving.

Frequently asked questions

Can a degu have fruit as an occasional treat?

I would not. The insulin response of this species is slow and blunted by design, and a single piece of fruit is a glucose load it is not built to clear. If you insist on a treat, use a pinch of dried dandelion or a fresh leaf, and weigh the animal the same evening. That weighing habit is the real safety net, not the choice of treat.

How much fibre should be in a degu pellet?

Look for 18-22 % crude fibre on the guaranteed analysis, with a named grass or hay meal near the top of the ingredient list. Below 15 % I would not feed it to an adult. Above 25 % is not automatically better — very high-fibre pellets can be less palatable, and a degu that refuses its pellets is a degu eating nothing but hay and losing weight. Read the label, then watch the animal.

My degu is losing weight but eating hay all day — what is happening?

Two likely causes. First, the hay may be stemmy and low in digestible fibre; switch to a softer, leafier grass hay and see if weight stabilises over 10-14 days. Second, the animal may not be drinking enough to move that fibre through the gut. Check the water bottle daily, offer a second source, and if the weight is still falling after a week, that is a vet appointment.
