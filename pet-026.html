Russian Tortoise Hibernation: The Weight Threshold That Decides Whether to Brumate

Brumation is not a calendar event, it is a metabolic budget. Below one ratio the animal has nothing to spend, and a fridge in November becomes the cause of death rather than the method.

An adult Testudo horsfieldii that weighs 400 g in October should weigh roughly the same 400 g in February — a loss of more than 1 % of body mass per week inside the brumation box is the needle that tells you a winter is going wrong. In horsesfield tortoises the pre-brumation assessment is a mass-budget problem: the animal has about 8-12 weeks of cool, dark conditions ahead, and it can only draw on the fat body and hepatic reserves it carries in. Get the arithmetic wrong and the animal does not wake up thin. It wakes up dead, or it wakes up with a respiratory infection that was already there before you boxed it.

The night the scale read 312 g

I keep a Jackson ratio chart taped inside the lid of a plastic tub in the shed. The chart is a crude thing — carapace length in millimetres against weight in grams, plotted to give a single number — and it is the only pre-hibernation tool I trust enough to use every autumn. In October 2019 I had a female I had bought that spring at 108 mm straight carapace length, and she weighed 312 g. The Jackson ratio came out at about 0.238, which sits right on the line where the literature says "borderline, do not brumate". I brumated her anyway, because I had read a forum post saying females were heavier than the formula allowed for, and because I had already told myself she was "a big-boned animal". She went into the fridge at 5 °C in late November. By the fourth week she had lost 38 g. I pulled her out on 2 January, warmed her slowly over six hours, and she refused food for nineteen days. She survived. She should not have. I have not brumated an animal below 0.245 since, and I now weigh every animal weekly through the winter rather than monthly, which is the single change I would push on any keeper.

What the Jackson ratio actually measures — and what it does not

The Jackson ratio is a weight-to-length index, not a body-condition score. It is calculated as weight in grams divided by the cube of the straight carapace length in centimetres, and the classic guidance puts a healthy adult Testudo in the 0.19-0.23 band, with 0.23 and above regarded as safe to brumate and below 0.19 as a hard no. The number is a proxy for density: a tortoise that has packed away fat and has adequate bone and shell mass will float higher on the scale than a dehydrated or emaciated one of the same length. It is not a measure of hydration, organ health, or the presence of an infection, and it is not reliable in juveniles, in gravid females in the weeks before egg-laying, or in animals with shell deformities that distort the length measurement.

The "0.23 safe" line is hobby consensus, not a controlled trial, and it is worth saying so. Exotic-animal veterinary teaching hospitals generally treat the Jackson ratio as a screening tool to be combined with a physical exam and a faecal check, not as a standalone passport into the fridge. Some clinicians use 0.21 as the lower cut-off in smaller or first-winter animals. I use 0.23 for adults going into a full 10-12 week brumation and 0.21 for a short 6-8 week brumation in a subadult, and I say plainly that the evidence for the exact threshold is thin.

Building the pre-brumation assessment

The assessment is a sequence, not a single weighing. Run it over four weeks in late summer and early autumn, and write every number down, because your memory will lie to you about whether the animal was 380 g or 408 g in August.

  1. Weigh weekly, on the same scale, at the same time of day. Use a 0.1 g resolution kitchen scale — the cheap ones are fine, but check them against a known mass once a season. Weigh after the animal has been at the cool end for two hours and has not eaten for 48 hours. Record the mass in grams and the date.
  2. Measure straight carapace length. Run a ruler or calipers from the front edge of the carapace (over the neck) to the rear edge of the supracaudal scute. Record millimetres. Do this three times and take the median.
  3. Calculate the Jackson ratio. Mass in grams divided by (length in cm)³. For a 108 mm, 400 g animal: 400 / (10.8 × 10.8 × 10.8) = 400 / 1259.7 = 0.318. Record it.
  4. Check the mass trend. An animal gaining 2-5 g per week through September is putting down reserves. An animal losing weight while still eating is a red flag — check for parasites, check for impaction, check the basking temperature.
  5. Do a physical exam. Look at the eyes (should be clear, not sunken), the mouth (no discharge), the nostrils (no bubbles), the vent (clean), and the shell (no soft patches, no pyramiding that distorts the measurement). Feel the abdomen behind the plastron — it should feel firm and rounded, not hollow.
  6. Get a faecal check. A vet can do a direct smear and a flotation for pinworms and protozoa. Pinworm burdens are common in wild-caught or badly quarantined animals and can tip a marginal animal into a bad winter.
  7. Decide, and write the decision down. This is the step most keepers skip. A note in the log — "brumate 2023, start 20 Nov, target 5-7 °C, review weekly" — forces you to be honest about why you decided what you did.

If the animal is going into an outdoor or shed hibernation box, the temperature the box sits at across the winter is the next parameter. Aim for a stable 4-8 °C at tortoise level, checked with a max-min thermometer, not the air temperature at the top of the shed. A box that swings from 2 °C to 12 °C across a week is more dangerous than a steady 6 °C, because every warm spell burns reserves and every cold snap risks frost damage to the eyes and extremities. An infrared thermometer gun is useful for spot-checking surface temperatures around the box and the animal's shell, but it is not a substitute for a probe left in the bedding.

The numbers that decide the winter

The table below is the one I use to set the plan each September. The Jackson ratio gives the pass/fail; the body mass trend and the physical exam give the confidence; the brumation duration gives the budget.

Life stage Straight carapace length Typical mass range Jackson ratio target Brumation plan
Hatchling (first winter) 30-45 mm 8-20 g Not reliable — do not use Do not brumate. Keep at 20-28 °C, feed and hydrate through winter.
Juvenile (1-3 years) 50-80 mm 40-100 g Not reliable — do not use Short brumation only if mass trend is stable: 4-6 weeks at 5-7 °C.
Subadult (3-6 years) 85-110 mm 120-280 g 0.21+ accepted by some clinicians 6-8 weeks at 4-7 °C, weekly weighing.
Adult female 120-160 mm 300-700 g 0.23+ 10-12 weeks at 4-8 °C. Check for eggs before boxing.

The 1 % per week figure is the rule I run the winter on. An adult at 500 g can afford to lose up to 5 g in a week; if it loses 8 g in a week, the box is too warm, the animal is waking and burning fuel, or both. Weigh weekly on the same scale and plot it — a steady decline is normal, a sudden step down is a reason to end the brumation early. The practical decision tree: if the animal has dropped more than 5 % of its starting mass by week eight, bring it out. If it has dropped more than 10 %, bring it out and get it to a vet.

Failure modes — what goes wrong when the numbers are wrong

The most common failure is brumating an animal that is not carrying enough reserve. A tortoise that comes out of brumation weighing 8 % less than it went in may look fine for two weeks, then go off food, become lethargic, and develop a runny nose. That runny nose is not a cold — it is a sign of stomatitis or a respiratory infection, and in a post-brumation animal it is very often the visible end of a process that started in the box, where the immune system was suppressed by the cold and an existing subclinical infection took hold.

The second failure is a brumation box that is too warm. Above about 10 °C, many tortoises will wake sporadically, urinate, and burn fat without eating. A Testudo that has urinated during brumation is in trouble — the bladder water is a reserve, and losing it leaves the animal dehydrated. If you find a wet bedding patch mid-winter, the animal has voided; bring it out, rehydrate it with shallow lukewarm soaks for 20 minutes a day for a week, and either restart the brumation with a corrected box or overwinter it at 20-25 °C and feed it. There is no shame in an overwinter — an animal kept awake and fed through the winter will often be healthier than one that was brumated badly.

The third failure is brumating an animal that is still carrying gut contents. An empty gut is a precondition: stop feeding 2-3 weeks before the target start date, keep the animal at normal temperatures and provide soaks, and wait for at least one clear, well-formed bowel movement in the final week before the box. A tortoise that goes into brumation with food in the gut will ferment it at low temperature, and the result can be a gut impaction or a fatal enteritis. I check the last bowel movement date in the log and I do not box an animal until I have seen it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use the Jackson ratio on a hatchling?

No. The formula assumes an adult body plan and mature bone density; hatchling and juvenile tortoises are still growing, and the ratio swings widely with normal growth. Judge juveniles on mass trend, physical exam and appetite, and do not brumate any animal under about 80 mm straight carapace length without veterinary advice.

My tortoise is 0.22 — is that safe to brumate?

Borderline. Some clinicians accept 0.21 in subadults for a short brumation, but at 0.22 in an adult I would overwinter it, feed it up through the winter at 20-25 °C, and aim for 0.25 or above by the following autumn. If you do brumate, keep the duration to 6-8 weeks and weigh weekly.

How much weight loss during brumation is normal?

A healthy adult losing 0.5-1 % of starting mass per week is normal; a 500 g animal losing 2.5-5 g in a week is expected. More than 1 % per week, or a total loss over 5 % by week eight, means bring the animal out, warm it slowly and get it drinking.
