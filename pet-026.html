Russian Tortoise Hibernation: The Weight Threshold That Decides Whether to Brumate

Brumation is not a calendar event, it is a metabolic budget. Below one ratio the animal has nothing to spend, and a fridge in November becomes the cause of death rather than the method.

A Russian tortoise coming out of the fridge at 4 °C has already spent four months running on a fat budget it cannot top up, and the number that decides whether it survives that is not the fridge temperature but the mass it carried in. Get the pre-brumation assessment wrong by 20 g on a 450 g animal and you are asking the liver to fund a winter it was never provisioned for. The Jackson ratio — mass in kg divided by straight-line carapace length in cm cubed, multiplied by 10 for readability — exists precisely because mass alone tells you nothing without length to anchor it.

The relationship matters because brumation is not sleep. It is a controlled shutdown: heart rate falls to single figures, gut motility stops, and the animal burns stored fat and, if it runs short, protein from muscle and liver. A tortoise that enters too light has no margin between "using reserves" and "catabolising organs". The assessment you do in October is the one that decides whether you have a brumation or a hospital case in March.

The one that went in at 380 g

November 2019. A male Testudo horsfieldii I had bought that August, wild-caught paperwork, straight-line carapace 12.4 cm, weighed on the kitchen scale at 380 g the week before I planned to cool him. I had read the Jackson ratio tables, run the numbers, and got a figure that sat just inside the "acceptable" band. What I had not done was check whether that 380 g was a real 380 g or a dehydrated shadow of one. He had been passing urates like chalk for a fortnight. I chilled him anyway.

He came out in mid-March at 349 g, eyes shut, and did not open them for four days. The vet's first question was not "what temperature did you brumate at" but "what did he weigh in July". I had no answer, because I had not weighed him in July. The 31 g he lost was survivable. The lesson cost me £180 in fluids and a subcutaneous vitamin A shot, and I have weighed every tortoise monthly from June onward ever since. The October number is worthless if you have no trend behind it.

Measuring the animal properly

Before any threshold means anything, the inputs have to be real. Straight-line carapace length, in cm, measured with the tortoise flat against a wall, vernier jaws touching the nuchal scute at the front and the supracaudal at the rear — not the curve of the shell, not over the top. Mass in grams, on a scale that reads to 0.1 g, taken at the same time of day, ideally after the morning void, and consistently. A tortoise that has just eaten a fistful of dandelion can carry 15 g of food and water it will lose overnight.

Weigh weekly from August, not just once before cooling. The trend is the assessment. A steady plateau through September is what you want; a decline of more than 1 % of body mass per week with no obvious cause means something else is happening and the animal should not be cooled at all. The Jackson ratio figure itself is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. Clinical consensus, including the approach taught at exotic-animal teaching hospitals, is that a ratio sitting in the nominal safe band on an animal that is otherwise unwell buys you nothing. Use it as one gate among several.

The thresholds, and what each one is for

The Jackson ratio bands most keepers work from run roughly 0.19–0.21 for a healthy adult heading into brumation, with 0.17 treated as the floor for a "reasonable" animal and anything below 0.16 flagged as do not brumate. These are not gospel. They were derived on Mediterranean testudines and applied to Horsfieldii by extension, and a heavy, broad female can sit at 0.22 without being obese while a long, lean male sits at 0.18 without being thin. That is why you pair the ratio with a physical check.

Physical check, in order, hands on the animal:

  • Weigh, then palpate the inguinal fossae either side of the tail base — the fat pads should be present but not bulging tight.
  • Look at the eyes: sunken or dull means dehydration, and the fix is soaking in shallow water at 25-28 °C for thirty minutes daily for a week, weighing before and after, not chilling.
  • Check the mouth and nares for discharge; any respiratory sign is an absolute stop, not a caution.
  • Observe feeding — a tortoise that has stopped eating for a fortnight before planned cooling has not "naturally fasted", it is unwell or too cool, and needs investigating.
  • Feel the shell: soft or pliable plastron in an adult is not normal and points at metabolic bone disease, a separate problem that also rules out brumation.

The absolute numbers I now use as gates, in plain terms:

  1. Adults below 0.16 Jackson ratio: do not brumate, overwinter warm and feed through.
  2. Adults 0.16–0.18: brumate only if the trend is stable and the physical check is clean, and shorten the cooling to 8–10 weeks rather than the full 16.
  3. Adults 0.18–0.21: full brumation, 12–16 weeks depending on your setup.
  4. Any animal with discharge, weight loss still ongoing, or a soft shell: no brumation regardless of ratio.

Juveniles are a different animal entirely. Under about three years or under roughly 9 cm straight-line carapace, the mass margins are too thin to gamble on a multi-month fast — the safe move is to keep them warm and growing through their first two or three winters and start considering brumation once they are consistently above 0.17 with a stable September plateau. Subadults in the 9–11 cm range can be cooled but I shorten it to eight weeks and weigh fortnightly through the winter, which means disturbing them — worth it.

Temperature, the actual stakes, and the first winter

Brumation temperature is 4–7 °C, held steady, in a dark fridge or a cool outbuilding with a max-min thermometer you check daily. Not 10 °C — that is warm enough for the animal to burn reserves while doing nothing. Not 2 °C, which risks gut contents freezing and necrosing. The fridge needs a second thermometer on the shelf with the tortoise, because fridge thermostats lie by several degrees.

The substrate in the brumation box — I use dampened coir, not soil — should hold at 50–60 % relative humidity and no wetter, because a wet carapace at 5 °C is how you get shell rot by February. A cheap infrared thermometer gun lets you check the surface temp of the coir and the tortoise's shell without opening the box repeatedly.

The failure mode when the weight threshold is ignored is not dramatic weight loss — that is the visible part. It is hepatic lipidosis: the liver mobilises fat faster than it can process it, fat accumulates in the hepatocytes, and the animal comes out in spring dull, jaundiced, and often too far gone for a £180 bag of fluids. This is the reason the pre-brumation assessment exists. It is also the reason the physical check matters as much as the ratio — a tortoise at 0.19 with a mouth full of discharge will die in a fridge faster than a chronically thin animal kept warm.

Frequently asked questions

Can I brumate a slightly underweight tortoise if I shorten the winter?

No. A shortened brumation on an underweight animal still asks the liver to mobilise reserves it does not have. Keep it warm, feed it through the winter, and reassess in the spring. A tortoise that misses one brumation is not harmed; one that dies in the fridge because you gambled on a shorter window is a different outcome.

Does the Jackson ratio work the same way on all Russian tortoises?

Roughly, with caution. It was derived on Mediterranean species and applied to Testudo horsfieldii by extension, and shell shape varies between individuals and bloodlines. Broad females can sit above the nominal band without being fat, and lean long males below it without being thin. Treat the ratio as one gate and let palpation and the weight trend decide the rest.

My tortoise is 0.20 but stopped eating in September. Should I still cool it?

Stop and investigate first. A healthy tortoise at 0.20 that stops eating is usually too cool or unwell — check the basking spot at 30–32 °C and the cool end around 20–22 °C, then look for discharge or soft shell. Never cool an animal that is already not eating; once it is in the fridge you cannot tell refusal from illness.
