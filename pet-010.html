Hedgehog Torpor, Obesity and the Calcium-to-Phosphorus Ratio That Causes Both

Torpor, obesity and a lopsided calcium-to-phosphorus ratio are the same welfare problem caught at three different stages.

An African pygmy hedgehog's core body temperature sits around 35–37 °C while it is awake and active, and that is a narrow ledge. Drop the ambient temperature below about 18–20 °C for more than a night or two — or drop the animal's blood glucose far enough — and the thermoregulatory set-point itself moves downward. That is torpor: not sleep, not "being quiet," but a controlled physiological retreat in which heart rate can fall from a resting 200-plus beats per minute to under 50 and body temperature approaches room temperature. In Atelerix albiventris it is a genuine survival tool in the wild and a genuine killer in a centrally heated flat with a cold spare room. The old hedgehog literature calls it Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome-adjacent collapse or, more accurately, "post-hibernation syndrome"; keepers just say the hog went cold.

The night I put a scale under a cold hedgehog

The animal was an adult female, two and a half years old, 412 g on a Tuesday and 389 g on the Friday. Nothing had changed in her bowl. I had moved her from the living room to the box room the previous weekend because a guest was staying, and the box room sits against an unheated north wall. At 9:40 pm she was out of the hide but walking as though the floor were soft — slow, high-stepping, one leg lagging. I put a thermometer on the shelf beside the vivarium: 16.8 °C. The vivarium's own probe, taped to the back wall, read 21 °C, which is exactly why I had stopped trusting wall probes two winters before. I warmed her in a carrier on a towel over a 30 °C heat mat, took her to the out-of-hours vet at midnight, and paid for a blood glucose reading (2.1 mmol/L) that took four minutes and cost less than the taxi. She recovered in six days. The box room now has a thermostatic oil radiator and I have not trusted a single wall-mounted probe since.

The calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is not a hedgehog number — and that is the point

Here is where I have to be blunt about a number that gets passed around. The familiar 2:1 calcium-to-phosphorus (Ca:P) ratio that governs reptile and sugar glider feeding is not the axis on which hedgehog nutrition turns. Pygmy hedgehogs are insectivores and opportunistic scavengers by anatomy: a short gut, teeth built for crushing beetle carapace, and a mineral metabolism tuned to a diet of whole invertebrates and small vertebrates. Insect prey is itself roughly 1:1 to 1.5:1 calcium to phosphorus by dry matter, with a pinch of bone and shell in the mix, and the animal evolved to cope with that. What you cannot do, however, is feed a diet where phosphorus massively outstrips calcium — a bowl of cat kibble alone, a mealworm-heavy treat habit — and expect a growing juvenile or a lactating female to lay down bone or maintain serum calcium. Nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism (NSHP, in which low dietary calcium forces the parathyroid to mobilise calcium out of bone) is a recognised consequence of mineral imbalance across mammals, and hedgehogs are not exempt.

The working target most experienced keepers I know use is this: the base ration should land somewhere between 1:1 and 1.5:1 Ca:P, and you supplement downward from there if the animal is young, pregnant or recovering. What is genuinely contested is the exact figure — you will find hobby sheets quoting 1.5:1, 2:1, and "just use a good complete food." The evidence base for hedgehog mineral requirements specifically is thin; most of it is extrapolated from insectivore and small-carnivore work. So I state a range, keep a log, and watch the animal rather than chasing a decimal point.

What actually goes in the bowl

A pygmy hedgehog needs roughly 25–35 kcal of metabolisable energy per day at adult maintenance weight, and that figure scales with body condition, life stage and activity. For a 400–500 g adult that is comfortably met by two to three tablespoons of a high-quality complete diet (a hedgehog or high-protein cat kibble, 30 %+ crude protein, 10–15 % fat) split across an evening feed and a small top-up. Insects are enrichment, not the staple — and that is the opposite of what the internet tells new owners. A mealworm is roughly 0.5 g and mostly fat and phosphorus; a small handful of mealworms a night is a treat, not a diet.

The mineral correction is simple and cheap: dust insects with a calcium-with-D3 powder at roughly one small pinch per insect, and put a shallow water bowl in every enclosure — bowls, not bottles, because a hedgehog's snout does not do bottles well. Portion by weight, not by eye. A 0.1 g kitchen scale turns "she seems about the same" into "she lost 23 g in five days," which is the difference between catching a torpor event early and finding her cold on the seventh morning.

Life stage changes the numbers:

  • Juvenile (weaned to about 8 weeks): small meals twice daily, higher protein, liberal calcium dusting. Growth is the most calcium-demanding phase.
  • Subadult (8 weeks to about 6 months): once daily with a light second feed; monitor weight weekly against the growth curve you keep yourself, since published pygmy hedgehog curves are sparse.
  • Adult (6 months to about 4 years): evening feed, 25–35 kcal, insects 3–4 times a week. Watch for obesity — a healthy adult should look like a rugby ball, not a pear.
  • Senior (4 years plus): expect dental loss and a slower gut; softer texture, smaller portions, more frequent weighing.

Torpor, obesity and the same root problem

Here is the mechanism in one chain. A hedgehog that is overweight carries fat reserves that insulate poorly and, more importantly, signal a body that "can afford" a metabolic slowdown. Combine that with an ambient temperature drift below about 20 °C and a short, low-energy feeding window, and the animal's hypothalamic thermostat does not fight to hold 35 °C — it lets go. Heart rate drops, respiration drops to a few breaths a minute, and the gut stops moving. A torpid hedgehog that is warmed and fed within the first 24–48 hours usually recovers. Past that, the risk of aspiration (if you feed a cold, slow gut) and of multi-organ shutdown climbs fast. Obesity and torpor are not two problems; they are the same problem seen from two angles: a body that has lost the margin to defend its own temperature.

The temperatures to run are these. The warm end of the enclosure should sit at 24–26 °C, with a cool end you can walk away from at 20–22 °C. That gradient lets the animal choose. A digital probe on the warm end, a second on the cool end, both readable from the room, and no relying on the one that came taped to the vivarium. If your room heating fails in a winter power cut, a simple under-tank heat mat on a dimming thermostat, set to 24 °C, is the difference between a bad night and a vet bill.

When the animal will not eat, or has gone cold

The decision a keeper has to make on a cold hedgehog is not whether to warm it, but how fast. Do not plunge it into a hot vivarium. Put the animal in a carrier on a towel over a heat mat set to 30 °C, with a shallow water dish, and check every 30 minutes. Warm it gradually — roughly 1 °C per hour of body temperature, which is to say slowly enough that you cannot measure it by feel. Once it is warm and responsive, offer a small amount of food and note how much it takes; if it takes none and is warm, that is a vet visit, not a waiting game.

A hedgehog that is warm but refusing food for more than 24–36 hours in an adult, or any length of time in a juvenile, needs a vet. In my experience that refusal is far more often a low-grade infection or a blocked/overloaded gut than a fussy appetite. Keep the bowl weighed: the number in your log is data the vet can use.

A final word on the calcium question as it meets the refusal: a hedgehog on a cat-kibble base with no insects, no dusting, and no documented mineral intake is the profile where I would ask for a blood panel sooner rather than later. You do not need a perfect ratio; you need to know roughly what is going in and be able to show it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just feed a complete hedgehog kibble and skip insects entirely?

You can keep an adult alive and reasonably healthy on a good complete kibble alone if the mineral profile is sound. But you lose enrichment, dental wear and the behavioural feeding response, and you lose control over the Ca:P ratio because you cannot see it. Most experienced keepers run a kibble base plus dusted insects three or four times a week.

What is the lowest room temperature before torpor risk becomes real?

Below about 20 °C ambient, sustained for more than a night or two, is where I start watching closely. Below 18 °C is a genuine risk for adults and a serious one for juveniles and seniors. The enclosure's warm end should remain at 24–26 °C regardless of room temperature.

Does obesity really cause torpor, or is that just correlation?

It is a well-recognised pattern in hedgehog clinical practice, though the precise mechanism is not fully pinned down. The practical link is that overweight animals have less metabolic margin, and cold-plus-low-energy availability is the trigger. Manage weight with weekly weighings and portioned feeding, and keep the enclosure warm, and you address both at once.
