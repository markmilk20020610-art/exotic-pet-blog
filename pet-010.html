Hedgehog Torpor, Obesity and the Calcium-to-Phosphorus Ratio That Causes Both

Torpor, obesity and a lopsided calcium-to-phosphorus ratio are the same welfare problem caught at three different stages.

An African pygmy hedgehog (Atelerix albiventris) has a body temperature it cannot defend below roughly 20 °C, and its answer to a cold night is not to shiver but to shut down — a torpor state that drops metabolism by up to 80 %. That is a wild survival trick, and in a centrally heated flat it is almost always a husbandry accident. The numbers that keep it from happening are a cage held at 24–27 °C, an adult weight between 300 and 600 g, and a diet with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 1.5–2 : 1. Miss the third and you get the obesity and the wobbly gait that get misdiagnosed as wobbly hedgehog syndrome for months.

The hedgehog that went cold in a warm room

She came in at 240 g, small for an adult but not alarming, and the owner's complaint was that she had "gone quiet". I asked the one question that mattered — what temperature is that room at night — and got a shrug. The overnight reading in the spare bedroom was 17 °C. She had torpored four nights running, and every time she came out of it she ate less. By the time I weighed her she was 198 g, the spines along her flank were sparse, and she swayed when she walked. The owner had already read about wobbly hedgehog syndrome and had convinced herself of the worst. I told her it was not WHS; it was four cold nights, a bowl of mealworms and a scale nobody owned. We moved the cage into a 25 °C room, I cut the mealworms down to a garnish, and she was 372 g five months later. The sway went with the weight.

Why repeated torpor is not free

Torpor is a controlled metabolic depression: heart rate falls from around 250 beats per minute to under 20, core temperature tracks ambient, and the immune system largely stops. Coming out of it costs a large burst of thermogenesis, which is paid for in brown adipose tissue and, in a captive animal with limited reserves, in muscle. Repeated arousal cycles — which is what happens when a hedgehog torpors every night and is woken every morning by a warm room — deplete glycogen, suppress immune function and are associated with fatty liver change. The animal does not die of cold. It dies of the metabolic debt of going in and out of it.

The dietary half is separate and additive. Mealworms and waxworms are the two things every new keeper feeds and the two worst things to feed freely: their calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is around 0.1 : 1, which means they actively pull calcium out of the body to be metabolised. A diet anchored on them produces a growing animal with soft bones, tremors and a swaying gait — nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, the same mechanism as metabolic bone disease in reptiles. It looks like a neurological disease because it is one, downstream.

The dusting instruction is where most keepers go wrong in the other direction. Calcium with D3 should be applied so the insect looks lightly frosted, roughly 0.1 g per mealworm, and the supplement should be a reptile-grade powder with a known calcium content rather than a crushed human tablet. Over-supplementation causes its own problems — soft tissue calcification, constipation — which is why the ratio matters more than the absolute amount.

The weekly routine that catches problems early

  1. Weigh every Sunday, same time, before food. A 5 % change in a month is noise; 10 % is a veterinary appointment.
  2. Hold the room at 24–27 °C overnight. A thermostat on a heat mat under a third of the cage, plus a thermometer at hedgehog height, not at the top of the vivarium.
  3. Feed once, at dusk. Hedgehogs are crepuscular; a bowl left all day is a bowl the animal grazes on and a weight you cannot interpret.
  4. Track the wheel. A hedgehog that stops running is a hedgehog that is either cold, ill or obese, and the wheel is the earliest signal you will get.
  5. Dust, do not drench. Every insect meal gets calcium powder, and no meal is insects alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is torpor the same as hibernation?

No. Hibernation is a planned, seasonal, multi-week state; torpor is an emergency short-term shutdown that an African pygmy hedgehog should never need in captivity. This species does not hibernate in the wild, and a captive one that goes cold is a husbandry failure, not a natural cycle.

My hedgehog is 780 g. Is that obese?

Almost certainly, unless it is a very large male at the top of the range. Above 700 g the risks are fatty liver, arthritis and difficulty self-anointing. Cut the insect component out entirely, reduce the kibble by a fifth, and increase out-of-cage time before changing anything else.

Can I use a reptile calcium supplement?

Yes, and it is usually the better choice, because reptile powders are formulated for an animal that also needs D3 and are dosed by weight. Avoid anything with added phosphorus — the point of the supplement is to correct a ratio, and a calcium-plus-phosphorus product does not move it.
