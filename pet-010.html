Hedgehog Torpor, Obesity and the Calcium-to-Phosphorus Ratio That Causes Both

Torpor, obesity and a lopsided calcium-to-phosphorus ratio are the same welfare problem caught at three different stages.

An African pygmy hedgehog's active core body temperature sits near 35 °C, and its digestion is built around holding it. Let the ambient warm end fall below about 22 °C for several nights and the gut slows, the appetite follows, and the animal starts down the road to torpor — a controlled metabolic drop that is normal in the wild and lethal in a vivarium. The calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the bowl decides whether that slide is a stumble or a bone problem.

The night the scale told me first

My four-year-old female had never refused a meal. Last November I moved her vivarium to redecorate and left the heat mat on the same dimming thermostat setting, assuming nothing had changed. Three nights later she weighed 218 g, down from 342 g in a fortnight. Still eating — just slowly, curled on the cool side, flank cool to the back of my hand. The thermostat read 24 °C at the warm end. I had not probed it since the move. She climbed back to 330 g over four weeks once the gradient was right. The animal had been telling me for days; I had been reading the food bowl instead of the hedgehog.

Why torpor is a nutrition problem too

The mechanism explains why diet and temperature are one conversation. An African pygmy hedgehog (Atelerix albiventris) has a short gut and limited capacity to sustain a cold challenge the way a true hibernator does. When ambient temperature falls below what it can compensate for — in my experience below 22 °C at the warm end, though published figures vary and I would not defend a hard line — metabolic rate drops, gut motility slows, and torpor begins. In torpor the body's calcium demand shifts, and an animal already drawing on skeletal reserves has nothing in the bank.

The second piece is the ratio. Insects are heavily phosphorus-biased; a mealworm runs roughly 1:10 calcium to phosphorus or worse. On that diet the hedgehog cannot absorb enough calcium, parathyroid hormone rises, and bone is resorbed to keep serum calcium stable. In a juvenile that shows as rubbery limbs; in an adult, fractures and an animal that cannot roll. Keepers generally aim for around 2:1. I have seen 1.5:1 defended, and the honest answer is that the exact target is unsettled in the hobby literature — but anything below 1:1 is a problem. Most complete hedgehog foods sit between 1.2:1 and 2:1, which is why I read the guaranteed analysis rather than the front of the bag.

Running the feeding regime

Adults: one evening meal of complete hedgehog kibble as the base, insects as a supplement rather than the main event — roughly 1-2 tablespoons of kibble for a 350-450 g animal, insects dusted with calcium. Weigh weekly on a 0.1 g scale; it catches trouble about a week before the animal shows it. Juveniles and subadults need the same ratio, more per body weight, and two smaller feeds a day under about 250 g, because they are laying down bone at a rate adults are not. That is where most deficiencies I have seen actually start.

Temperature, gradient and the quiet thermostat failure

Run a warm end of 26-30 °C, a cool end of 22-24 °C, and a photoperiod of 12-14 hours. A heat mat under one half of the floor goes on a dimming thermostat with the probe taped to the floor on the warm side, not floating in the air. Check that probe against a second thermometer monthly — a failed thermostat still displays a number, and it will be wrong.

A hedgehog that is cool to the touch, slow to uncurl and off food is a veterinary case, not a thermostat tweak. Torpor in captivity can kill within days, and warming is done under veterinary guidance, not with a hot water bottle. The failure mode is undramatic: a quieter animal, eating less, losing weight over a week while you assume it is sulking.

Enclosure as a small system

The floor is what most keepers get wrong. I stopped using loose wood shavings in 2019 after a respiratory episode that cost £200 in diagnostics; I now run a solid liner with a shallow dust-free dig box in one corner, changed weekly — enrichment, not hygiene. Ventilation matters as much as heat, because a warm humid box is a bacterial incubator. Keep a hygrometer on the cool side at 40-50 % relative humidity.

Frequently asked questions

Can I feed only mealworms?

No. Mealworms are strongly phosphorus-biased and drag the ratio below 1:1 if they dominate. Use a complete hedgehog kibble as the base, keep insects as a dusted extra, and read the guaranteed analysis. An all-insect diet is one of the commonest routes to deficiency in young animals.

How cold is unsafe?

Below about 22 °C at the warm end I worry; below 20 °C for more than a night I treat as an emergency. A short spell the animal can compensate for, but a sustained drop pushes it toward torpor. Check with a probe, never the thermostat display.

How often should I weigh an adult?

Weekly, on a 0.1 g scale, same time of day before feeding. A drop of more than about 10 % over two or three weeks warrants a vet visit. Weight catches torpor, dental problems and obesity before they show in behaviour, and it takes thirty seconds.
