Giant African Land Snail Shell Growth: Calcium, pH and the Rings That Record Both

A snail cannot repair a thin shell, only add to it. Every ridge on the whorl is a fortnight of whatever calcium the keeper happened to supply.

A giant African land snail's shell is roughly 98 % calcium carbonate by dry weight, and every gram of it has to come out of the environment — from food, from cuttlebone, from the substrate itself. A juvenile Lissachatina fulica adding 2-4 mm of new lip per week at 24-26 °C is pulling mineral faster than a gut can supply it from lettuce, which is why the whorl either thickens or it doesn't. There is no reabsorption, no repair of a cracked apex, no second attempt at a thin growth ring. What gets laid down stays laid down, and it records exactly what you gave the animal that week.

The night I found the flaking lip

November 2019, a two-year-old fulica in a 60 × 45 × 45 cm tub. I'd been running her on coco fibre straight out of the block, damp, no cuttlebone because she'd ignored the last piece and I'd taken it as a preference rather than a symptom. Her shell measured 11 cm when I got her. Three months later the new lip — the freshest few millimetres of growth at the aperture, still pale and soft — had gone chalky and was curling outward instead of thickening inward.

I weighed her on the kitchen scale: 84 g, down from 91 g. The substrate pH strip read about 5. That was the whole story. Coco fibre is acidic out of the block, I'd been flushing calcium out of her gut with every wet feed, and she'd been spending calcium on shell before digestion. I switched substrate, added cuttlebone grated over the food rather than sitting on top, and gave her eight weeks. The lip thickened. The bad ring never went away.

What the shell actually is and how it grows

Snail shell is laid down by the mantle, the fleshy skirt of tissue that sits against the inside of the whorl. The mantle secretes three layers: an outer proteinaceous periostracum that gives the brown banding, a middle prismatic layer of calcite crystals, and an inner nacreous layer. Growth happens at the aperture, where the mantle edge extends and calcifies a new strip. That strip is the growth ring you can see, and it is a hard record of the conditions that week: wide and pale when calcium and warmth are plenty, narrow and dark when they aren't.

Calcium comes in through the gut and travels in the haemolymph — snail blood — to the mantle. The critical part most keepers miss: calcium absorption needs an acidic gut, but shell deposition needs a neutral-to-alkaline environment. That's not a contradiction; it's a two-stage process. The stomach is acidic enough to dissolve calcium carbonate from cuttlebone or limestone. The mantle, receiving that calcium, works best when the whole animal isn't sitting in an acidic bath that leaches mineral back out through the skin and the substrate interface. Substrate pH below about 6.5 shifts the balance against deposition.

So the husbandry question is not just "are you giving calcium". It's "is the calcium arriving, and is the environment letting the mantle keep it".

Setting calcium, temperature and pH: the numbers I actually run

These are the ranges I've landed on across a decade of fulica and a few years of sturdier archachatina work. They are not a single published protocol — snail husbandry evidence is thin and much of what circulates is inherited from agricultural pest management rather than pet care. Treat them as starting points to measure against, not gospel.

Temperature: 22-26 °C (72-79 °F) is the productive band. Below 20 °C the animal eats less, and calcium intake falls with appetite before shell growth notices. Above 28 °C you get stress, retraction and shell erosion from accelerated metabolism without matching intake. A cheap digital thermometer in the substrate and one on the lid is the cheapest diagnostic you'll ever buy.

Humidity: 70-90 % relative humidity keeps the mantle moist and the periostracum intact. Dry air cracks the periostracum first — that's the dull, flaky look on older shell — and once it's gone the calcite underneath is exposed to the acid in the air and substrate.

Substrate: coco fibre or a coco/coir mix, 8-12 cm deep, damp enough to clump in your fist but not dripping. The pH matters. Coco fibre out of the block often runs 4.5-6.0. Buffer it up to 7.0-7.5 by mixing in a handful of crushed oyster shell or agricultural lime per litre of substrate — you're not sterilising the pH, you're keeping it from leaching calcium out of the animal.

The piece of gear I won't run a snail tub without is a pair of probes you can leave in place — one in the substrate, one at the surface — so you're not guessing at gradients by hand.

Substrate recipe I use, in order:

  1. Rehydrate a compressed coco block to roughly 3× its dry volume with dechlorinated water.
  2. Mix in one handful (about 20 g) of crushed oyster shell or garden lime per 5 litres of hydrated fibre.
  3. Let it stand 24 hours, then check the pH with a soil strip at three depths. Aim for 7.0-7.5.
  4. Add a drainage layer — 2 cm of coarse gravel or leca — under 8-12 cm of the mix if your tub has no false bottom. Fulica drowns in standing water as readily as it desiccates in dry air.
  5. Top with a layer of dead leaves and a cuttlebone laid flat. The cuttlebone goes in whole and stays until it's gnawed to nothing; grating it onto the food is a supplement, not a substitute.

Calcium sources ranked by what actually gets used

Cuttlebone is the standard, and deservedly so — around 85 % calcium carbonate, rough enough to rasp and hard enough to last a week in a humid tub. Limestone flour mixed into the food works, but it cakes in humidity; you'll want to refresh it. Eggshell is calcium carbonate too, but the membrane and protein slow uptake, so it's a backup. Spinach, chard and beet greens are high in oxalates that bind calcium in the gut and push it out the other end — a snail raised on spinach can look well-fed and still be calcium-deficient. Calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the diet is less well studied in gastropods than in reptiles, but the principle holds: aim for calcium well in excess of phosphorus, and don't build the diet on high-phosphorus produce.

Preferred feeding: a base of sweet potato, courgette and a little leafy green, with cuttlebone always available, and a pinch of grated cuttlebone or limestone dust over the wet food two or three times a week. Fresh food every 24-48 hours, removed before it moulds.

Reading the ring: what a keeper can actually see

You don't need a lab. You need a ruler, a scale and a photo every fortnight. A healthy juvenile adds growth at the aperture at roughly 1-3 mm a week in a well-run tub. If that slows, check in this order:

  • Temperature — has the room cooled, or has the heat mat stopped?
  • Food intake — is the animal actually eating, or just sitting on the food?
  • Calcium availability — is the cuttlebone fresh and rough, or has it gone smooth and inert?
  • Substrate pH — strip it once a month; the buffer depletes.

The ring itself tells you which failure you're looking at. A pale, wide ring means growth happened fast — usually good, occasionally too fast off a poor diet, which produces a thin but large shell. A dark, narrow ring means growth slowed. A ring that looks chalky or porous on the surface means mineral was deposited but not densely — that's usually a pH problem or a calcium source that wasn't being absorbed.

None of this is reversible. The shell you build in the first year is the animal's spine for the rest of its life. I'd rather over-buffer a substrate than under-feed calcium, and I'll say that plainly even though the literature doesn't settle the optimal pH band.

Frequently asked questions

Can a snail repair a cracked or thinning shell?

It can seal a crack with new shell material, but it cannot reabsorb or rebuild what's gone. A thinned shell from a calcium-poor season stays thin at that ring. What you can do is stop the loss and rebuild from the aperture forward: fix substrate pH, keep cuttlebone constantly available, and measure growth at the lip weekly.

Is cuttlebone enough, or do I need a powdered supplement?

Whole cuttlebone should always be present — it's the staple. Grated cuttlebone or limestone dust on wet food two or three times a week covers juveniles and fast-growing subadults. Adults on a stable diet often gnaw the cuttlebone and ignore the dust, which is fine. Skip calcium-dusting the substrate; it feeds mould, not the snail.

My substrate read pH 5 out of the block. Is that dangerous?

It's the most common cause of slow growth in new tubs. Coco fibre runs acidic. Mix in crushed oyster shell or agricultural lime — roughly one handful per 5 litres — let it stand a day, then re-strip. Target 7.0-7.5. Below 6.5 the substrate leaches mineral rather than supplying it, and no amount of cuttlebone will fully compensate.
