Leachianus Gecko Enclosure Size: Why the Minimum Is Bigger Than You Think

The largest gecko in the world does not fit in the smallest vivarium on the shelf. Here is the floor space it actually uses.

An adult Rhacodactylus leachianus needs a thermal gradient rather than a hot spot: roughly 24-26 °C at the warm end, 20-22 °C at the cool end, and a daytime humidity of 70-80 %. Get that band right and digestion, ecdysis and appetite run inside it. Get it wrong at the warm end and you have a gecko sitting flat on the glass, grey as wet cardboard, refusing the feeder you were counting on.

One evening, a 380 g gecko and a thermostat set too warm

February, 11:40 pm, and the male on my top shelf — a Grande Terre import I've had seven years, thick as my wrist — was pressed flat against the front glass, chin down, colour gone. That posture reads as "too hot", not "hungry". I'd nudged his thermostat to 27 °C three days earlier on the logic that warmer means more active. Wrong logic. He was trying to escape a warm end behaving like a warm end for a different species.

I rechecked the probe against a second thermometer and found the shelf he actually sat on reading 28.5 °C — the thermostat probe was sampling air 15 cm below the lamp, not the surface. That night cost me a bruise (380 g of objection is memorable) and an hour on my knees recalibrating. Measure the surface the animal lives on, not the air above it.

The housing numbers that actually matter

Leachianus is a big, heavy-bodied New Caledonian gecko, and the familiar 45 × 45 × 60 cm glass box is a floor for a subadult and a compromise for an adult. My adult male lives in 60 × 45 × 90 cm and uses all of it. Footprint matters more than height — these are trunk-and-branch animals, not canopy acrobats.

  • Warm end: 24-26 °C, never sustained above 27 °C.
  • Cool end: 20-22 °C, a genuine 4-6 °C gradient across the enclosure.
  • Night drop: 18-20 °C is fine and, in my experience, beneficial.
  • Humidity: 70-80 % daytime, drying to 60-65 % overnight.
  • Photoperiod: 12 hours on, 12 off year-round.

I don't use a basking lamp for adults. A low-wattage heat mat under a third of the footprint, thermostatted, gives a warm spot with far less dry-out risk. The surface it heats should never exceed 26 °C, measured with an infrared gun.

Setting it up: the physical system

  1. Seal and cure the enclosure; drill drainage if it's planted. Standing water under 10 cm of substrate grows mould.
  2. Lay 2-3 cm of drainage medium, a barrier, then 8-12 cm of coco-fibre substrate. One compressed block rehydrates to two or three times its dry volume and comfortably beds a 60 × 45 cm floor.
  3. Fit horizontal cork rounds at floor, mid-tank and one high shelf. Bare glass reads as exposure.
  4. Set the thermostat probe on the warm-end substrate, then calibrate it against a second thermometer before trusting it. That's the mistake above.
  5. Mist mornings to 75-80 % and evenings, letting it fall to 60-65 % between. A hygrometer at each end tells you whether you're hitting it.

Substrate is what keepers underestimate. A heavy gecko compacts wet coco fibre into an anaerobic brick within weeks. I turn the top 2 cm fortnightly and replace the whole bed every three to four months, sooner if I smell ammonia at the base.

Life stages and what changes

Juveniles aren't small adults. A hatchling at 8-10 cm total length does best in a 30 × 30 × 45 cm enclosure — go bigger and it struggles to find food and water, and I've watched juveniles go thin in oversized tanks. I move to the full footprint at around 18-20 months and 120-150 g, once the animal eats reliably and is gaining.

Juveniles get a complete gecko diet every second day, with a small insect offering weekly if taken. Adults get the same diet three times a week, weighed monthly; a loss over about 5 % body weight in a month gets a look at temperature, hydration and a vet.

The clean-up crew, drainage and reading the animal

A planted, bioactive enclosure solves the humidity problem from the inside. Springtails and dwarf isopods in the drainage layer break down faeces and uneaten diet before mould takes hold, and plants transpire to keep the daytime band up without misting yourself into a swamp. The trade: keep the crew alive. They want a damp drainage layer and leaf litter, and if the surface dries out too long, the isopods retreat and waste sits.

I stopped using loose substrate for juveniles in 2019, after a subadult took a mouthful of wet coco lunging at a cricket. It passed without incident, but I wasn't waiting for the second time. Juveniles get a solid, disinfectable base with paper towel and cork bark until they take food off a dish reliably; subadults and adults get the deep bed.

Most worries are behavioural. A gecko pressed flat with colour drained is uncomfortable — usually too warm, occasionally too dry. One hanging head-down at the front wall for hours is hunting the gradient. One that stops feeding and flicks its tail slowly is stressed and wants a few days alone. The colour-flash of a fired-up adult is a stress display, not a mood. When I see any of it, I check both hygrometers before anything else; nine times in ten a reading has drifted.

Frequently asked questions

Can two leachianus share an adult enclosure?

No, and I won't advocate it. Adults are territorial and the mass involved makes any fight costly. Even experienced breeders separate pairs outside a short, supervised pairing. Housing singly is the safest default for a keeper, and it's what I run.

What's the absolute minimum enclosure for an adult?

45 × 45 × 60 cm is the floor most keepers recognise, but an adult that never leaves it will be cramped. Treat that as a subadult size and give an adult 60 × 45 × 90 cm if space allows. Larger is better for this species; smaller is an act of faith.

Does a leachianus need UVB?

The evidence is genuinely thin. Many keepers run without UVB and rely on a complete gecko diet for vitamin D, and that works for most animals. I offer a low-output UVB tube for a few hours a day in summer, mostly out of caution, but the diet matters more than the lamp.
