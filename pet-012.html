Leachianus Gecko Enclosure Size: Why the Minimum Is Bigger Than You Think

The largest gecko in the world does not fit in the smallest vivarium on the shelf. Here is the floor space it actually uses.

NF
Nell Farraday

An adult Rhacodactylus leachianus wants a thermal gradient of roughly 72-79 °F (22-26 °C) across the whole footprint, and — this is the bit that surprises people — a footprint big enough that the gradient actually exists. In a small box you get one temperature, not a gradient. Give a 12-inch juvenile something the size of a 45 × 45 × 45 cm cube and it will use every corner of it within a week. The measurement that matters is not the wall height. It is how far the animal can travel from the warm corner to the cool one without meeting its own shed skin twice.

The night the bigger cage went back on the floor

I built a 60 × 45 × 90 cm enclosure for an eight-year-old female and moved her in on a Tuesday. By Thursday her weight was down four grams — noise in a gecko this size, but I checked the probe and the warm corner was reading 81 °F, two degrees over. She had stopped using that half of the tank entirely and was wedged behind a cork round in the bottom-left corner, which was sitting at 71 °F and humid. I spent an evening swapping the thermostat for a dimming one, moved the heat panel higher, and let it settle for 48 hours before I touched her again. Friday feeding: two insects and a lick of paste, which is not her usual. Saturday she did nothing. Sunday morning she was on the warm perch with her chin up and her abdomen still plump. Nothing was wrong with her. The tank was wrong and she was telling me. I had built for the footprint and forgotten that the vertical drop from the basking shelf to the leaf litter is part of the gradient.

Enclosure size: the working minimums

The community figure that gets quoted is 45 × 45 × 60 cm minimum for an adult, and I think that is a floor, not a target. Grand Terre locale animals and large males will make a 45 × 45 × 60 cm enclosure look cramped within a season, mostly because they use the glass as a climbing surface and end up walking a groove in the same three square inches. My working minimum for a lone adult is 60 × 45 × 75 cm, and for a proven, large female I go to 90 cm in the longest dimension. A juvenile under about six months runs smaller — 30 × 30 × 45 cm is plenty — because a young animal in an oversized box with poor feeder recovery will lose weight before you notice.

Two things drive the number: mass and the gradient. A 250 g adult dropped into a 45 cm cube cannot find a cool end more than 40 cm from the heat source, and that is not far enough. The old rough rule people parrot — roughly one square inch of floor per gram of body weight — is not a husbandry standard, it is hobby shorthand, and I have seen it produce enclosures far too small for large males. Treat it as a starting sanity check and trust the gradient.

The thermal and humidity envelope

Leachianus do not want a hot basking spot. They want a warm-not-hot corner, a cool end, and a humidity cycle that swings between wet and dry. I set a dimming thermostat to a probe on the warm perch and I watch a second probe in the cool bottom corner. The gap between those two readings is the real instrument. There is no published thermal preference study for this species that I would stake a vet bill on; the numbers below are consensus from long-term keepers, and I will say plainly that they are consensus, not a paper.

Above 82 °F they go flat, stop feeding and start breathing through the mouth, which is a heat-stress sign and not normal. I have seen this on a hot week in a south-facing room with no air conditioning, the thermostat reading fine because the probe was on the wrong side of the tank. The fix was a second thermostat and a fan on the cool end. The £200 vet visit was for a gecko that had simply been too warm for four days, and the cause was a thermostat I trusted without ever checking it against a thermometer.

Setting up the gradient in practice

  1. Place the heat source, then measure, then place furniture. Mount the heat panel or pad at one top corner, run it through a dimming thermostat, and leave the tank empty of decor for 24 hours with a thermometer at each corner. If the warm-to-cool gap is less than 4 °F, the enclosure is too small or the room too uniform.
  2. Add vertical structure so the gecko can choose by height. Cork rounds, a hollow log and a solid vertical branch partition the tank into microclimates. A gecko three inches off the ground is in a wetter, cooler band than one at the top perch.
  3. Cycle humidity, do not soak it. One heavy mist in the evening so the substrate reads wet to the touch, then let it dry to 55-60 % by mid-afternoon. Standing humidity above 80 % for days on end, without airflow, is how you get skin problems and a tank that smells.
  4. Provide a clean water bowl and a second one for soaking if the animal shows interest; some do, some ignore it entirely.
  5. Feed in the enclosure, remove leftovers within 12-18 hours. Leachianus are largely frugivorous and take a paste diet, with insects offered sparingly. The first time you portion a paste mix, weigh it — 10-15 g is a reasonable adult meal and overfeeding produces a fat animal that still looks healthy.

What happens when the housing is wrong

A single temperature with no gradient produces a chronic, low-grade stress the animal cannot escape by behaviour — there is no cool corner to walk to. The first signs are appetite loss and long motionless periods in one spot, then a slow weight drift you will only catch if you weigh weekly. In a cramped enclosure you get the same thing for a different reason: the gecko runs out of novel surfaces, spends more time on the glass, and rubs its chin. In a tank that is too warm, the failure is faster — open-mouthed breathing, lethargy, refusal to eat — and it needs to be fixed within a day, not a fortnight. Housing problems do not announce themselves. They look, from the outside, exactly like a bored animal.

One more thing worth checking weekly: shed. A leachianus shedding in one piece on paper towel in a dry tank is normal. One shedding in patches, with a ring left around a toe, is a humidity problem, and that is where a second hygrometer earns its place.

Frequently asked questions

Is 45 × 45 × 60 cm really the minimum for an adult leachianus?

It is the accepted community floor, and it will keep a small adult alive. It will not let a large male or a big female find a genuine cool end, and most keepers who start at that size move up within a year. I would treat 60 × 45 × 75 cm as the practical minimum for a leachianus you intend to keep for twenty years.

Can I keep two leachianus together in one large enclosure?

Breeding pairs are sometimes housed together for short periods, but long-term cohabitation is not something I recommend or do. Two large geckos in one footprint means one dominates the warm corner and the other loses weight quietly. If you must pair them, do it in a footprint at least double the single-animal minimum and weigh both weekly.

Does the enclosure need a heat lamp, or is room temperature enough?

Most centrally heated rooms sit at 68-72 °F, which is a few degrees cool for a leachianus to digest and behave normally. A dimming thermostat on a heat panel, sized to keep one corner at 75-78 °F, is enough. Skip the basking bulb; this species does not want a hot spot and will avoid it.
