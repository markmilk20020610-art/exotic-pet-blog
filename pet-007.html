Why Your Corn Snake Refuses Food in Winter — and When It Is Not Brumation

A four-month winter fast is normal biology. Losing a tenth of the body weight is not — here is how to tell them apart before you intervene.

A corn snake's gut is a chemical reactor with a narrow operating window: below about 18 °C (65 °F), digestive enzymes slow to the point where a meal sits undigested, and the animal usually stops taking prey before that happens. Winter in a centrally heated house makes this stranger, not simpler, because the warm end can sit at 30 °C (86 °F) while the floor of the vivarium drops to 17 °C overnight. Pantherophis guttatus reads a shrinking thermal gradient in October and often refuses food for eight weeks before the keeper has changed anything.

The night I wasted £140 on a thermostat

November 2021, half past ten at night, and I had a subadult male on the kitchen counter with a refused mouse in a bag and a growing conviction he was ill. He'd eaten reliably all summer. Now he turned his head away from a warm pinkie with the polite contempt only colubrids manage. I panicked, ordered a new dimming thermostat and a replacement heat mat, and booked a vet visit. The vet asked two questions: what's your cool-end temperature at 2 a.m., and what's your light timer set to? I couldn't answer either. He was brumating. He was fine. The thermostat was £140 I didn't need to spend, and the refund went on an infrared thermometer that told me the truth about my own floor.

What winter feeding actually is

Two strategies exist and both are legitimate. The first is to keep the snake entirely out of brumation: hold the thermal gradient steady, keep photoperiod at 12 hours, and continue feeding through the winter. The second is to let brumation happen deliberately. Where keepers go wrong is landing in the middle — a warm room, a cold floor, a shortened day and a snake that has gone off food without any physiological reason to stop.

Brumation is not hibernation. The animal is awake, just running at a lower idle. Core body temperature sits in the low teens Celsius, and a gut full of food at that temperature will putrefy rather than digest. That is why the rule is absolute: empty gut before cooling. Give the snake 14-18 days at normal temperatures after its last meal before you begin dropping the gradient, so the meal has fully passed. If you cannot commit to that gap, do not cool the animal.

For a healthy adult of good body weight, a brumation window of 8-12 weeks at 12-15 °C (54-59 °F) is what I run. Juveniles and any snake under about 30 g stay warm, full stop — they have no fat reserve to carry them and the risk is not worth it.

Troubleshooting a refusal, in order

Work down this list before you assume illness. Nine times in ten the answer is in the first four items.

  1. Measure the cool-end surface with an infrared thermometer. If it is below 18 °C, the snake is not refusing food, its gut is refusing to run.
  2. Check the photoperiod. Twelve hours on the timer. No timer, that is your first fix.
  3. Warm the prey properly. Thaw completely, then bring the prey surface to 35-38 °C (95-100 °F) with warm water before offering. A cold mouse reads as dead and uninteresting.
  4. Offer at dusk. Corn snakes are crepuscular and the feeding response peaks in low light.
  5. Check for shed. A snake in the opaque stage will refuse food reliably for 4-7 days before ecdysis. Wait it out.
  6. Weigh the snake. No weight loss means the refusal is seasonal. Weight loss means escalate.

Offer once weekly, leave the prey in for 20 minutes, then remove it. Offering every two days trains a refusal response and sometimes a defensive strike. If the first four items check out and the snake has been off food for more than four weeks while warm, book a vet visit — the most common clinical finding is a husbandry fault the keeper cannot see because nobody measured it.

Life stages, and the ecological half

Hatchlings and juveniles under 30 g should not be brumated. Feed every 5-7 days on pinkies or fuzzies, keep the gradient stable, and watch body condition rather than the calendar. A juvenile that refuses food for more than ten days in winter needs a vet because its reserves are too thin to burn. Subadults from 30 to 150 g can go either way, but I keep them warm and feeding until their second winter, then reassess. Adults over 150 g in good body condition are where brumation earns its place.

If you run a bioactive substrate with a springtail and isopod clean-up crew, winter is harder on the crew than the snake. A vivarium at 12-15 °C slows isopod reproduction and stops them feeding at around 10 °C. Springtails tolerate cooler conditions and I use them almost exclusively for winter crews. Waste accumulates more slowly but persists longer — the biological filter is running at half speed. On paper with a hide at each end, my honest opinion is that you should not. It is defensible for quarantine and mites, but as a permanent setup it gives the animal no way to regulate. I stopped using paper permanently for this species in 2019; refusals went down and shed success went up, and I do not think that is a coincidence.

Morphs do not change husbandry, with one exception. Scaleless animals have reduced or absent belly scales and, in my experience, higher susceptibility to abrasion and humidity-related skin problems. Keep them on smoother substrate, at the upper humidity band, and watch for minor injuries that scaled animals would shrug off.

Frequently asked questions

When should I panic about a corn snake not eating in winter?

Panic is the wrong word. Act when the snake has lost more than 5 % of its body weight, has been off food for more than four weeks while warm, or shows a change in behaviour — lethargy, extended soaking, skin lesions. Without weight loss and with normal behaviour, a winter refusal in a healthy adult is almost always a temperature or photoperiod issue.

Can I just skip brumation and keep feeding?

Yes, and in a heated house it is the simpler, safer route. Hold your gradient and photoperiod steady through winter, feed every 10-14 days, and measure the cool end because that is what drifts. Brumation is not required for health in a captive corn snake, and a properly maintained winter is a legitimate husbandry choice.

My snake ate, then the room got cold, and now it is sluggish — what do I do?

That combination worries me. Undigested prey in a cold snake can putrefy, and the risk of regurgitation or infection is real. Raise the enclosure temperature to 28-30 °C for 48 hours, keep the animal hydrated, and if it regurges or does not perk up within two days, call an exotics vet. Do not feed again until it has passed the previous meal.
