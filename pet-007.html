Why Your Corn Snake Refuses Food in Winter — and When It Is Not Brumation

A four-month winter fast is normal biology. Losing a tenth of the body weight is not — here is how to tell them apart before you intervene.

A corn snake's appetite is governed by a temperature band of roughly 25-30 °C (77-86 °F) at the warm end of the enclosure, and once the ambient reading drops below about 21 °C (70 °F) the gut simply stops doing its job. Digestion in Pantherophis guttatus is an enzymatic process, and enzymes are temperature-dependent proteins: too cold and they do not catalyse, the meal sits in the stomach, and the snake vomits it or refuses the next one. That is why a corn snake not eating in winter is usually not sick — it is cold, and it knows it.

The night I got the thermostat wrong

February, a twelve-year-old male called Pilgrim, 4 p.m. and I had just come back from a week away. I dropped a thawed weaner in at 26 °C on the warm mat and a 19 °C cool end — looks fine on paper. He struck, swallowed, and spent the next four days coiled on the mat like a knot. On day five the smell hit me before I opened the lid: a partially digested weaner, regurgitated, and a snake who then refused everything for six weeks. My error was not the temperature I set; it was that my heat mat was running on a plain on/off plug and the surface was swinging between 24 and 31 °C while the probe sat at the wrong end of the box. The regurgitation cost me six weeks of feeding and a vet visit that confirmed exactly what I should have known: the gut was not warm enough, long enough. I now use an infrared thermometer gun on the surface before every feed.

Reading the temperature before you blame the snake

Before you read any meaning into a refusal, get numbers. A corn snake enclosure should run a gradient: 29-30 °C directly over the heat source, 22-24 °C at the cool end, measured with a probe at snake height, not a dial stuck to the glass. Use an infrared gun on the actual floor of the warm hide and write the number down. If the surface is 26 °C when it should be 29 °C, that is your answer.

The next read is body weight, not appetite. Weigh the snake weekly on a 0.1 g scale — an adult male is typically 350-600 g and a healthy animal should lose no more than about 5 % of body weight over a whole winter. If the weight holds while the food goes uneaten, you are watching brumation, not starvation. If the weight drops fast, the animal is genuinely in trouble and the temperature is probably the first thing that broke.

What actual brumation is, and when to run it deliberately

Brumation is not hibernation. A corn snake in brumation is awake, alert, and simply running at a lower metabolic rate — the process is thermoregulatory dormancy, and it is triggered by shortening photoperiod and falling night temperatures. In the wild, a Florida corn snake in winter may sit at 12-16 °C for weeks. In captivity, most keepers who breed deliberately brumate at 12-15 °C for 8-12 weeks from late November, with the animal fasted for 10-14 days beforehand and water available throughout.

If you are not breeding, you do not have to brumate at all — plenty of keepers hold 12 hours of light and 25-29 °C year-round and the snake stays feadable. What you must not do is let the enclosure drift into 16-18 °C unknowingly, which is the classic cold-garage mistake: cold enough to shut down appetite, not cold enough to be a genuine planned brumation. Set the photoperiod and temperature deliberately, or don't.

The decision tree when the food comes back out

  1. Measure the warm-end surface with an infrared gun. Under 28 °C? Fix heating before offering food again.
  2. Weigh the snake on a 0.1 g kitchen scale. Note the grams. A 400 g adult that drops below about 380 g needs veterinary attention.
  3. Check for a pre-shed: blue eyes, dull scales. A corn snake that is 3-5 days from shedding will refuse regardless of temperature.
  4. Withhold food for 10 days if it regurgitated — the oesophageal and gastric lining needs time to recover before the next meal.
  5. Reoffer a smaller prey item, warmed to 37-38 °C at the head end, always with the warm hide available.

The failure mode at every step is the same: a snake sitting on a hide that is 26 °C when it thinks it is 30 °C. The cheapest fix is a proper dimming thermostat on the heat mat, set to hold the surface at 29 °C — not a pulse plug that overshoots. The second cheapest is a 0.1 g scale so you can actually tell weight from appetite.

Frequently asked questions

My corn snake stopped eating in November and it is now February. Is it dying?

Probably not, if it has not lost more than about 5 % of body weight. Weigh it now on a 0.1 g scale and check your warm-end surface reading — if that is below 28 °C, you have found the cause. A healthy adult can go three months without food; a juvenile cannot, so age matters here.

Do I need to brumate my corn snake?

No. Brumation is a breeding tool, not a husbandry requirement. If you are not planning to breed, keep the enclosure at 25-30 °C, run 10-12 hours of light, and feed normally through winter — the snake does not need a cold rest to stay healthy, only a genuine temperature gradient to feed properly.

Should I force-feed a snake that has not eaten for six weeks?

No. Force-feeding is a clinical procedure, not a home fix, and it is traumatic and easily damages the oesophagus. First rule out warmth, pre-shed, and stress; if the snake is losing weight steadily, get it to a reptile vet for a proper examination rather than reaching for a syringe.
