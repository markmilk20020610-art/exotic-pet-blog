Why Your Corn Snake Refuses Food in Winter — and When It Is Not Brumation

A four-month winter fast is normal biology. Losing a tenth of the body weight is not — here is how to tell them apart before you intervene.

A corn snake (Pantherophis guttatus) that refuses food in November is usually not ill — it is answering a photoperiod and thermal cue, and the whole diagnosis turns on two numbers: a warm spot of 30–32 °C (86–90 °F) and a body weight you have actually written down. Lose more than 10 % of that weight and the fast has stopped being brumation and started being a problem. Everything below is about telling those two states apart before you intervene, because intervening in the first one is how keepers cause the second.

The winter I force-fed a snake that was not sick

She was a nine-year-old female, 480 g in October, and by the first week of December she had refused three feeds in a row. I did what a worried keeper does: I raised the hot spot to 34 °C, left a warmed weaner in overnight, and covered the glass with a towel so she would feel private. She took it at 2 a.m. and regurgitated by morning. The smell in that room is something I can still call up. What it cost her was eleven days of an empty gut, a course of electrolytes, and a shed that came off in three pieces. What it cost me was the lesson that a refusal is information, not an emergency. She was 471 g — nine grams down, about 2 % — and she went on to brumate for ten weeks and eat in March like nothing had happened.

What a drop in temperature does to a digestive tract

Snakes do not chew and they do not grind. Digestion is a chemical process run by stomach acid and a gut flora that only work inside a narrow thermal window — broadly 26–30 °C for this species. Below roughly 18 °C that machinery stops, but the prey in the stomach does not. It sits there, it putrefies, and the animal's only defence is to reverse the peristalsis and eject it. Regurgitation is therefore not a sign of illness; it is a sign that food and temperature were mismatched. The danger is what follows: each regurgitation costs water, electrolytes and the gut lining's recovery time, and a snake that regurgitates three times in a season can develop oesophagitis and a permanent aversion to prey.

The same logic runs the other way in spring. A snake brought out of brumation is not ready to eat on the day you raise the thermostat — the gut needs seven to ten days back at full operating temperature before it can handle a meal, and the first meal should be smaller than usual.

Note what is missing from that table: appetite. A snake that is brumating will often still strike at a warmed prey item, and a snake with a gut infection will sometimes take one too. Appetite is the least diagnostic signal available, which is why the weight column does the work.

Reading the refusal, in order

  1. Weigh the snake. Grams, same day of the week, before feeding. Two readings two weeks apart tell you more than any behaviour.
  2. Measure the surface, not the air. An infrared thermometer on the hot-spot hide; 30–32 °C is the target, and a 3 °C drift downward is enough to stop digestion.
  3. Check the cool end is actually cool. A single mat in a small vivarium can raise the whole gradient to 28 °C, which removes the thermal choice the snake needs to regulate itself.
  4. Check the prey temperature. A thawed rodent at room temperature reads as carrion. Warm it to 35–37 °C in a bag in hot water and offer it at dusk.
  5. Leave it alone for a week. No handling, no re-offering, no covering the glass. Repeated disturbance is the most common cause of a refusal that outlasts the season.

If the weight holds and the parameters are right, the correct intervention is no intervention. If the weight is falling, stop treating it as brumation and get a faecal check — the two most common findings in a winter refusal that is not seasonal are flagellates and a low-grade respiratory infection, and neither is visible from outside the glass.

Frequently asked questions

Should I brumate my corn snake every year?

No. Brumation is a breeding tool, not a health requirement. A healthy adult can be kept at normal temperatures all winter and simply fed less often — every 14 to 21 days rather than every 7. Only brumate if you intend to breed, and only after a veterinary check and a full gut clearance of at least two weeks.

How long can a corn snake go without food before it is dangerous?

An adult in good body condition can go eight to twelve weeks without meaningful risk, provided fresh water is available and the weight loss stays under 10 %. Hatchlings are a different animal entirely: two weeks of refusal in a 15 g hatchling is an emergency, because they have no fat reserve to draw on.

My snake ate, then regurgitated two days later. What now?

Wait ten to fourteen days before offering anything — the gut lining needs that long to recover — then offer a prey item half the usual size with the hot spot verified at 30–32 °C. If a second regurgitation follows, stop feeding entirely and get a vet involved; repeated regurgitation in corn snakes is more often infectious or obstructive than husbandry-related.
