Why Your Ball Python Is Refusing Frozen-Thawed Rats

Before you change the feeding schedule, change the thermometer. Nine out of ten feeding strikes are a temperature, security or presentation problem — in that order.

A ball python's warm-end floor should sit at 88-92 °F (31-33 °C) with a cool end at 78-80 °F (25-27 °C), and appetite is the first thing those numbers govern. Python regius is a sit-and-wait ambush predator: it has no reason to strike at a rat it cannot smell, cannot see in the dark, or lacks the heat to digest. Drift below the warm-end target for two weeks and the gut slows, the animal reads the season as lean, and a thawed rat on the tongs gets the same look you'd give a brick. The rat is rarely the problem. The room usually is.

The keeper's diary

I bought my first ball python as a confirmed frozen-thawed feeder and took the breeder's word for it, so I checked nothing. She took two meals in October, then stopped. November, December, nothing. My mistake was the warmth: her hot hide read 84 °F on the probe and the room sat at 68 °F overnight, and I had never checked the floor of the enclosure, only the air above it. She wasn't sick. She was cold. On the 14th of February I moved the heat under the hide, added a second identical cave on the cool side, and put in a damp moss box. On the 17th, around half past nine, she took a 60 g thawed small rat in her own time. She'd lost 40 g across four months and put it back by April. That was a lesson about my instruments, not her.

Work the checklist before you change the food

Refusal is a symptom with a long list of causes. Work through these in order and change one thing at a time, so you know what fixed it.

  1. Temperature at the floor. Measure the surface of the hot hide, not the air 30 cm above it. Aim for 88-92 °F under the hot hide and 78-80 °F at the cool end. A gun reads this in seconds; your hand does not.
  2. A gradient, not one hot spot. A snake that cannot choose its temperature will not eat, because it cannot thermoregulate after a meal.
  3. Humidity. Hold 60-70 %. The evidence on exact figures is thinner than the hobby pretends, so aim for the middle and watch the shed.
  4. Two identical hides. Same size, same shape, one warm end, one cool. Security is the variable most often missed.
  5. The rat. Thaw overnight in the fridge, then bag it in hot tap water until the head end reads 100-105 °F (38-40 °C). A rat still cold at the skull gets refused.
  6. Presentation. Dim light, quiet room, tongs not fingers, a slow drag. Thirty seconds, then wait a week.

If the numbers are right and the snake still refuses, the two-hide pairing is the first change I'd make.

Weigh the snake weekly on a scale that reads to 0.1 g and write it down. That log is what stops you panicking: a mature male can skip four to six months over winter and lose almost nothing, while a two-year-old female dropping 60 g in six weeks is a different problem. If weight holds, the fast is metabolic. If it falls, recheck temperature and hygiene.

Offering the rat, and what not to change

Offer in the evening with the room quiet. Hold the rat at the shoulder, not the tail, and let the snake take it. If there is no strike within thirty seconds, leave it on a paper plate under the hot hide for an hour in the dark — some take it that way — then bin it. Never re-freeze a thawed rat. Offer weekly, not daily; a snake offered food every day becomes a snake that ignores food.

  • Change: surface temperatures, hide count, rat temperature, time of day.
  • Change slowly: humidity. Hold each change two weeks before judging it.
  • Don't swap to live prey as a shortcut unless a vet or experienced keeper advises it; it's harder to reverse than to avoid. Don't force-feed. Don't handle on a feeding day.
  • Don't guess: if you can't measure a number, you can't rule it out. Get the gun and the scale before another rat.

If the animal looks genuinely unwell — sunken eyes, limp posture, stuck shed in patches — that's a vet trip, not a husbandry puzzle. A convalescent formula belongs to a recovery plan a vet sets, not to a refusing snake you're trying to coax.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a ball python go without eating?

A healthy adult male can fast four to six months, sometimes longer, with little weight loss — I've known one go seven and come back fine. Juveniles and breeding females are less forgiving. Check weight monthly: if it's stable and the snake is alert, wait it out. If weight is falling, treat it as a problem.

Why will my snake take live but ignore frozen-thawed?

Usually the rat's heat and smell. A thawed rat straight from the fridge sits near 40 °F in the head, and the animal's heat pits read that as dead and cold. Warm it to 100-105 °F at the head, dry it, present it at dusk. If it still refuses a properly warmed rat for weeks, check husbandry before assuming prey preference.

Should I worry about a refused meal during shedding?

No — most ball pythons refuse while their eyes are clouded and the skin is loose, then take food a few days after a clean shed. Check humidity holds 60-70 % so the shed comes off in one piece, and offer normally the following week. If the refusal isn't tied to a shed, work the checklist above.
