Why Your Ball Python Is Refusing Frozen-Thawed Rats

Before you change the feeding schedule, change the thermometer. Nine out of ten feeding strikes are a temperature, security or presentation problem — in that order.

Your ball python has eaten frozen-thawed reliably for eight months. Then it stops. It ignores the rat, balls up, and looks at you with the flat indifference that only a Python regius can manage. Two weeks pass. Three.

This is the most common question in the species, and the honest answer is that a feeding strike is usually not about food at all. Work through these in order, and do not skip ahead.

1. Temperature, measured not guessed

A ball python digests by choice. If the hot spot is 27 °C instead of 31 °C, digestion slows, the animal feels wrong, and it stops eating. This is the single most common cause, and the most commonly missed, because keepers trust the thermostat dial instead of a reading.

Measure the surface under the hot hide with an infrared gun. You want 31–32 °C. Cool end 24–26 °C. If the numbers are off, fix them and wait two weeks before drawing any conclusion.

2. Security — the hide test

A ball python that does not feel hidden will not eat. It is that simple, and it is why so many glass tanks with one oversized hide produce chronic problem feeders.

  • Two hides, identical, one warm one cool.
  • Snug: the snake should touch the walls when coiled.
  • Opaque back and sides on the enclosure — cover the glass if you must.
  • Clutter. Empty floor space reads as exposure.

If your snake spends all day in the cool hide and never uses the warm one, the warm hide is probably too big or too hot.

A ball python that is not eating is usually a ball python that does not feel safe. Fix the room before you fix the rat.

3. Presentation: the rat is probably too cold

Frozen-thawed prey must be thawed thoroughly and then warmed to roughly 37 °C at the head. A rat that is thawed but cool reads as carrion, and many ball pythons will refuse it indefinitely.

The reliable method: thaw in the fridge overnight, then place in a sealed bag in warm water for 10–15 minutes. Check the head with an infrared gun. Then offer with tongs, holding the rat at the level of the snake's head, with a small twitch — not a dance.

Do not refreeze. Do not microwave. Do not leave a refused rat in the enclosure overnight.

4. Overfeeding, and the schedule nobody follows

An adult ball python eats every 7–14 days, one prey item of roughly 10–15% of its body weight. Keepers who feed every five days "because it always takes it" end up with a fat snake that goes off food for months. If the spine is buried and the body is round in cross-section, the animal is overweight.

5. The fast that is not a crisis

Healthy adult ball pythons fast. Two to four months, typically over winter, is normal and requires no intervention if the animal is not losing weight quickly, is drinking, and is behaving normally.

What is a crisis: rapid weight loss, regurgitation, wheezing, mucus in the mouth, or a snake that is suddenly lethargic. Those are veterinary appointments, not husbandry puzzles.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a ball python go without eating?

A healthy adult can go several months. The number that matters is weight, not days. Weigh monthly; a loss of more than 10% of body weight is the point to involve a vet.

Should I try live prey to break the strike?

No. Live feeding is a welfare problem for the rodent and an injury risk for the snake, and it teaches a hunger strike to escalate. Fix temperature, security and prey temperature instead.

Will handling make it worse?

Yes, temporarily. Reduce handling to zero while you troubleshoot, and never handle in the 48 hours after a feed.
