Cockatiel Chronic Egg Laying: The Light Schedule That Switches It Off

Removing the eggs does not stop the cycle, it restarts it. What ends a chronic layer's season is a shorter day and a depleted calcium reserve that has to be caught early.

A cockatiel (Nymphicus hollandicus) exposed to more than 12 hours of light a day will, in many hens, keep a reproductive axis switched on indefinitely, and the oviduct will keep producing yolk after yolk at roughly 48-hour intervals. The cost is paid in medullary bone — the calcium store inside the long bones that a laying hen draws down to shell each egg — and that store refills from the gut. Get the photoperiod and the calcium wrong together and you get a bird that is thin, fluffed, passing soft shells or no shell, and eventually unable to stand.

Two o'clock in the morning, and she is still scratching in the corner

I lost a hen called Piper in a November, and the thing that still bothers me is that I had the calendar right and the lamp wrong. She was in a 36 × 24 × 36 inch cage in a room where I kept a desk lamp on until midnight and the window blind was thin enough to let the streetlight in. So she was on something like 16 hours of useful light. I knew about light schedules in the abstract. What I did not know was that Piper had been laying every other day since August, that 14 eggs had gone through her, and that her keel had gone sharp under my thumb weeks before I noticed. When I finally weighed her on the kitchen scale she was 78 g. She had been 94 g in the spring. That is a 17 % drop in body weight and I had read it as "she's flying more". The last egg she passed had no shell at all, just a membrane, and she was dead four days later. The post-mortem word was egg peritonitis, with a contributing hypocalcaemia. I have not kept a cage in a lamp-lit room since.

What a laying hen is actually doing to herself

The reproductive cycle in cockatiels is driven by day length. Extended photoperiod — anything pushing reliably past about 12 hours of light — plus food abundance plus a cavity or dark corner to inspect is, to a hen, a green spring. She does not need a mate. She will lay anyway, and chronic egg laying is defined more by frequency than by total: hens producing clutches back to back without a rest period, or laying from a perch with no nest at all, are the ones that get into trouble. A normal clutch is 4-6 eggs, one every other day, incubated 18-21 days if she sits. A chronic layer does not sit, does not stop, and can produce in the region of 20-60 eggs in a year against a biological expectation of a clutch or two a season.

Each egg is a calcium project. The shell is mostly calcium carbonate, and the hen mobilises it from medullary bone under oestrogen-driven osteoclast activity — osteoclasts are the cells that break bone down and release its mineral into the blood. When demand outruns supply, the bone comes off faster than it can be rebuilt. That is the same pathway seen in any laying vertebrate, and in birds it is brisk: a hen can lose a substantial fraction of her skeletal calcium across a heavy laying season. This is why a chronic layer's first visible problem is often not the egg count but the bones — a keel that feels like a knife, a bird that sits low, a wing that droops because the humerus is soft. Egg binding, prolapse and egg peritonitis are the acute events that follow. Soft-shelled eggs, shell-less eggs and eggs with a chalky rough finish are the warning shot.

Setting the light, and the rest of the season

The single most effective intervention is to shorten the day. Take the light down to 8-10 hours and hold it there until the hen has stopped laying for at least a month, then bring it back slowly. Do not simply switch the ceiling light off — a window with a thin blind is still light. Blackout, or move the cage to a room with no lamp after dark. Cover the cage at a fixed hour if the room is unavoidable, but a cover that is the wrong size and lets light in at the seams achieves nothing measurable.

  1. Measure the actual day. Log the time the room first receives daylight through a window and the time the last artificial light goes off. That total, not the lamp alone, is what the bird reads.
  2. Target 8-10 hours now. A hen in an active laying run drops to a shorter day; bring it back up to a stable 10-12 hours once she has rested.
  3. Remove the nest. Dark corners, boxes, under-sofa hideaways and the inside of a cereal packet are all nesting cavities. If she is inspecting and shredding, she is being invited.
  4. Cool the heat and cut the soft food. Drop a warm spot if the room is warm; warm, food-rich, lit, private accommodation is the recipe, not the antidote.
  5. Do not remove her from the room. Separation stress can trigger more laying in some hens. Keep the routine steady, change the light, change the furnishings, and let her settle.

Where to put the calcium: not a cuttlebone alone. A hen may scrape cuttlebone but a heavily laying hen needs a bioavailable calcium source with vitamin D3, because D3 is the hormone-side switch that lets her gut absorb calcium at all. A working balance is 1.5-2 % calcium in the dry food, with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio around 1.5:1 to 2:1, and a calcium+D3 supplement offered separately so she can take what she needs. If she is on a seed mix, the ratio is often the wrong way round — seeds are typically low calcium and relatively high phosphorus, which pushes the ratio towards 2:1 the other way. Pellets are usually formulated with the correct ratio and the D3 built in; a seed-only hen is fighting her own food. I am not a purist about pellets, but a laying hen should not be on a seed-only diet through a laying run.

The supplement is not a substitute for changing the light. A hen on a perfect diet and a 16-hour day will still lay herself thin. The diet buys her the calcium to survive the behaviour; the light stops the behaviour. Do both.

The weight log is the early warning, and the cage is not the only place it matters

Weigh the same hen in the same conditions at the same time of day — on a small digital scale with 0.1 g resolution, ideally before the first feed of the morning after a normal night. A healthy adult hen sits roughly between 80 g and 115 g, though size varies widely in this species and the true reference is her own baseline. A hen that has dropped 5 % over a few weeks is losing faster than she can rebuild, and that is the point at which I would be ringing the vet rather than waiting.

Two things get written off that should not be. The first is weight loss that everyone reads as "she's more active". The second is a keel that is sharp to the touch — run a thumb along the breastbone; a rounded ridge is normal, a blade with the muscle fallen either side is not. A hen whose bones have softened may still fly, still eat, still call, and then crumble in a week.

Handling matters here too. A hen in a chronic run is a fragile animal and the standard restraint — towel, fingers around the body, thumb over the wings — needs to be gentler than usual and briefer than usual. Weighing is the one handling that earns its keep. Everything else is a stressor that can itself prompt laying.

What the vet does with a hen you kept too long

Clinical management of chronic egg laying is not a DIY path. A veterinary surgeon has options that the household does not: a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist implant, which is the standard veterinary-adjacent treatment for reproductive suppression in pet birds; treatment for hypocalcaemia if the bird is actually fitting or unable to perch; and surgery or hospitalisation if there is a retained egg. I am not going to pretend I know the exact protocol for an implant because it varies by clinician and by country, and because the evidence base is genuinely thinner than the parrot forums make it sound. The one I would want done is a proper physical examination with radiographs and a calcium check before anyone starts fiddling with hormones.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just let her lay it out and stop when she's done?

Sometimes, for a first clutch, yes — provide calcium and a quiet dark nest-free space and let her finish. But if she has laid more than one clutch in a row, or laid without a nest, she is in a laying run and will not self-terminate. Shortening the day is the intervention, not waiting.

Will a cock bird make her lay more, or less?

Presence of a male can trigger laying, so a shared cage complicates the picture. If the two are bonded and she is laying chronically, separate them during the intervention period. Do not separate bonded birds abruptly without watching for distress — but for a hen at risk, the laying has to be brought down first.

Is cuttlebone enough calcium for a chronic layer?

Often not. Cuttlebone is mostly calcium carbonate and helps, but a hen laying every other day can outpace what she scrapes. Offer a calcium+D3 supplement in addition, and check that the base diet's calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is correct — a seed-heavy diet often is not.
