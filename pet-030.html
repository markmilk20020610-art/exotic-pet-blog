Cockatiel Chronic Egg Laying: The Light Schedule That Switches It Off

Removing the eggs does not stop the cycle, it restarts it. What ends a chronic layer's season is a shorter day and a depleted calcium reserve that has to be caught early.

On a natural Australian daylength of roughly 10-12 hours of light, a hen Nymphicus hollandicus shuts her reproductive axis down until the following spring. Push her to 14-16 hours under a domestic ceiling lamp and that same axis stays open — the gonadotrophin signal keeps arriving — and she starts laying clutch after clutch with no male and no nest. Chronic oviposition is not a behaviour problem. It is an endocrine problem with a lighting trigger, and it can bleed a hen's skeleton dry one egg at a time.

The evening it clicked

I remember a grey hen from a few years back — "Pip", a hand-fed bird about three years old, kept in a warm front room where the family watched television until eleven. She had laid nineteen eggs in four months, each one on the cage floor, each one leaving her a little more fluffed and a little less steady. I'd been treating it as a calcium problem, adding calcified grit and a cuttlebone to her dish, when the actual problem was the lamp in the corner. The moment I put her on a 9-hour light day and a blackout cover, the laying stopped inside a fortnight. The lesson cost me one bird's reserves and a vet visit I should have made sooner. She never fully refilled that skeleton.

Why the hen lays and why it drains her

Cockatiel egg production is driven by photoperiod, not by the presence of a mate. When the retina registers long days, the hypothalamus releases gonadotrophin-releasing hormone, which drives the pituitary to secrete follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone; those signal the ovary to mature follicles and the shell gland to lay down a shell. Take the long days away and the whole chain falls quiet. Leave them in place and the hen keeps recycling the same pathway month after month.

Each shell is calcified from the medullary bone, a temporary calcium store laid down inside the long bones and drawn on during laying. If dietary calcium does not cover the withdrawal — and a seed mix typically does not — the hen mobilises structural bone instead. That is nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism in slow motion: parathyroid hormone rises to pull calcium from the skeleton, the bones thin, and the bird that was laying happily last month is suddenly weak in the legs and prone to fracture. In a chronically laying hen the egg-binding risk climbs too, because a depleted shell gland cannot build a proper shell.

The light schedule that stops it

The single most effective intervention is photoperiod. Get the hen to a genuine winter day — a short light period with a long, complete dark — and the reproductive drive winds down.

  1. Set a strict schedule: 8-10 hours of light and 14-16 hours of total darkness. Do not let a television, a hallway lamp or a kitchen strip-light count as "dark". Cover the cage with an opaque blackout cover at a fixed time each evening.
  2. Keep the room the cage sits in at a steady 18-22 °C (64-72 °F). Warmth nudges the axis toward breeding; a cool, still room helps suppress it.
  3. Remove every nest cue: no happy hut, no box, no dark corner, no shredded paper she can arrange. Hens will lay on a bare floor, but a nest site accelerates the cycle.
  4. Hold that schedule for at least 6-8 weeks before you judge whether it is working. The axis takes days to fall quiet and weeks to stay quiet.
  5. Resume a normal 12-hour-ish day only when the hen has been egg-free for several months, and watch for the first sign of a new egg — that is your signal to dial the light back again.

Do not confuse a short light day with a cold, dark cage. The hen still needs her basking spot and a gradient she can use. A warm zone around 28-30 °C (82-86 °F) at one end and a cool end near 20 °C (68 °F) gives her somewhere to choose from, which matters more when she is depleted and thermoregulating poorly. The number that belongs on a timer is the light, and the number that belongs on a thermometer is the gradient — keep them separate.

Calcium: the number behind the shell

Once a hen is in lay, she needs a calcium source she actually uses. Cuttlebone is fine as a choice, but the realistic target is a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the region of 2:1 in the overall diet, with vitamin D3 present so the gut can absorb it. A seed-only diet sits closer to 1:1 or worse and leaves the hen drawing on bone.

For a hen recovering from chronic laying, I keep a dish of a fine calcium-with-D3 powder available and dust the small amount of soft food she takes. Weigh her weekly on a 0.1 g kitchen scale — a laying hen can drop 3-5 g in a week and it is easy to miss by eye. The scale is not optional.

When diet is not enough

If a hen is still laying on a strict short day after 8-10 weeks, or if she is egg-bound, or if she has laid a soft-shelled egg, that is a veterinary problem, not a husbandry one. A chronically laying hen may need a hormonal implant to switch the axis off, and that is a decision an avian vet makes with radiographs or bloodwork in front of them. I have seen keepers try to wait it out and lose a hen to egg-binding while waiting. The vet bill for an implant is one of the few that pays for itself in a live bird.

There is no magic food here. A hen that has been producing eggs for months needs calories and calcium and a dark, cool, boring cage. That is it.

Frequently asked questions

Will a hen stop laying if I take away her cuttlebone?

No. Removing calcium does not switch off the reproductive axis, it just forces the hen to pull calcium from her bones to build the next shell. That accelerates the bone loss. Keep the calcium available and fix the light instead.

Is a short light day cruel?

A natural winter photoperiod of 8-10 hours is what the species experiences in the wild. The hen is not being deprived — she is being given back her seasonal cue. What matters is a consistent, dark night, not the number on its own.

How long before the eggs stop?

Most hens stop within 2-4 weeks of a strict short day, but the axis can take longer to fully quieten and the occasional final egg is common. If she is still laying after 8-10 weeks of true darkness, get her seen by an avian vet.
