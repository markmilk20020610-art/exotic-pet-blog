Do Leopard Geckos Need UVB — or Just a Heat Mat?

The honest answer is more interesting than either camp admits: leopard geckos are crepuscular, not nocturnal, and a low-level UVB zone changes how you supplement calcium.

Ask ten keepers whether leopard geckos need UVB and you will get two confident, opposite answers. Both camps are arguing from the same wrong premise: that this is a nocturnal animal living in total darkness.

Leopard geckos are crepuscular. They are active at dawn and dusk, they bask briefly at burrow mouths, and field data shows them exposed to low-level sunlight. That reframes the question: not "UVB or no UVB", but "how much, and what changes if I provide it".

What UVB actually does for this species

UVB drives endogenous vitamin D3 synthesis, which is what allows calcium to be absorbed. Without it, you must supply D3 in powder form — and that works, but it is a narrow window. Too little causes metabolic bone disease; too much is toxic to the kidneys.

Providing a low-level UVB zone gives the animal a choice, and lets you reduce dietary D3. That is the entire argument in favour, and it is a good one.

The practical setup

  • Use a low-output tube — 2–7% UVB (a "shadedweller" type), not a desert-strength bulb. This is a crepuscular animal from scrubland, not a bearded dragon.
  • Mount it at one end, so the gecko can leave the UV zone entirely. Choice is the point.
  • Provide shade and cover — cork flats and a plant. An exposed UV zone is not used.
  • 10–12 hour photoperiod, on a timer. No coloured night bulbs; they disturb the animal.
  • Replace the tube every 9–12 months, even if it still lights. UV output decays long before visible light does.
UVB is not a substitute for belly heat. A gecko with perfect lighting and a 27 °C floor will still stop digesting.

Belly heat is still the main event

Leopard geckos absorb heat ventrally, from warm rock. A heat mat or deep heat projector under one third of the floor, on a dimming thermostat, giving a 32 °C surface and a 24 °C cool end, is the foundation. UVB sits on top of that, never instead of it.

Measure the surface with an infrared gun. Human hands read 32 °C as "pleasantly warm" and tell you nothing.

How supplementation changes

With UVB: dust with plain calcium at every feed, and use a D3 supplement rarely — once or twice a month at most, or not at all depending on the bulb and the animal's exposure.

Without UVB: calcium with D3 once or twice a week, plain calcium at every other feed. This is a well-established, safe protocol — it simply requires more discipline.

Never do both at full dose. Over-supplementation looks a lot like the MBD you were trying to prevent, and it is harder to diagnose.

Signs something is wrong

Rubbery jaw, difficulty lifting the body off the floor, tremors in the toes, a curved spine, or repeated shedding problems. Any of these means a vet visit with a blood panel — husbandry changes alone will not reverse advanced metabolic bone disease.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a UVB bulb instead of a heat mat?

No. A bulb warms the air and the back, not the belly, and it dries the enclosure. Use both: heat for digestion, UVB for calcium metabolism.

Is a coloured night bulb acceptable?

No. Leopard geckos see colour and are active at dusk; red and blue bulbs disrupt their cycle. Use a ceramic heater or a heat mat if you need night warmth.

My gecko never sits under the UVB. Is it wasted?

It is still providing a gradient and a light cycle, and many geckos use it briefly at dawn. If the animal avoids the zone entirely, check the output — a bulb that is too strong will be avoided.
