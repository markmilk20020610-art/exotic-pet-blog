Do Leopard Geckos Need UVB — or Just a Heat Mat?

The honest answer is more interesting than either camp admits: leopard geckos are crepuscular, not nocturnal, and a low-level UVB zone changes how you supplement calcium.

A leopard gecko's warm-side floor wants 88-92 °F (31-33 °C), its cool side sits near 75 °F (24 °C), and its skin is built for a UV index between 0.6 and 1.1 UVI at the basking spot — a fraction of full sun and roughly what a shaded rock edge throws off in the morning. Those numbers matter because a leopard gecko is a crepuscular animal that will sleep through a great deal of your setup and only feel the parts that touch its belly. Get the floor temperature wrong and the gut stalls before the animal ever looks thin.

What a bad bulb cost me on a Tuesday

Ellis, my nine-year-old male Eublepharis macularius, stopped taking food the second week of October. I had a 40-gallon tub build with a heat mat on a dimming thermostat and a cheap strip of "tropical" UVB from a previous project, and I had stopped checking the bulbs because the mat was doing the obvious work. On the Tuesday I finally sat with him — 21:40 by the clock — I put the infrared gun on the floor and got 84 °F on the warm hide, 79 °F on the cool end. A thermostat reading, not a guess. He had eaten one cricket in nine days.

What I had missed was the other instrument: a UVI meter held six inches under that strip showed 0.1. Barely a reading. The lamp was old and the wrong output. I swapped to a proper fixture, kept the mat where it was, and watched him spend the next four evenings sitting half-out under the light with one back leg splayed. He ate on day six. The cost was a wasted bulb, a wasted month, and the vet consult I paid to confirm my paranoia.

Heat first, and what "done right" looks like

Leopard geckos are nocturnal in habit but not in physiology; they are active at dusk and they digest in the dark. The floor is the spine of the setup.

  1. Set the heat mat under one third of the floor, never under the whole thing, so the animal can always step off it.
  2. Run it through a dimming thermostat, not a simple on/off switch, so the surface does not swing several degrees every twenty minutes.
  3. Measure the surface with an infrared gun, not the air, because the gecko is lying on the floor, not floating.
  4. Aim for 88-92 °F at the warm hide, 72-77 °F at the cool end, and let the middle gradient form itself.
  5. Replace bulbs and check output quarterly.

The failure modes are predictable. Too hot and you get a gecko that lives permanently on the cool side and refuses to eat from heat stress. Too cool and you get one that looks fine but will not digest, and the first visible sign is often a regurgitated cricket left on the paper two days later. So does a leopard gecko need UVB, or just a heat mat?

The honest answer is that a leopard gecko can survive and often thrive on a correct heat mat alone with dietary vitamin D3 supplementation. It does better with measured UVB, because a light-handled animal synthesises its own D3 and evidence from related gecko work suggests better calcium handling — but the research specifically in this species is thin, and I will say so plainly.

  • No UVB: you must dust every feed with calcium+D3, and you must not overdo the D3, because the vitamin is fat-soluble and can accumulate.
  • With UVB: use a low-output tube or a compact lamp that gives 0.6-1.1 UVI at the basking spot, on a day cycle of 10-12 hours, and a slightly lighter dusting schedule.
  • Always: a separate heat source for the floor. UVB lamps do not reliably heat a leopard gecko's belly, and a basking bulb alone can cook a small tub. The two jobs are different and should not be merged.

The cheap test is a UVI meter or a lamp with a stated distance on the box. If you cannot measure it, aim for the lower end of the range and dust accordingly. Calcium is second on the list only because lighting is first: every feeder, every time, for a growing animal.

Reading the gecko

The animal will tell you which part of the setup is wrong before the numbers do. A gecko that spends twelve hours in the warm hide is not necessarily happy; it may be telling you the ambient is too cool. One that lives on the cool side and flattens itself there is telling you the floor is too hot. One that sits under the lamp with a leg out is doing the thing you want. Watch where the body is after dark, not where it is at noon, because dusk is when this species actually uses the room. Keep a paper log: date, warm floor, cool floor, UVI, feeding response. After six weeks you will see a pattern that no forum post will give you.

Frequently asked questions

Can a leopard gecko live on a heat mat alone forever?

Yes, many do, provided the floor temperatures are right and every feed is dusted with calcium plus D3. It is a managed compromise rather than a natural setup. The animal has no way to make its own vitamin D3 from light, so the diet does all the work, and you must keep an eye on intake.

How many hours of UVB should I give?

Ten to twelve hours, matching a summer day, and switched off at night. The animal is crepuscular, so it will mostly use the light at the edges of the cycle. Do not run it during winter cool-down, and check the lamp's output every quarter — a lamp that still glows may no longer emit useful UVB.

How do I know the bulb is actually working?

Measure it. Hold a UVI meter at the basking distance and read 0.6–1.1 for a leopard gecko. If you cannot measure, replace low-output tubes on the manufacturer's schedule, which is usually six to twelve months, not when they fail to light. A dull glow and a dead lamp look identical to the gecko.
