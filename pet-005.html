Do Leopard Geckos Need UVB — or Just a Heat Mat?

The honest answer is more interesting than either camp admits: leopard geckos are crepuscular, not nocturnal, and a low-level UVB zone changes how you supplement calcium.

Wild Eublepharis macularius spend most of the day underground, and a burrow mouth can sit at 0.6-1.1 UVI for a few minutes at dawn and dusk while the soil two inches down never gets above dim. That gap between what the sun offers and what the animal actually sits in is the whole UVB argument in one sentence. A leopard gecko is a crepuscular, burrow-dwelling animal, not a basking desert lizard, and the lighting decision has to match that.

The keeper's diary

I had a 14-year-old male, blind in one eye, in a 40-gallon with a heat mat and no light for the first eleven years of his life. He ate, he shed, he bred twice. Then I moved him under a low-output tube at the cool end and watched him for a fortnight. On the ninth evening, at 7:40, he shuffled out and lay side-on under the tube for eleven minutes with his head flat, then went back to his hide. I had it wrong for eleven years is the honest version of that note. His calcium absorption had been carried by dusted feeders, and it worked, but I had never once given him the choice. The cost was negligible; the guilt was not.

Do leopard geckos need UVB light or just heat mats?

Neither answer alone is honest. They need heat, and a heat mat gives it, but a heat mat gives no light and no UVB. The question is really whether you provide UVB on top of thermal gradients, and the answer is: it is strongly recommended, not strictly required, provided the diet is correctly supplemented. The evidence is thinner than the forums claim — there is no controlled long-term study on captive leopard gecko UVB exposure that I would stake a husbandry decision on alone, so what follows is what I run and why.

  • Heat mat only. Works, requires rigorous dietary calcium with D3, and gives no visual light cue.
  • Heat mat plus low-level UVB tube. My current setup. Provides a dawn/dusk gradient and a fallback for dietary shortfalls.
  • UVB and heat from one lamp. Workable, but the hot spot and the UVB zone overlap, which is not how this species uses space.

Setting the numbers

Run a warm-side surface of 88-92 °F (31-33 °C) on the mat or slate, a cool side of 72-78 °F (22-26 °C), and a photoperiod of 12 hours on, 12 off in summer, dropping to 10/14 in winter. Measure surface temperature with an infrared gun, not the air probe; air temperature over a mat reads several degrees low and it is a common reason keepers over-crank the thermostat. Set a dimming thermostat to the mat, never a fixed-output one.

For UVB, use a low-output tube (2-5 % UVB, roughly a 290-320 nm band, in a fitting that flattens the beam) mounted at the cool end, roughly 12-18 inches above the floor, and give the animal a hide with a clear line of sight to it. Do not put a high-output lamp over the hot spot. Replace the tube every 12 months even if it still glows — output fades before light does.

What goes wrong

The most common failure is a mat with no thermostat cooking the slate to 110 °F while the keeper's hand feels "warm". The second is a UVB lamp over the hot hide, which pushes the animal into a corner and forces it to choose between warmth and light. The third is vitamin D3 overshoot: supplementing D3 heavily and running a strong UVB lamp can tip an animal toward hypervitaminosis D. If you run UVB, dial the D3 back to a light dusting once a week rather than every feed. For a mild-mannered feeder, Rep-Cal stays on my shelf for exactly that reason:

Symptoms to watch for: a rubbery jaw, tremors in the toes, or a gecko that stops eating for more than 10-14 days in the warm season. Any of those mean a vet visit and a husbandry audit before you add or remove a lamp.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a heat mat with no light at all?

Yes, and many leopard geckos live full lives under it. You will need to be disciplined about calcium and D3 supplementation and you will lose the natural light cue that helps regulate appetite and shedding. I use a mat plus a low tube because it costs little and gives the animal a choice.

What UVB strength do leopard geckos need?

Low. A 2-5 % tube is the range I use, mounted at the cool end at 12-18 inches. Anything sold as a desert basking lamp is too strong for this species and will push it off the thermal gradient. If a shop cannot tell you the output percentage, do not buy it.

Does my leopard gecko need a day/night cycle?

Yes. Twelve hours of light and twelve of dark, on a timer, is the simplest reliable setup. Avoid coloured night bulbs; they still emit visible light and disturb sleep. If the room drops below 68 °F at night, a thermostat-controlled mat is the correct answer.
