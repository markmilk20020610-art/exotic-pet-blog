Do Leopard Geckos Need UVB — or Just a Heat Mat?

The honest answer is more interesting than either camp admits: leopard geckos are crepuscular, not nocturnal, and a low-level UVB zone changes how you supplement calcium.

A leopard gecko's warm-end floor sits at 88-92 °F (31-33 °C) and the cool end at 72-77 °F (22-25 °C), and almost every mistake keepers make with this species traces back to confusing light with heat. Those are two separate inputs an animal needs, and a heat mat only delivers one of them. The UVB question is really a question about what your gecko can manufacture for itself once the lamp is off.

The keeper's diary

I had a nine-year-old male, a big lavender-phase animal, who lived his whole first winter with me under a mat and nothing else. At 9:40 one evening I found him on the cool-side slate with his eyes half shut and his tail tip barely twitching when I touched it, and I had the infrared gun out before I had my shoes off — 68 °F at the far corner, 96 °F over the mat. That 4-degree overshoot on one end and 4-degree undershoot on the other is what a cheap thermostat and a bare mat will do. His appetite had been flat for eleven days. I put a low-output tube and a dimmed heat source over him the following week, and he started taking food again inside a fortnight, though whether that was the light or the corrected gradient I will never be able to tell you for certain. That uncertainty is the honest answer.

What UVB actually does for a leopard gecko

Leopard geckos (Eublepharis macularius) are crepuscular — active around dusk and dawn — and wild ones spend daylight hours in burrows or under rock. That is the honest argument on both sides of this hobby fight. Keepers who skip UVB point at the burrow behaviour. Keepers who use it point out that even a crepuscular animal gets some filtered light at the burrow mouth, and that no one has measured wild exposure in a way I would call settled science.

What is not contested: the animal needs vitamin D3 to pull calcium from the gut, and it can either make D3 in its skin from UVB or get it from food. Both routes work. Neither is free of risk.

  • No UVB route: you supply D3 in the dust, so every feed must be dusted correctly and stored correctly, and you carry all the dosing risk yourself.
  • UVB route: the animal makes its own as needed, but only if the lamp output and the distance to the basking spot are right — a weak tube at 18 inches delivers almost nothing worth measuring.

The wavelength that matters is 290-320 nm, the UVB band; anything below about 290 nm is the part you want shielded by the mesh or the tube's own coating. Use a low-to-moderate output tube rated for a forest or shaded species, mounted over the warm side, and check the manufacturer's distance chart — most of them want 8-12 inches of clearance, and they decay in output over 6-12 months even while still glowing.

Heat and light are two jobs, not one

A heat mat heats the floor and nothing above it. A lamp heats the floor and raises the air, and something has to hold the air volume steady overnight. My approach, and the one I would hand a new keeper:

  1. Set the warm-side floor at 88-92 °F (31-33 °C) with a dimming thermostat probe laid flat on the substrate over the mat.
  2. Run an overhead lamp for daytime light on the warm side, dimmed on its own thermostat, so the air doesn't cook while the floor stays correct.
  3. Keep the cool end at 72-77 °F (22-25 °C) at all times — if you cannot find that gradient, the enclosure is too small, not the thermostat too weak.
  4. Drop the whole enclosure to a nighttime floor of 70-75 °F (21-24 °C). Leopard geckos benefit from a real night drop; a mat left running all night at full tilt does not give them one.
  5. Measure with a surface-temperature tool, not with your hand on the glass. Skin is a bad instrument.

Dusting, feeding and the D3 overlap

If you use UVB, don't also hammer every feed with high-dose D3 — you can overdo a good thing, and the signs of D3 excess are easy to miss until the animal is sick. I dust roughly one feed in two with a calcium-plus-D3 powder, and the rest with plain calcium, and I watch for the chalky white urate as my clumsy visible check. Pairs of adults get a feed every 3-4 days; growing juveniles more often.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep a leopard gecko with only a heat mat?

Yes, and many do. The animal gets its D3 from dusted food instead. The catch is that you must dust carefully and never skip it, and your warm-side floor still has to hit 88-92 °F (31-33 °C). A mat on an unregulated plug will not do that reliably.

Is a red night bulb safe for leopard geckos?

I don't use them. There's debate about whether geckos perceive red light, and I would rather not gamble a husbandry decision on unresolved biology. A dimmed heat source or a heat mat with a thermostat does the night job without putting visible light in a burrow-dwelling animal's enclosure.

How often should I replace my UVB tube?

Every 6-12 months for most tubes, regardless of whether it still glows. Output falls away before the light fails, and you can't judge that by eye. If you're serious, buy a UVB meter and check it; if you're not, put a recurring reminder on your phone and replace on schedule.
