Do Leopard Geckos Need UVB Light or Just Heat Mats? The Ultimate Lighting Guide

You brought home your first leopard gecko, set up a beautiful tank, and then jumped onto a forum only to be hit with a wave of conflicting advice. One keeper says UVB bulbs are essential, another swears by a simple heat mat, and a third insists that UVB will blind your gecko. If your head is spinning, take a deep breath. You are not alone.

This is one of the most debated topics in reptile husbandry, and the short answer is: leopard geckos do not strictly need UVB light to survive, but providing it correctly can significantly improve their health and welfare. In this guide, we will cut through the noise, explain the science of lighting and heating, and help you build a setup that actually mirrors nature.

The Heat Mat vs. UVB Debate: What Leopard Geckos Actually Need

Leopard geckos are crepuscular, meaning they are most active in the dim light of dawn and dusk. They are not basking lizards that sit out in the midday sun like a bearded dragon. This lifestyle has led to the widespread myth that they need zero lighting whatsoever. However, wild leopard geckos in Pakistan and India are exposed to filtered sunlight during their active hours.

The Case for Heat Mats

Leopard geckos are thigmothermic, which means they absorb heat from surfaces their bellies touch. A heat mat attached to the underside of the tank is excellent for providing the vital belly heat required for digestion.

  • Provides 24/7 heat, even at night when temperatures drop.
  • Encourages natural basking behavior (lying flat on the warm spot).
  • Essential for digesting food and preventing impaction.

The Case for UVB

While leopard geckos can survive without UVB by synthesizing vitamin D3 from dietary supplements, recent studies suggest that providing low-level UVB offers significant benefits:

  • Allows the gecko to self-regulate vitamin D3 production naturally.
  • Supports calcium metabolism and bone health more effectively than supplements alone.
  • Encourages natural behaviors and reduces the risk of Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD).

The Verdict: The best setup uses both. Use a heat mat for the warm hide to provide consistent belly heat, and add a low-output UVB bulb on a timer for a natural day/night cycle and optional D3 synthesis.

How to Set Up the Perfect Lighting and Heating System

Getting the balance right is key. Too much UVB can harm a crepuscular gecko, while too little heat causes digestive issues. Follow this step-by-step approach to build a safe habitat.

1. Choose the Right UVB Strength

Leopard geckos are sensitive to high UV levels. You need a low-output bulb, such as a T8 or T5 fluorescent tube in the 2% to 5% range (often labeled as "Tropical" or "Shade Dweller" strength). Avoid desert-strength bulbs (10%+), which are designed for basking species and can cause eye damage or burns in leopard geckos.

2. Position the UVB Correctly

  • Place the UVB tube on the cool side or spanning the middle of the tank.
  • Provide plenty of leaf cover and hides so the gecko can escape the light if it wants to.
  • Never place a UVB bulb directly next to the heat mat without a gradient, as this can overheat the animal.

3. Use a Heat Mat for Belly Heat

The heat mat should cover roughly one-third of the tank floor and be connected to a thermostat. The hot spot should be around 88-92°F (31-33°C), while the ambient air on the cool side stays around 75°F (24°C).

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Can I use a red light for my leopard gecko at night?

No. It is an outdated practice. Leopard geckos can see red and blue light, and it disrupts their natural day/night cycle. If you need night heat, use a Ceramic Heat Emitter (CHE) or a Deep Heat Projector connected to a thermostat. These emit heat without any visible light.

Will my leopard gecko get Metabolic Bone Disease without UVB?

Not necessarily, provided you are dusting their food with a high-quality calcium + D3 supplement. However, relying solely on supplements puts the burden of health on you. UVB allows the animal to regulate its own D3 production naturally, reducing the risk of both deficiency and overdose. It is a safer, more natural insurance policy.

Do leopard geckos need a light at night?

No. They need a distinct drop in temperature and complete darkness at night to mimic their natural environment. Unless your room temperature drops below 65°F (18°C) at night, you can turn off all lights and heating (except the heat mat, which should stay on).

How often should I replace my UVB bulb?

Even if the bulb still lights up, the UVB output degrades over time. You should replace your UVB tube every 6 to 12 months, depending on the brand. Set a reminder on your phone so your gecko doesn't go without essential UV rays.
