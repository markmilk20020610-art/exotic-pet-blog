How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

A plump abdomen is a better calendar than any schedule. Here is what adult jumping spiders actually eat, how often, and the two feeding mistakes that shorten their lives.

You brought home a spider the size of a fingernail, and it has been watching you ever since. Now the question that every new keeper asks: is it hungry? The answer matters more than most care sheets admit, because adult jumping spiders are far more likely to be overfed than underfed — and overfeeding is the quieter, slower problem.

What adult jumping spiders actually eat

Jumping spiders are visual hunters. They do not build webs to catch dinner; they stalk, triangulate, and pounce. That means prey has to move. A dead insect will be ignored, then mould.

  • Blue bottle or house flies — the best enrichment food. Fast, and they trigger a full hunting sequence.
  • Small crickets (4–6 mm) — a reliable staple, ideally gut-loaded for 24 hours first.
  • Hydei fruit flies — small, but useful for variety and for adults that are slowing down.
  • Mealworms — occasional only. High in fat, and they burrow if uneaten.
  • Waxworms — a treat. Think of them as the spider equivalent of cake.

Size is the rule that matters most: prey should be no longer than the spider's body, legs excluded. Oversized crickets are not a challenge, they are an injury risk.

How often to feed an adult

Every two to four days, one or two appropriately sized prey items. That is the whole schedule. Adults have a far slower metabolism than spiderlings, and a spider that eats daily will become obese within a couple of months.

Three things move you within that range:

  • Abdomen shape. Plump and oval is correct. Flat and pinched means feed now. Stretched and balloon-like means skip several days.
  • Sex. Gravid females eat more often and should not be rationed.
  • Season. Activity and appetite drop in cooler months, even indoors.
Feed the abdomen, not the calendar. A jumping spider's body condition is visible from across the room — use it.

The two mistakes that actually cause harm

1. Leaving live prey in the enclosure. A cricket left overnight will chew on a resting or moulting spider. Remove anything uneaten within 24 hours. This single habit prevents most captive injuries.

2. Feeding straight through a moult. When a spider stops eating and seals itself into a silk hammock, it is in premoult. Do not feed, do not open the enclosure more than necessary. Wait until it is actively hunting again — usually five to ten days after the moult — before offering prey.

Frequently asked questions

Can I feed my jumping spider dead insects?

No. Jumping spiders hunt by sight and movement. Dead prey is ignored, then spoils. The exception is pre-killed prey offered on tongs with a slight wiggle, which some captives will take — but live food is the default.

My spider stopped eating. Is it dying?

Usually not. Refusing food for a week or two is normal before a moult, and adult females also fast around egg-laying. Check the abdomen first; if it is still plump and the spider is alert, wait.

How much water does it need?

Very little. A shallow cap of water plus a light mist on one side of the enclosure every second or third day is enough. Never mist the spider directly.
