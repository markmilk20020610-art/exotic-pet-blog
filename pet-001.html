How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider and What Do They Eat?

You've just brought home your first jumping spider, and the excitement is real — until the panic sets in. Should you toss in a cricket right now? Was that fruit fly enough? Is your spider starving, or are you about to overfeed it into oblivion? If you've found yourself second-guessing every feeding decision, you're not alone. The two most common questions new keepers ask are simple but critical: how often should you feed an adult jumping spider, and what do they eat? Get these wrong, and you risk anything from malnutrition to a refused meal and a very confused keeper. In this guide, we'll break down exactly what and when to feed your adult jumper, with practical schedules you can follow starting today.

What Do Adult Jumping Spiders Actually Eat?

Jumping spiders are obligate carnivores and active hunters — they don't scavenge dead prey, and they won't touch commercial pellets or powders. In the wild, they stalk and pounce on live insects, and in captivity you need to replicate that live-prey experience. The key is matching feeder size to the spider's body length; a general rule of thumb is that prey should be no larger than the spider's abdomen, and ideally around half their body size for easier takedowns.

Best Live Feeder Insects

  • Fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster or hydei) — staple food for smaller adult species and a great "snack" option.
  • Crickets (pinhead to small) — excellent nutrition for medium to large species like Phidippus regius; gut-load them first for maximum value.
  • House flies and blue bottle flies — highly stimulating prey that trigger strong feeding responses.
  • Waxworms and small mealworms — treat feeders; high in fat, so use sparingly (no more than once every 1–2 weeks).
  • Bean beetles and small roach nymphs — good variety options for keepers who want to rotate feeders.

What to Avoid

  • Dead or freeze-dried insects (jumpers ignore them).
  • Overly large crickets that can injure or stress your spider.
  • Wild-caught insects from pesticide-sprayed areas — a real risk to your spider's health.

How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

This is where most beginners go wrong. Adult jumping spiders have slower metabolisms than spiderlings, and overfeeding is far more common than underfeeding in captivity. A healthy adult jumper should be fed roughly 2–3 times per week, with 1–2 appropriately sized prey items per feeding. Some keepers feed every other day; others do a "feed, then rest" cycle. The best approach is to watch your spider's abdomen — it should look plump but not stretched or balloon-like.

Signs Your Feeding Schedule Is Working

  • Plump, rounded abdomen — a well-fed jumper's abdomen is visibly filled out but not distended.
  • Eager feeding response — an active stalk and pounce at mealtime means your spider is hungry and healthy.
  • Refused meals — if your spider ignores prey, it's likely full or preparing to molt. Don't force it.
  • Shriveled or sunken abdomen — a sign of underfeeding or dehydration; increase feeding frequency and mist the enclosure.

Adjusting for Molting and Pre-Molt

Jumping spiders stop eating in the days before molting, sometimes for a week or more. Remove uneaten prey during this period to prevent stress or injury, and never disturb a spider that's mid-molt. After a successful molt, wait 24–48 hours before feeding again — the new exoskeleton needs time to harden.

Setting Up the Perfect Feeding Environment

Feeding success isn't just about what and when — it's about where. Jumping spiders need a secure, well-ventilated enclosure with anchor points for climbing and pouncing. A stressed spider in an empty deli cup will refuse food, no matter how perfect your schedule is. Humidity matters too: mist lightly every few days (never soaking the substrate), and always provide a small water source or droplets on the enclosure walls, since dehydration is a leading cause of appetite loss in captive jumpers.

Pro tip: feed with tongs or by dropping prey directly into the enclosure near your spider, and never leave uneaten crickets in overnight — hungry crickets can nibble on a resting spider.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I feed my adult jumping spider dead insects?

No. Jumping spiders are visual hunters that rely on movement to trigger a feeding response. Dead, freeze-dried, or canned insects will typically be ignored and can rot in the enclosure. Always offer live prey.

How do I know if I'm overfeeding my jumping spider?

Look at the abdomen. A well-fed adult jumper's abdomen should be plump but proportionate to its body. If it looks stretched, shiny, or balloon-like, cut back feeding frequency. Overfed spiders are more prone to molting issues and reduced activity.

How long can an adult jumping spider go without food?

Healthy adults can safely fast for 1–2 weeks, especially during pre-molt. However, they still need hydration — keep misting the enclosure. If your spider refuses food for more than two weeks without signs of molting, check temperature, humidity, and enclosure stress factors.

With the right feeders and a sensible 2–3 times per week schedule, your adult jumper will stay active, hunt enthusiastically, and live out its full lifespan. Watch the abdomen, respect the molt, and you'll have a thriving little predator on your hands.
