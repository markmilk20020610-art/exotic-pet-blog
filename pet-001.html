How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

A plump abdomen is a better calendar than any schedule. Here is what adult jumping spiders actually eat, how often, and the two feeding mistakes that shorten their lives.

A regal jumping spider (Phidippus regius) is a 12–18 mm animal, and at that body length its whole hunting economy runs on vision and timing rather than scent. Eyes that size commit the spider to a stalk-and-pounce, which means prey has to be alive, moving and close enough to trigger the leap, and it means feeding is a two-minute event, not a grazing session. Get the timing right and the abdomen stays plump and the moults come clean; get it wrong and you're either cleaning up dead crickets or looking at a spider that won't take anything.

One evening, one miss

I fed a subadult female on a Tuesday, 19:40, and got cocky. She'd taken two Hydei in a row the week before, so I dropped four into the cube at once and went to make tea. When I came back at 20:15 she was hanging off the mesh lid with a fly buzzing behind her and three more loose in the substrate. Two days later I found her abdomen noticeably thinner and a fat, healthy fly sitting on the cork bark like it paid rent. What it cost me was a rehouse, a tweezer-by-tweezer cleanup, and a week of paranoid watching. What it taught me: one item at a time, and stay in the room until the spider has either struck or turned away. She has not been fed a scatter since.

What a regal actually eats

Live invertebrates, sized to roughly half the spider's body length or smaller. In practice that means flightless fruit flies for slings, Hydei fruit flies and small crickets or roach nymphs for juveniles, and large Hydei or small crickets for adults. They are visual ambush hunters, so anything that sits still and plays dead in the corner is not food — it's a tenant.

Portion by item count, not by scoop. A useful working rule: offer one item, wait up to five minutes, and offer a second only if the first is taken. Stop at the point where the abdomen looks full and firm. Adult females in a breeding cycle and pre-moult spiders will refuse entirely, and that's normal.

Size the feeder to the spider, not to the calendar. A 12 mm adult can manage a small cricket but struggles with a large one, and a badly sized feeder is how spiders get injured rather than fed.

How often — the schedule that works

This is where hobby practice genuinely splits, and I'll give you the range I use rather than pretend there's one answer. For adult regals kept at 22–27 °C (72–80 °F):

  • Adult females, maintenance: 2–3 feeders, 2–3 times a week.
  • Adult males: 1–2 feeders, 2 times a week; they run leaner and overfeeding shortens their already short lives.
  • Pre-moult or post-moult: nothing until the exoskeleton hardens, typically 3–5 days after the moult.
  • Gravid females: feed on demand and watch the abdomen, not the clock.

The failure mode is overfeeding, not underfeeding. A chronically overfed spider gets a distended abdomen that drags, and I have lost animals to what looked like simple obesity. The checkable version: look at the abdomen from above. Plump and roughly as wide as the carapace is good. Wider than the carapace and glossy-stretched means skip a feed.

Water, humidity and the room

A spider this small loses water fast. Keep humidity around 60–70 % and mist one side of the enclosure lightly every second or third day — never soak the substrate, because stagnant wet substrate grows mould and mould kills spiders. A small water dish is optional and often ignored; the droplets on the mesh are what they actually drink.

Temperature matters more than most keepers admit. Below about 20 °C (68 °F) appetite collapses and digestion slows; much above 30 °C (86 °F) and you're pushing dehydration. Room temperature in a normal heated home usually lands in range. Measure it — a cheap digital thermometer stuck to the side of the cube is the whole instrument you need.

Frequently asked questions

Can an adult regal jumping spider go a week without food?

Yes, comfortably, provided it has water. A plump adult in good condition can go 7–10 days with no feeder and no ill effect, and many refuse food for longer before a moult. What it cannot do is go a week bone dry. Prioritise misting over feeding if you're away and someone is checking on the animal.

Should I remove uneaten feeders?

Always, and within a few hours. Live crickets left in an enclosure will chew on a resting or moulting spider, and fruit flies just breed in the substrate. If the spider hasn't struck within about ten minutes of an item going in, take it out and try again in two days rather than leaving it to chance.

Why has my spider stopped eating?

Three usual causes, in order of likelihood: it's approaching a moult, the temperature has dropped, or it's simply full. Check the abdomen first, then the thermometer, then look for the dull, stretched look of a pre-moult animal. If the abdomen is shrinking and none of those fit, that's a genuine problem and worth a vet with invertebrate experience.
