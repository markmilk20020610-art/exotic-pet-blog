How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider and What Do They Eat?

You've just brought home your first jumping spider. It's tiny, it's adorable, and it's watching you with those big, curious eyes. Then the panic sets in: Is it hungry? Did I feed it too much? What do I even feed it? If you're second-guessing yourself, you're not alone—feeding schedules are one of the most common worries for new keepers. The good news? Jumping spiders are forgiving, hardy little hunters, and once you understand their feeding rhythm, caring for them becomes second nature. In this guide, we'll break down exactly how often you should feed an adult jumping spider and what they eat, so you can keep your eight-legged buddy thriving.

What Do Adult Jumping Spiders Eat? A Complete Prey Guide

Jumping spiders (family Salticidae) are active, visual hunters. They don't build webs to catch food—they stalk and pounce. That means they need live prey that moves, so they can trigger their hunting instinct. Here's what should be on the menu:

  • Fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster or hydei) – Perfect size for smaller adults and great for variety.
  • Crickets (pinhead or small) – A staple feeder; gut-loaded crickets offer excellent nutrition.
  • Mealworms and mini superworms – Offer occasionally; high in fat and best for larger species like Phidippus regius.
  • Waxworms – A tasty treat, but use sparingly due to high fat content.
  • Small roaches (like red runner nymphs) – Great for larger adult spiders and easy to keep.
  • Houseflies or blue bottle flies – Excellent enrichment and fast-moving prey for active hunters.

Pro tip: Prey should be roughly no larger than your spider's body length (excluding legs). Oversized prey can stress or even injure your spider. Always feed live insects—dead prey usually gets ignored, and it can mold quickly in the enclosure.

How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

Adult jumping spiders have slower metabolisms than spiderlings, so you don't need to feed them daily. A reliable baseline is every 2 to 4 days, but the exact frequency depends on a few factors:

1. Watch the Abdomen—Not Just the Calendar

The single best hunger indicator is your spider's abdomen. A well-fed jumping spider has a plump, rounded abdomen. If it looks flat or shriveled, it's time to feed. If it looks swollen like a tiny balloon, hold off for a few days. Overfeeding adults can lead to obesity, molting complications, and a shortened lifespan.

2. Adjust for Species and Size

  • Larger species (Phidippus audax, P. regius): 1–2 appropriately sized feeders every 3–4 days.
  • Smaller adults (Platycryptus or Habronattus): 2–3 fruit flies every 2–3 days.
  • Gravid females: Feed slightly more often to support egg production.

3. Respect Molting and Pre-Molt

Adults still molt, though less frequently. When your spider stops eating, hides in a silken retreat, or refuses prey, it's likely in pre-molt. Remove uneaten live feeders immediately—crickets can nibble on a molting spider—and don't feed again until the new exoskeleton hardens (usually 3–5 days post-molt).

4. Remove Uneaten Prey Within 24 Hours

Leftover feeders stress your spider and foul the enclosure. If a feeder is still roaming after a day, take it out and try again in a couple of days with a smaller prey item.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I feed my adult jumping spider dead insects?

Generally, no. Jumping spiders are triggered by movement, so dead prey is usually ignored. A dead insect left in the enclosure also molds quickly and attracts pests. Stick to live feeders—it's safer and far more enriching for your spider.

How do I know if my jumping spider is hungry?

Look at the abdomen: a flat or thin abdomen signals hunger, while a plump, rounded one means your spider is well-fed. Behavioral cues help too—active pacing and tracking movements often mean it's hunting time.

How long can an adult jumping spider go without food?

Healthy adults can safely go 7–14 days without food, and longer during pre-molt. Water is more critical than food—keep misting regularly. If your spider refuses food for more than two weeks outside of a molt, check your temperature, humidity, and prey size.

With the right prey, a flexible schedule, and a well-designed enclosure, feeding your adult jumping spider becomes one of the most rewarding parts of keeping these charismatic little hunters. Happy feeding!
