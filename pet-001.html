How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

A plump abdomen is a better calendar than any schedule. Here is what adult jumping spiders actually eat, how often, and the two feeding mistakes that shorten their lives.

An adult regal jumping spider (Phidippus regius) measures 12-18 mm in body length depending on sex — females at the top of that range, males nearer the bottom — and that small body sets the feeding clock. A spider that size has a gut roughly the volume of a grain of rice, so every meal is a large fraction of its body mass and every fast is a real metabolic event. Feed too often and the abdomen distends past the carapace like an overfilled balloon; feed too rarely and the abdomen shrinks to a flat wedge behind the eyes. Reading which one you have is the entire skill.

The keeper's diary

The evening I learned this, the clock said 19:40 and my adult female was sitting on the cork bark with her abdomen visibly pinched behind the cephalothorax — the fused head-and-chest plate. I had been feeding her three crickets a week on a schedule I'd carried over from a juvenile, and she'd taken every one. That was the mistake: she took them because they were there, not because she needed them. She was sluggish on the glass, dropping a leg when she misjudged a jump. I backed off to one medium feeder every four days, weighed nothing, measured nothing — just watched the abdomen. Two moults later she was plump and fast again. The cost was four weeks of a spider I'd made slow through kindness.

What regal jumping spiders actually eat

They are active visual hunters, not web-trap sitters. In the wild that means flies, small moths, leafhoppers, and anything with a body soft enough to be subdued by a bite to the back of the head. In captivity the diet is live insects, and it must be live — P. regius hunts movement, and dead prey is usually ignored entirely. Suitable size is anything up to roughly the spider's own body length, but err small.

  • Fruit flies — ideal for adults as a staple; small, active, and hard to overfeed.
  • Crickets — one medium cricket is a full meal for an adult. Not three.
  • Waxworm or mealworm pieces — occasional treat, high fat.

Dusting matters less than for reptiles, but a light calcium or gut-loaded diet for the feeder insects does no harm. The cheapest reliable feeding routine is a live fruit fly culture you keep going yourself, so there is always something small on hand.

How often to feed an adult

The honest answer in the hobby runs from one feeder every two days to one every five, and I won't pretend the evidence is settled. What I use: one appropriately sized feeder every three to four days for an adult female, every four to five days for an adult male. Males eat less and live shorter lives; that is not a husbandry fault.

The checkable signal is the abdomen, not the calendar. Look down at the spider from above:

  1. Abdomen distinctly wider than the cephalothorax and taut — skip the feed, try again in three days.
  2. Abdomen roughly the same width as the cephalothorax — feed now.
  3. Abdomen narrower than the cephalothorax, flat sides — feed now and check your temperatures.

The failure mode when you overfeed is a distended, slow spider that is more prone to dragging its abdomen and, in my experience, moults awkwardly. Underfeeding shows as a wedged, shrunken abdomen and a spider that stops hunting.

Water, temperature and the moult

Jumping spiders drink. A light mist on one wall of the enclosure every second or third day, allowed to dry between, is enough — they take droplets, not baths. Keep the enclosure in the low-to-mid 20s °C (roughly 72-80 °F); below about 20 °C appetite drops off and the abdomen check stops being reliable. An arboreal cube with vertical surfaces to climb and a small retreat is the whole habitat requirement.

Pre-moult changes everything. A spider building a thick sealed hammock and refusing food for a week or more is moulting, not starving. Do not open the retreat and do not drop feeders in. Wait. Offer food again two or three days after the old exoskeleton is out.

Frequently asked questions

Can I feed my adult regal jumping spider dead crickets?

Generally no. P. regius is a pursuit hunter triggered by movement, and a motionless insect usually goes unnoticed or ignored. There are anecdotal reports of take on freshly killed prey with a wiggle, but it is unreliable. Use live feeders, size-matched, and remove anything uneaten within a day.

How do I know my spider is too fat?

Look at it from above. If the abdomen is much wider than the cephalothorax and looks stretched and shiny, skip the next two feeds and recheck in a week. A healthy adult sits with the abdomen roughly equal to slightly wider than the carapace. Distension is gradual; weigh-by-eye is a fine standard here.

My spider has refused food for a week — is it dying?

Not necessarily. Refusal plus a sealed web retreat usually means pre-moult, which can take seven to fourteen days in adults. Check temperature first; below 20 °C appetite falls sharply. If the abdomen is still plump and the spider is active, wait. If the abdomen is shrunken and it is lethargic outside a retreat, that is worth attention.
