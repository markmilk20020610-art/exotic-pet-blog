How Often Should You Feed an Adult Jumping Spider?

A plump abdomen is a better calendar than any schedule. Here is what adult jumping spiders actually eat, how often, and the two feeding mistakes that shorten their lives.

An adult Phidippus regius female measures 12-18 mm body length and hunts inside a 24-28 °C window in the wild, and that temperature band governs almost everything about how often you feed her. A salticid (jumping spider) has no stomach to fill — it has a diverticulum — so a meal is less a daily event than a small, infrequent deposit.

My Monday, and the cricket that got away with it

Last Monday I opened the lid of a female's enclosure at 7.40 in the evening to drop in a large cricket. She had moulted ten days before. The cricket was 18 mm, unfed, and I knew it was too big. I dropped it in anyway. For six seconds she tracked it, then the cricket kicked a disc in the acrylic and she bolted to the top corner and stayed there with legs pulled in, abdomen flattened against the wall. That is a stress posture and I had made it. I fished the cricket out with tongs, left her alone, and fed a Hydei culture the following night instead. She took two flies without hesitation. The lesson cost me one wasted cricket. It was not the money that stung, it was the eight extra days she spent hiding.

What they actually eat — and why size beats quantity

Regals are visual ambush predators. They eat live, moving prey, and they assess size before they decide to commit. Anything close to the spider's own body length is a risk to her; anything around 40-60 % of it is comfortable and easily subdued.

My feeder list, in order of how often I actually use them:

  • Fruit flies — perfect for juveniles and for adults in the first days after a moult
  • Small crickets, 5-8 mm, as the staple protein for juveniles
  • Medium crickets, 10-12 mm, for adult females and the occasional large male
  • Bottle-fly larvae (Phoenix worms) — rarely, and only by hand

Feed crickets a gut-load diet for 24-48 hours before offering them. A cricket fed on dry bran is mostly bran. The nutrient density of the cricket, not the spider's appetite, decides how much of the meal actually reaches her.

How often — the only honest answer

For an adult female, I feed every 4-7 days during warm weather. Drop below 20 °C and she may take three weeks to eat at all; she is ectothermic and her digestion runs on the ambient temperature. Adult males eat less often — every 7-10 days is my rule.

The abdomen is the instrument. A female in good condition carries an abdomen roughly 1.5 times the width of her carapace. If it's noticeably plumper, skip the feed; if it's a thin wedge, feed. I have seen keepers settle for "she looks hungry", which is not a measurement.

What is not known: nobody has published a satiation curve for this species. I am working from ten years of animals and a scale. The acceptance signal is movement — if she ignores a correctly-sized, moving feeder twice, she is either close to a moult or already full.

The moult problem, and why feeding schedules go wrong

A moulting spider cannot eat. The cuticle is being dissolved from underneath, and the old exoskeleton becomes rigid; the mouthparts are not functional. Feeding during a premoult leaves dangerous live prey in the enclosure. After the moult, the new cuticle is soft and takes two to three days to harden. This is why the schedule breaks.

My rule: the moment the spider stops hunting, refuse all prey, lower the ambient to the cool end of the range, and cut humidity to 60-65 % until the moult completes. Then wait 48 hours past the final cast-off before offering the smallest feeder you have. A 5 mm cricket is not too small for a post-moult adult.

Frequently asked questions

Can I leave live crickets in the enclosure between feeds?

No. A hungry cricket will nibble a moulting or resting spider, and a moulting spider cannot defend herself. I feed only what she will take in one sitting, then remove anything left after two hours. This also teaches her that the tongs mean food, which makes handling far easier.

Do adult jumping spiders need calcium or vitamin powders?

Not in the same way a reptile does. A spider's exoskeleton is chitin, not bone, and the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio debate is not one that translates. The gut-load of the cricket matters far more. Dust only if a vet has specifically recommended it; otherwise feed well-fed insects and let the animal do the work.

Why is my adult refusing food for weeks?

Three common causes: premoult, temperature below 20 °C, or a recent stressor like handling. Check the abdomen first — a fat spider refusing food is normal. Check the temperature next. If both look right and she is still refusing after ten days with correct prey, consult an exotics vet.
