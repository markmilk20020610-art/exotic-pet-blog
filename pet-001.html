How Often Should You Feed a Adult Jumping Spider and What Do They Eat? The Complete Feeding Guide

You've just brought home your first jumping spider, you're completely captivated by those big, curious eyes—and then the panic sets in. How often should you feed a adult jumping spider and what do they eat? Do you offer food every day? Once a week? And what on earth counts as a proper meal for a creature smaller than your thumbnail? Overfeeding is one of the most common beginner mistakes, and it can shorten your spider's lifespan or cause dangerous molting problems. The good news: feeding a jumping spider is simpler than you think once you understand a few core principles. In this guide, we'll break down exactly what adult jumping spiders eat, how often to feed them, how to spot a hungry spider, and how to avoid the most common feeding pitfalls.

What Do Adult Jumping Spiders Eat? Building the Perfect Menu

Jumping spiders (Salticidae) are active, visually hunting predators. Unlike web-builders that wait for prey to come to them, your spider stalks, pounces, and wrestles its food—which is half the fun of keeping one. In captivity, adult jumping spiders are almost exclusively fed live insects. Here's what works best:

  • Flightless fruit flies (Drosophila hydei) — the gold standard staple. Easy to culture, readily available, and perfectly sized for most adults.
  • Small crickets (pinhead to ⅛ inch for adults of larger species like Phidippus regius) — great for variety and enrichment.
  • Waxworms and small mealworms — excellent occasional treats for putting on weight, but too fatty for daily feeding.
  • Small roaches (red runner nymphs) — a nutritious option for keepers comfortable with roach colonies.
  • Bean beetles and small moths — good enrichment feeders that stimulate natural hunting behavior.

As a general rule, prey should be no larger than your spider's abdomen—ideally about half its body length. A live prey item that's too big can injure or even kill a spider during a struggle. And never feed dead prey: jumping spiders rely on movement to trigger their hunting instinct, so a motionless insect will simply be ignored.

Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

Feeding frequency changes dramatically as a spider grows. Adults have slower metabolisms than spiderlings and need far less food. Here's a quick reference:

  • Spiderlings (L1–L4): Feed every 1–2 days with appropriately sized prey.
  • Juveniles (L5–subadult): Feed every 2–3 days.
  • Adult females: Feed every 3–5 days; more frequently when producing eggs.
  • Adult males: Feed every 5–7 days—males have lower body mass and a shorter lifespan, and overfeeding accelerates their decline.

How Often Should You Feed a Adult Jumping Spider? Reading the Signs

So, how often should you feed a adult jumping spider? The short answer is every 3 to 7 days, depending on the species, sex, and body condition. But the most reliable feeding guide isn't a calendar—it's your spider's abdomen. A well-fed jumping spider has a plump, rounded abdomen. An overly fat one looks almost balloon-like, which signals you should back off. A hungry spider, meanwhile, will be active, roaming the enclosure, and will readily pounce on prey.

Signs Your Spider Is Hungry

  • Actively pacing or exploring the enclosure walls
  • Holding its front legs raised in a "ready" hunting posture
  • Visibly thinner, shrunken abdomen
  • Aggressively tracking and lunging at movement outside the enclosure

Signs You're Overfeeding

  • A distended, balloon-like abdomen that looks ready to burst
  • Refusing food and retreating from prey
  • Lethargy and hiding (a precursor to molting—stop feeding immediately)
  • Prolonged fasting can be normal, but in adults it sometimes signals premolt

One important nuance: never feed a spider that is about to molt. A molting jumping spider is vulnerable and immobile, and live prey left in the enclosure can injure or kill it. Remove all feeders during the molt and wait a few days after the old exoskeleton is shed before resuming feeding.

Creating the Ideal Feeding Environment: Setup, Hydration & Escape-Proofing

How you feed matters as much as what you feed. Jumping spiders are notorious escape artists—their ability to squeeze through tiny gaps is legendary—so a secure, well-ventilated enclosure is non-negotiable. Beyond the basics, here's what to prioritize:

  • Hydration is critical: Provide a small water source. Mist one side of the enclosure lightly 2–3 times a week, and offer a shallow bottle cap with a cotton ball or a few small stones so your spider can drink without drowning.
  • Enrichment promotes natural hunting: Add a few twigs, cork bark, and silk plants. A spider that can climb, perch, and stalk will feed more readily than one in a bare box.
  • Remove uneaten prey: Crickets and other feeders left overnight can chew on a resting spider. Feed one item at a time and remove anything uneaten after 24 hours.
  • Keep feeders well-fed: Gut-load your crickets and dust them lightly with calcium if your spider is a species prone to molting issues.

Getting all of this right is dramatically easier with the right habitat from day one. A purpose-built setup saves you endless DIY trial and error—and gives your spider room to hunt the way nature intended.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I feed my adult jumping spider dead or freeze-dried insects?

Generally, no. Jumping spiders are visual hunters that need movement to trigger their predatory response. Freeze-dried or dead prey is usually ignored entirely. Stick to live, appropriately sized insects—flightless fruit flies and small crickets are your best bets.

How long can an adult jumping spider go without food?

Healthy adults can comfortably survive 1–2 weeks without food, and they often fast voluntarily before molting or laying eggs. However, they cannot go without water for long. Always keep a small, safe water source available and mist the enclosure regularly.

Is it okay to feed my spider the same insect every time?

It's acceptable in a pinch, but variety is better. Rotating between fruit flies, crickets, and an occasional waxworm or mealworm provides broader nutrition and keeps feeding exciting and enriching for your spider. Think of fruit flies as the dependable staple and other insects as supplements that round out the diet.

Feed to your spider's abdomen, not the calendar, respect the molt, and keep the enclosure secure and hydrated—do those three things and you'll have a thriving, long-lived companion. Happy keeping!
