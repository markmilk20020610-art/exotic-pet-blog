Pacman Frog Setup: Why the Substrate Is a Chemical Filter, Not Decoration

An amphibian drinks through its skin, so the substrate is not decoration. It is the water supply, the filter and the infection risk in one layer.

A Pacman frog (Ceratophrys cranwelli) has no diaphragm, no chest cavity worth the name, and skin that absorbs water and dissolved ions directly from whatever it is sitting on. That single fact turns the substrate question from a matter of taste into a matter of chemistry: at 70–85 % relative humidity and 24–28 °C, a waterlogged 8–10 cm layer of coconut fibre is a functioning biological filter, and a sour, compacted one is a bath of ammonia in direct contact with an animal that drinks through its belly. Most of the "mystery" deaths in this species happen in the second kind of tub.

The tub that smelled like a pond

I kept a juvenile in a 45 × 45 × 30 cm tub on 10 cm of coco-fibre, misting twice a day because every care sheet said twice a day. By week three the frog had stopped burrowing, sat in the corner with its hind legs splayed, and had a faintly sweet smell I could not place. I changed the substrate and it came back within four days — then went off again a week later. What I had built was not a humid enclosure, it was a swamp: the bottom four centimetres were anaerobic, the drainage was nonexistent, and the ammonia reading at substrate level was somewhere north of 4 ppm. The fix was unglamorous. A 2 cm leca drainage layer, a mesh divider, 8 cm of fresh fibre, and misting measured with a hygrometer instead of a schedule. He has burrowed every night since.

How amphibian skin turns a dirty substrate into a systemic problem

Amphibian skin is not a barrier; it is an exchange surface, densely supplied with capillaries and used for respiration, osmoregulation and ion uptake. Nitrogenous waste — ammonia from the frog's own urine and from uneaten prey rotting in the fibre — dissolves in the film of water on the skin and crosses it. Ammonia at low concentrations burns the epidermis; at higher ones it drives the systemic load that presents as lethargy, splayed posture and the reddening of the hind limbs that keepers call red-leg, which is usually a Aeromonas or Pseudomonas septicaemia riding on top of a husbandry failure. This is why a Pacman frog can look perfectly healthy and be sitting in the cause of its own decline for a fortnight.

The same absorptive surface is why the water bowl matters more than its size suggests. Dechlorinated water, changed daily, deep enough to cover the vent but shallow enough to stand in — 2–3 cm for an adult. Chlorinated tap water left to stand overnight is not dechlorinated; chloramine does not off-gas, and a dechlorinator is a two-pound bottle that removes the whole question.

The ammonia row is the one nobody tests and the one that explains most of the failures. A cheap aquarium ammonia test on water squeezed from the bottom of the substrate, once a week, tells you when to change it far more reliably than a calendar.

Building the tub so it stays clean

  1. Drainage first. 2 cm of leca or gravel, then a mesh divider. Without it the fibre sits in its own runoff and goes anaerobic within a fortnight.
  2. Eight to ten centimetres of coco-fibre, damp enough to clump in the fist without a drop coming out. That is roughly 60 % water by weight and it is the whole humidity trick.
  3. One side warm, one side cool. A thermostat-controlled mat under a third of the base, never the whole thing, so the frog can choose 24 °C or 27 °C.
  4. Full substrate change every four to six weeks, spot-clean daily. A bioactive setup with springtails can stretch that, but only if the drainage layer is real.
  5. Feed out of the substrate. Tong-feed or use a smooth feeding dish. A cricket that dies buried in damp fibre is an ammonia source you will never see.

Do those five things and the species becomes almost boringly hardy, which is the reputation it deserves and rarely gets.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use potting soil instead of coco-fibre?

Only if it is guaranteed free of fertiliser, perlite and wetting agents, which most retail compost is not. The risk is not worth the saving: a single bag with slow-release fertiliser beads will kill an amphibian through its skin within days. Coco-fibre is cheap, consistent and inert, and there is no strong reason to look further.

My frog has not burrowed for a week. Is that normal?

Sometimes — a frog that is about to shed or that has just eaten heavily will sit out. But if it is sitting exposed, splayed and dull, check the substrate temperature first and the ammonia second. Both push a Ceratophrys out of the substrate, and both are fixable within an hour.

How often should I mist?

When the hygrometer says so, not on a schedule. In a sealed tub with a mesh vent, 70–85 % typically holds for two to three days after a heavy misting. Mist the substrate, not the frog, and never to the point where water pools at the bottom.
