Pacman Frog Setup: Why the Substrate Is a Chemical Filter, Not Decoration

An amphibian drinks through its skin, so the substrate is not decoration. It is the water supply, the filter and the infection risk in one layer.

An adult Ceratophrys cranwelli on substrate at 72-75 °F (22-24 °C) and 70-85 % relative humidity digests a mouse in about three days; at 68 °F (20 °C) it takes six and sits half-buried in between. The substrate is where the frog spends 23 hours out of 24, and its moisture content is what drives the humidity in the air above it.

The night the substrate went sour

I set up a 45 × 45 × 30 cm tank for a juvenile cranwelli in February, coco fibre rehydrated to what I judged "damp but not wet" — squeezed it, watched for drips, satisfied myself. Three weeks later the frog stopped eating. Then the smell arrived: ammoniacal, sharp at the back corner where the water bowl had been dribbling. I pulled the substrate out in one block and the bottom two centimetres were black and matted. I'd been misting from above, so the surface read 78 % while the base was anaerobic. The parasite screen afterwards cost more than the frog. I now mix the block with a measured volume of water and read a second hygrometer at soil level.

What the substrate is actually doing

Coco fibre is a cellulose matrix with enormous surface area per gram, which is why it holds water and why it holds everything dissolved in that water — urea, ammonia, bacterial waste. A frog on saturated organic substrate is sitting in a weak solution of its own nitrogenous waste. The chemistry is the fish-tank one: ammonia (NH₃) oxidises to nitrite (NO₂⁻), then nitrate (NO₃⁻). In a closed humid box with no drainage and no flow, that second step stalls and ammonia accumulates instead of cycling.

The fix isn't more misting. It's drainage and depth: 5-7 cm of substrate over a 2 cm gravel drainage layer, kept top-dry to bottom-damp rather than uniformly wet. The surface should feel like a wrung-out sponge; the base should clump but not pool.

Setting the numbers

Target 70-85 % humidity in the air column, dipping briefly to 60 % after a mist so the skin dries between cycles. Adults want 72-78 °F (22-26 °C) at the warm end and a 4-6 °C drop at the cool end. Juveniles under 6 cm snout-vent length need the same temperatures but only 3-4 cm of substrate — a small frog can't dig out of a deep wet layer, so it just sits in it.

  1. Rehydrate one coco fibre block in 3-4 litres of dechlorinated water; let it stand 20 minutes.
  2. Squeeze a handful. It should hold together and release two or three drops, no more.
  3. Pack the drainage layer, then the substrate, pressed down lightly — loose substrate lets the frog sink.
  4. Put one hygrometer probe at surface level and one at mid-depth; read both daily for a fortnight.
  5. Replace the whole substrate every 4-6 weeks, or immediately if you smell ammonia at any depth.

Run heat from an under-tank mat covering one-third of the base, on a dimming thermostat, probe on the substrate surface over the mat, target 76 °F (24 °C). Wet substrate over a controlled mat evaporates upward and holds humidity without constant misting.

Reading the frog, not just the gauge

A cranwelli in good substrate sits with its eyes above the surface, back half buried, and strikes at prey the moment it moves. Total burial with no feeding response usually means the substrate is too wet and cool. Skin is the other diagnostic: healthy skin is matte and slightly tacky, and slimy grey patches or a papery sheen mean too wet or too dry for too long. I've had both in one frog across a single winter; each time the fix was a moisture correction and a substrate change, not medication. Patches that persist past one shed cycle are a vet call.

Feeding behaviour reads temperature better than the thermostat does. At 70 °F (21 °C) a frog strikes and misses, or takes prey and spits it. At 76 °F (24 °C) it takes and swallows cleanly. If temperature is right and the refusal continues, check humidity — a frog on dry substrate refuses food before it shows any other sign.

Where keepers disagree

Bioactive cranwelli setups are contested. Springtails and dwarf isopods will colonise coco fibre, but a 5-7 cm layer in a humid box with a heavy frog that sits still for hours is poorly aerated, and the crew's vertical range is limited. I've stopped recommending full bioactive for this species and run plain coco fibre with complete replacement. Treat any crew as enrichment, keep the change schedule, and don't assume it's keeping pace. The long-term evidence is thin and most of what's online is anecdote.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a misting system instead of hand-misting?

Yes, but watch the depth. Automatic misters flood the base while the surface dries — exactly the wrong gradient. Run two short bursts a day and read a hygrometer at mid-depth before trusting it. Surface readings alone hide the problem for weeks.

How do I know when to replace the substrate?

On a fixed schedule — 4-6 weeks for adults, 3-4 for juveniles — plus a smell test at every change, digging down, not just looking. Ammonia is detectable by nose at low concentrations before it harms the frog. If in doubt, change it; substrate is cheaper than the vet.

Does depth matter for a frog that barely burrows?

Yes, for temperature as much as humidity. A shallow layer dries fast and sits close to the mat; a deep layer holds a stable gradient. I use 5-7 cm for adults even when the frog stays on the surface, because the depth keeps mid-layer humidity steady between mists.
