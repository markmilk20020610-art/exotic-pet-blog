How to Fix High Humidity and Mould in Isopod Cultures

Mould in an isopod tub is almost never a humidity problem. It is an airflow problem, and the fix takes five minutes and a drill.

Porcellio scaber — the common rough woodlouse — is a 12-18 mm animal that breathes through pleopods (flattened abdominal gills that must stay damp to work) and has no waxy layer on its cuticle to hold water in. That single fact explains almost every culture crash: the species needs 75-90 % relative humidity to keep those gills functioning, but the surface it stands on must not be wet enough to grow mould. Those two demands fight each other, and the keeper's job is to keep them separated in space.

My late colony, and what it taught me

I had a 12-litre tub of P. scaber going for two years, then moved it to the bottom shelf of a cupboard because the garage got cold. I didn't move the hygrometer with it. Six days later I opened the lid at 9 p.m. and the smell hit before the sight did — a wet, sweet, cellar smell. The bark hides were furred with grey mould and the whole tub felt heavy in a way that had nothing to do with the animals in it. I counted forty dead adults on the surface, and the ones still alive were pale and lying flat, gills splayed. The fault wasn't moisture. It was air: no vent, no gradient, and a lid that clipped shut like a lunchbox. I'd kept a wet box, not a culture.

Fix the air before you fix the water

Mould in an isopod tub is almost always a ventilation problem wearing a humidity costume. Standing water in the substrate, condensation on the lid, and no air exchange give you the same result: a wet, stale layer at the bottom and a dry layer at the top. Porcellio scaber wants the reverse — a moisture gradient it can walk along, choosing damp at one end and drier at the other.

  1. Measure, don't guess. Put a digital probe at substrate level, not taped to the lid. Aim for 75-90 % relative humidity in the damp third and 60-70 % in the dry third of the same tub. One number for the whole enclosure tells you nothing useful.
  2. Vent the lid, not the walls. Two or three 25 mm mesh discs across the top give you airflow without a draught across the floor. Cross-ventilation at substrate level dries the gills you're trying to protect.
  3. Cut back the water, not the depth. Keep 5-8 cm of substrate and damp only one end — pour water into that end until it darkens, then leave the other end alone. If water pools and sits, you've overdone it.
  4. Remove and replace, don't spray. Misting the surface feeds mould on the decor. Watering the substrate from below keeps the air humid and the surfaces dry.

If you have visible mould, do a full substrate change — don't spot-clean around it. Mould spores in a humid tub regrow within days. Replace the substrate, boil or discard the bark and leaf litter, and repopulate the clean tub with springtails before you add the isopods back, because springtails eat mould and fungus directly and outcompete it at the surface.

Run a moisture gradient you can actually check

The gradient is the whole system. Get it right and you rarely see mould; get it wrong and you're fighting the same bloom every fortnight. The table below is what I check on every tub, and what I do when the number is off.

A note on the humidity figures: hobby sources vary, and some keepers run drier than this without problems. The 75-90 % damp-end range is what I use because it keeps P. scaber gills working without soaking the decor. I can't tell you the exact threshold where mould always wins — nobody has measured it for this species — so treat the numbers as a starting point you tune against what you see.

When mould is already there

Sort the problem by where the mould is. Surface fuzz on decor means the air is too still; mould in the substrate means it's too wet; mould on dead animals means you're not finding them fast enough. Action order: remove and discard affected decor, do a full substrate change if any mould is below the surface, dry the tub to the dry-end target for 24 hours, then rebuild the gradient. Don't use anti-mould sprays or bleach near the animals — ventilation and replacement do the job without residue.

Add a springtail culture at the same time. They won't fix a flooded tub, but in a correctly vented one they keep the surface clean between your checks. Watch the isopods themselves, too: animals clustering on the lid or the dry end are telling you the substrate is too wet, and animals buried and pale are telling you it's too dry.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just leave the lid off to dry the tub out?

For a day, yes, with the tub somewhere cool and out of direct light. Longer than that and the damp end drops below 75 %, the gills dry out, and you trade mould for desiccation. Vent the lid properly instead of removing it, so you keep humid air in and still get exchange.

Will springtails get rid of mould on their own?

No. Springtails graze surface mould and fungus, and a healthy culture slows regrowth, but they can't fix standing water or dead air. Treat them as maintenance on a correctly vented tub, not as a cure for a wet one. Fix the ventilation and the water first, then add them.

Does high humidity always mean mould?

No — mould needs high humidity and still air and organic food on the surface. A well-vented tub at 80 % with dry decor and clean substrate won't bloom the way a sealed tub will. That's why I check ventilation before I touch the water, and why the surface should be dry to the touch even when the air is damp.
