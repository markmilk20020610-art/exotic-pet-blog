How to Fix High Humidity and Mould in Isopod Cultures

Mould in an isopod tub is almost never a humidity problem. It is an airflow problem, and the fix takes five minutes and a drill.

Relative humidity above 85 % in a Porcellio scaber culture is not a moisture problem — it is a ventilation problem wearing a moisture costume. At that figure, condensation films the bin walls overnight, and dormant mould spores get the one thing they need to break dormancy: liquid water sitting on a surface. The isopods themselves tolerate wet far better than keepers assume. What kills them is the anaerobic layer underneath, where leaf litter goes sour and oxygen falls away.

The bin that went sour

Twelve-litre bin on the shelf above my desk. Last November I opened it on a Tuesday evening and the surface was furred white. The lid had been tight since Wednesday — I remember because I'd refilled the spray bottle that morning and hadn't touched it since. The sour, wet-cardboard smell hit before I saw anything. Substrate was 5 cm deep and the top 2 cm was a mat of mycelium; underneath, leaves had collapsed into grey sludge. I lost roughly 40 adults out of 200. I had been misting twice a day and never opening the lid between mistings. Moisture went in and never came out.

Why wet bins mould

Mould needs three things: surface humidity above about 80 %, organic food, and still air. Isopod bins supply the first two reliably. The third is the one keepers can fix. A sealed tub with damp substrate is a humidity chamber — water goes in as mist and leaves only through gaps. Relative humidity climbs past 90 % and stays there. Mould then grows faster than a thin springtail population can process it, and the substrate beneath the surface loses oxygen as anaerobic bacteria replace the aerobic ones cycling the leaf litter.

Do not dry the bin out to solve this. P. scaber needs 70-85 % to moult cleanly; below 65 % you get stuck sheds on the rear segments, where the old cuticle is thickest. You want a wet side, a dry side, and enough air movement that condensation doesn't film the walls.

Building a bin that breathes

  1. Vent the lid properly. A 25 mm stainless mesh disc gives real airflow without the culture drying out in a day. Two discs per 12-litre bin.

  2. Stratify: 1 cm drainage (coarse gravel or charcoal), then 4-6 cm of leaf litter, compost and coco coir at 2:1:1. Water needs somewhere to go that isn't the leaf layer.
  3. Run a moisture gradient. Mist one third heavily, leave the other side dry to the touch. If every animal is pressed into the dry corner, the whole bin is too wet.
  4. Read it with a probe hygrometer, both ends, same day — stick-on dials drift and you can't calibrate what you can't see into.
  5. Seed a live temperate springtail culture. They graze new hyphae before it mats.

Skip ventilation and condensation drips back nightly, resetting the cycle. Skip drainage and the bottom 2 cm goes anaerobic while the surface still looks fine.

Life stage changes the band

Juveniles have thinner cuticle and lose water faster, so they tolerate a dry end far less than adults. Adults are 10-15 mm and breed in bins that would kill a young cohort — a setup that was safe for adults can fail the moment a juvenile boom hits it. Hold the wet end at 80-85 % and the dry end no lower than 65 % while young are present; adults can take a dry end down to 55 %. Once a bin is mature, with a dense springtail population and a stable gradient, mould becomes self-correcting.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just dry the bin out to kill the mould?

You can, and you will lose juveniles to failed moults and adults to stress. A dry bin kills mould and also kills the animals. Fix ventilation and rebuild the wet end instead. Cut misting back over four days rather than stopping dead — abrupt drying cracks the substrate and dumps humidity in one go.

Will the clean-up crew fix mould on its own?

Springtails and isopods process mould if the population is dense and air moves. A thin starter culture cannot outpace a bin sitting at 90 %. The crew partners with ventilation; it does not replace it. Get airflow right first, then let the springtails work the surface.

What substrate if I keep getting mould?

Coco coir, composted leaf litter and a charcoal or gravel drainage layer, 2:1:1 with leaf on top. Skip pure peat — it holds water and compacts into an anaerobic block. The drainage layer is the single biggest improvement most keepers can make, and it costs almost nothing.
