How to Fix High Humidity and Mould in Isopod Cultures

Mould in an isopod tub is almost never a humidity problem. It is an airflow problem, and the fix takes five minutes and a drill.

You open the tub and there it is: a white fur of mould spreading across the leaf litter, a sour smell, and isopods clustered on the walls trying to get away from the substrate. The instinct is to dry everything out. That instinct is wrong, and it usually kills the colony faster than the mould would have. A Porcellio scaber colony wants 70–85 % humidity with air moving across it; seal the air off and the moisture that was keeping them alive becomes the thing drowning them.

Mould is a symptom of still, saturated air. Isopods need high humidity and airflow, and almost every failing culture has one without the other.

Why drying it out backfires

Isopods breathe through pleopods that must stay moist. Drop the humidity sharply and you get a wave of deaths, failed moults, and a colony that never recovers its numbers. The mould goes away; so does everything else.

The correct move is to keep the moisture and move the air.

The tub that went sour in a week

I sealed a Porcellio scaber culture with cling film because the room was dry and I was worried about the substrate drying out. Seven days later the tub smelled like a drain and half the colony was standing on the lid. I had built a swamp with no exit. The isopods were not sick, they were trying to leave.

What I do now is boring and it works. Two vent holes covered with fine mesh, a lid that sits loose rather than clipped, and a moisture gradient I can see: wet end, dry end, leaf litter over both. I test the dry end with the back of my hand instead of a hygrometer — if it is cool and faintly damp, the colony has somewhere to retreat to. When I forget, the smell tells me before the isopods do.

The five-minute fix: ventilation

Most cultures are kept in sealed food containers with a few pinpricks in the lid. That is not ventilation, it is a humidity trap. What works:

  • Two to four vents of 25 mm, on opposing sides — not all in the lid.
  • Fine mesh (0.5 mm or smaller) glued over each, so springtails and hatchlings stay in.
  • One vent low, one high if you can manage it. That creates a convection loop instead of a dead zone.

After drilling, expect the surface to dry within a day. That is the point — the substrate below stays damp, and the isopods move down.

The moisture gradient, not a moisture number

Stop trying to hit one humidity figure. Soak one end of the substrate and leave the other end dry, then let the animals choose. This gives you three things at once: a hydration zone, a dry refuge, and a visible indicator — isopods clustered in the middle mean the gradient is working.

Re-wet the damp end when it starts to feel dry to the touch, roughly every five to seven days. Never flood it. Standing water in the bottom of a tub is how you grow anaerobic bacteria and fungus gnats.

Fix the food, not just the air

Mould needs fuel. The usual culprits:

  • Fresh vegetables left in too long. Remove after 48 hours, without exception.
  • Too much protein. Fish flake and dried shrimp are excellent — in pinches, weekly. A spoonful will mould and then crash the population.
  • Wet leaf litter. Leaves should be damp, not sodden. Bake or freeze collected leaves before use.
  • No springtails. Isopods are grazers, not mould specialists. Springtails are the mould specialists.

If the culture has already crashed

Salvage what you can: move surviving adults to a fresh tub with new substrate and a properly vented lid. Do not reuse the mouldy substrate, and do not try to "rescue" it by drying — the spore load is already high.

Then diagnose before you restock. Nine times out of ten it was ventilation, and the same tub will fail again.

Frequently asked questions

Is surface mould always a problem?

Not always. A little white mycelium on a dead leaf is normal decomposition and the crew will eat it. Mould that spreads across the substrate, smells sour, or makes the isopods climb the walls is a problem.

Do springtails really stop mould?

They suppress it by grazing on hyphae and bacterial films before it becomes visible. They are not a steriliser — they work alongside ventilation, not instead of it.

Should I use a lid at all?

Yes, but a vented one. Fully open tubs dry out in hours and let in fungus gnats, which are far more annoying than the mould you were trying to fix.
