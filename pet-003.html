How to Fix High Humidity and Mould in Isopod Cultures

Mould in an isopod tub is almost never a humidity problem. It is an airflow problem, and the fix takes five minutes and a drill.

A Porcellio scaber culture kept at 70-85 % relative humidity stays in the range where the animals can breathe through their pleopods — the flattened plates under the abdomen that work as lungs and need a wet film to move oxygen. Above 90 %, that same film stops being a lung surface and becomes a growing medium for mold and for the mites that follow it. The band is narrow; the culture lives or dies in the gap between two numbers on a cheap gauge.

Keeper's diary: the tub I nearly lost

Half past ten at night, and I was crouched over a 12-litre tub of P. scaber with a torch. The lid was fogged. I had been misting the whole surface twice a week since October — that was the habit, and it felt generous. When I lifted the lid the smell hit me before the light did: wet cardboard and something sweeter underneath. A patch of white fuzz the size of a ten-pence coin was spreading across the cuttlebone, and the springtails were nowhere on it. I tipped the tub gently; the leaf litter came out in one wet slab. Not crumbs. A slab. The isopods beneath it were alive but slow, and eleven of them were grey and soft-looking, which I now read as the wrong kind of plump. I had been watering an animal that lives under stones in dry leaf drift, and I had been doing it on a schedule instead of a gauge. Cost: one tub of leaf litter, a cuttlebone, and about a month of growth on that generation.

Where the water actually goes

Mold is a symptom, not the disease. The disease is stagnant air over saturated substrate. A P. scaber culture wants a moisture gradient, not a wet box: one end damp enough that a squeezed handful of substrate holds together without dripping, the other end drier than you think is right. Adults run 12-18 mm and cover ground, so they will self-select the end they need — provided both ends exist. Your job is to keep that gradient stable between 70 and 85 % relative humidity, measured at substrate level rather than at the lid, where the reading is always wetter.

  1. Stop misting entirely for ten days. Not a reduction — a stop. Lift the lid daily and fan the tub for thirty seconds.
  2. Measure at substrate level. Put one probe down in the damp end, one in the dry end. If the damp end reads above 85 %, leave the lid ajar overnight.
  3. Pull the wet slab. Remove any leaf litter that clumps rather than separates. Replace with dry, conditioned leaves from the dry end of the gradient.
  4. Vent the lid. A sealed lid over a damp tub is a terrarium, and terrariums grow mold. Two or three 25 mm mesh discs set into the lid give you airflow you can see and control.
  5. Add springtails before you need them. A temperate springtail culture colonises the surface and eats the mold before it fruits. Add them to the dry end so they establish rather than drown.

The decision point is simple: if the tub smells sour and the substrate clumps, you are over-watered and under-vented, and no amount of wiping the mold off the cuttlebone will fix it. If the isopods are clustering on the dry end and the substrate crumbles, you have overcorrected.

Rebuilding after a mold bloom

If the bloom has already taken hold, do not try to save the substrate. Move the animals to a clean tub with fresh, dry-conditioned leaf litter and a fresh cuttlebone for calcium — P. scaber needs it for the exoskeleton and will chew it hard after a molt. Discard the old substrate outdoors rather than composting it in the room. Keep the new tub at the dry end of the range for a week, then reintroduce moisture to one end only. Watch the next molt cycle: soft, grey animals a week after the move mean the dry-out went too far, not that the mold is back.

Mold spores are everywhere; you are managing moisture, not sterility. What I cannot tell you is the exact spore load that tips a healthy culture into a crash — nobody has published that number for this species, and I would be making it up. What I can tell you is that a gradient, a gauge, and open venting have kept every tub I have run since that October out of trouble.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just wipe the mold off and carry on?

You can, and it will come back within a week if the moisture is unchanged. Wiping removes the fruiting body, not the cause. Fix the airflow and the gradient first; the mold then stops returning because the surface dries between mistings. Treat wiping as cosmetic, and treat the gauge reading as the actual repair.

Will springtails outcompete my isopods?

No. Springtails are far smaller, work the surface film, and eat mold and decaying organic matter rather than competing with isopods for leaf litter. In my tubs they coexist without issue. The only risk is adding them to a soaking-wet tub, where they drown — always seed them into the drier end.

How often should I mist a P. scaber culture?

On a gauge, not a calendar. At 70-85 % relative humidity with a proper gradient, many tubs need misting only every one to two weeks, and some need less in a cool room. Check the damp end, mist only that end, and let the dry end stay genuinely dry. A fixed twice-weekly schedule is how my October tub flooded.
